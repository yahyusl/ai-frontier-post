Amazon and Meta are reportedly talking again — Muse may soon be allowed back to shop on Amazon

Two months after Amazon locked Meta’s Muse agent out of its store, the two companies are reportedly negotiating a deal to let it back in — with Amazon’s $68.6 billion advertising business at the center of the talks.

Two months after Amazon shut Meta's Muse agent out of its store, the two companies are talking about letting it back in. Fortune reported on Wednesday that Amazon and Meta have held several rounds of discussions about a deal that would connect Muse directly to Amazon's store — a reversal that would arrive just as the year's biggest shopping weekend gets underway.

Nothing is agreed yet. The talks are fluid, no timetable has emerged, and any deal would need the personal approval of both chief executives, Mark Zuckerberg and Andy Jassy. But the fact that the conversation is happening at all marks a sharp change of course: in September, Amazon treated Muse as an intruder. Now it is negotiating the terms of its return.

What happened in September#

Muse launched in the United States in September as Meta's always-on personal agent — software that can check email, manage a calendar, book a table, and shop on the user's behalf. Within weeks, users who asked it to buy something on Amazon hit a wall: checkout attempts failed, and the site served up an anti-bot warning saying continued access by an unauthorised AI agent violated Amazon's Conditions of Use.

Amazon's complaint had two parts. It said Meta had never asked permission before letting Muse crawl its pages, and it accused Meta of letting the agent pass itself off as an ordinary shopper instead of identifying as a bot. The standoff became the first big test of a question the whole industry is now wrestling with: when an AI agent shops for you, whose rules govern the trip?

The two sticking points: search and ads#

According to Fortune's reporting, the negotiations come down to two things: how search results are presented, and how advertising is handled. Both cut to the heart of Amazon's business.

The advertising question is the bigger one. Amazon's ad business brings in $68.6 billion a year, built on sponsored listings designed to be seen — and clicked — by human shoppers. An agent that finds the product, compares the options, and checks out on its own can walk straight past all of it. Amazon's worry, as people familiar with the talks put it, is that third-party agents weaken its direct relationship with the customer: if Muse becomes the storefront, Amazon risks becoming the warehouse behind it.

There is also a question of trust. If Muse places the wrong order or creates a problem with a purchase, it is Amazon's sellers and support staff who deal with the fallout — one reason Amazon may have wanted to see the agent mature before agreeing to anything.

The tone from Amazon's side has softened noticeably. Earlier this month, Jassy told a Semafor conference that the company had not ruled out letting third-party shopping agents onto its platform, provided they offered a “fair exchange of value.” That was a shift from October, when he argued that few customers were actually buying through outside agents — a claim that reads differently now that Amazon is sitting across the table from Meta.

Why the timing matters#

The calendar is doing some of the talking. Black Friday and Cyber Monday are Amazon's most important shopping days of the year, and a deal — even a narrow one — would put Muse in front of millions of holiday shoppers at exactly the moment they are buying.

Meta, meanwhile, has spent the last two months building the partnerships Amazon has so far refused. This week it announced a deal with Walmart that lets Muse shop the retailer's Black Friday sales and complete transactions in the app. It already connects to Shopify through a catalog API, finishing purchases with Shop Pay. And in the UK, where Muse launched two days ago, it can search Argos, Marks & Spencer, Currys and Sainsbury's, compare prices, and stage items for purchase.

More than 10 million people in the US and Canada had set up Muse as of October 21, according to Zuckerberg. Amazon is the conspicuous hole in that map — the largest store in the country, and the one Muse's users are most likely to ask it to visit.

What to watch#

The first question is whether the two chief executives sign off — and whether anything lands in time to matter for the holiday rush. The second is what the terms look like: if Amazon secures a deal that keeps its advertising visible inside Muse's answers, expect every other major retailer to ask for the same, and every other agent maker to face the same negotiation.

And if the talks fall apart, the blockade becomes the template instead — agents locked out at the door, retailers building their own. Either way, the next few weeks will set the pattern for how AI shopping actually works: whether agents browse the open web as guests, or enter through deals struck store by store.

Frequently asked questions

Why did Amazon block Muse in the first place?

In September 2026, shortly after Muse launched in the US, Amazon cut off the agent’s access to Amazon.com. It said Meta had not sought permission before letting Muse crawl its pages, and accused Meta of letting the agent present itself as an ordinary shopper rather than identifying as an AI bot. Users who tried to check out through Muse got an error instead of a completed purchase.

What would a deal mean for shoppers?

If the talks succeed, Muse would be able to search Amazon, compare products and complete purchases on a user’s behalf — the way it already does with Walmart and Shopify. But no deal has been reached, the talks are fluid, and it is unclear whether or when an agreement will be struck.

Why is advertising the hard part?

Amazon’s ad business brings in $68.6 billion a year from sponsored listings built for human eyes. An agent that picks products and checks out on its own can bypass those listings entirely, and Amazon executives worry that third-party agents could also weaken Amazon’s direct relationship with its customers.
