Thomson Reuters builds its next AI model on both sides of the US–China divide

Thomson Reuters will launch Thomson 1.1 by the end of 2026, built on China's GLM from Z.ai and Google's Gemma — a deliberate mix of open-source models from both sides of the AI race, aimed at cutting costs and keeping control of its AI stack.

Thomson Reuters is about to ship an AI model built from both sides of the world's sharpest technology rivalry. The company told Business Insider it will launch Thomson 1.1 before the end of 2026, running on open-source models from China's Z.ai and Google's Gemma — a deliberate pairing that mixes a Chinese model chosen for its agentic skill with an American one chosen for its size and efficiency.

The move says something about how large companies are starting to buy AI: not as a single vendor relationship, but as a stack assembled from downloadable parts, to cut costs and keep control of their own intellectual property — and a quiet admission that in 2026, no single country's open models cover everything a company needs.

A deliberate split: GLM for agents, Gemma for efficiency#

The pairing is not a compromise — it is the design. Chief technology officer Joel Hron said Thomson Reuters kept Z.ai's GLM because it performs well at using computers, the capability that matters most for AI agents that have to operate software on a user's behalf. Google's Gemma earned its place for the opposite reason: it is small and efficient, cheap to run at scale for the routine work that fills most of a legal and tax practice.

Because both models are open-source, Thomson Reuters can download and host them in-house instead of paying a closed-model vendor per request — cutting costs and keeping the weights, the tuning, and the data under its own roof.

A reversal from Thomson 1.0#

Thomson 1.1 is also a course correction. The previous in-house model, Thomson 1.0, was built on Qwen, the model family from Chinese tech giant Alibaba, and used no American AI technology at all. Now the company says it wants US and Western foundations wherever it can get them. “The US government, and many other governments around the world, prefer to have US and Western sovereign models at the foundation of what we do,” Hron told Business Insider.

That matters because Thomson Reuters' customers include governments, law firms, and tax practices — clients who care, sometimes contractually, about where the model under their tools comes from. An entirely Chinese-built stack is a harder sell to a Western government client than one with a Google foundation in the mix.

The economics: $40 million in, under 5% of tokens#

Building that capacity is not cheap, even when the base models are free to download. Hron said Thomson Reuters has spent about $40 million so far modifying and tuning its own models, covering chips, data, and salaries for AI researchers. And adoption is still early: fewer than 5% of all AI tokens used at the company currently come from its own models, though Hron expects that share to rise as the areas it covers expand.

Thomson 1.1 targets the middle of the work — mid-tier tasks such as tax advice — while the most difficult work still goes to OpenAI and Anthropic models. “There's a lot of work that happens in the middle, and that's where we get real economic benefit,” Hron said. The strategy, in short, is to own the routine and rent the frontier — paying top dollar only where frontier capability actually earns its price.

The awkward part: what the Chinese model brings with it#

Building on a Chinese model carries a cost that has nothing to do with money. Israeli startup Hirundo sent Business Insider an analysis showing that one version of the current Thomson model repeated Chinese government positions: it refused to acknowledge the Tiananmen Square massacre and stated that Taiwan is an inalienable part of China. Chinese AI models can carry censorship features that are difficult to remove, and a model trained under those constraints brings its blind spots with it.

A Thomson Reuters spokesperson said the system handles such questions substantially better when used as intended, with a specific set of instructions, and that the upcoming model further reduces bias. Lead AI scientist Andrew Bean framed the approach as sovereignty in practice: “Thomson is what AI sovereignty actually looks like in practice.” Whether the tuning actually scrubs the inherited positions is the kind of claim that invites independent testing — and the Hirundo finding guarantees someone will do it.

What to watch#

Three things. First, whether Thomson 1.1 ships on schedule by the end of the year, and how fast the share of tokens running on the company's own models climbs from under 5%. Second, whether other legal and data giants copy the mixed-stack playbook — pairing the best open model for each job regardless of which country built it — now that a company of Thomson Reuters' standing has done it in public. And third, whether the bias fixes hold up: a model that answers political questions with a foreign government's talking points is a liability for a news and legal company, and the next independent audit will be the real test.

Frequently asked questions

What is Thomson 1.1?

Thomson 1.1 is Thomson Reuters' second in-house AI model, built on Z.ai's GLM and Google's Gemma. The company told Business Insider it will launch by the end of 2026.

Why is it built on both a Chinese and an American model?

Each model does a different job: GLM was kept because it performs well at using computers, a capability AI agents need, while Gemma was chosen for its small size and efficiency. Both are open-source and can be downloaded and hosted in-house, which Thomson Reuters says cuts AI costs and gives it more control over its intellectual property.

What happened with political bias in the current model?

An analysis by Israeli startup Hirundo found that one version of the current Thomson model repeated Chinese government positions, refusing to acknowledge the Tiananmen Square massacre and stating that Taiwan is an inalienable part of China. A Thomson Reuters spokesperson said the system handles such questions substantially better when used with a specific set of instructions, and that the upcoming model further reduces bias.

Will Thomson 1.1 replace OpenAI and Anthropic at Thomson Reuters?

No. The company's own model is aimed at mid-tier work such as tax advice, while the hardest tasks still go to OpenAI and Anthropic models. Fewer than 5% of the AI tokens the company uses currently come from its own models.
