Governors draw a federalism line on AI: Washington handles the big risks, states keep the rest

The nation’s governors want Washington to handle the biggest risks from artificial intelligence — and the states to keep everything else. The nation’s governors want Washington to handle the biggest risks from artificial intelligence — and they want the states to keep everything else. On Sunday, November 22, the National Governors Association released six bipartisan principles for governing AI, staking out the middle ground in the fight over who writes the rules for AI in America.

The principles come from the association’s bipartisan Governors Council to Secure American AI Dominance, and they are aimed at the incoming Congress, which takes office in January and is expected to write a federal AI framework. The governors’ message is that a national approach is needed — but that it should be built with the states, not over them.

What the governors proposed#

The six principles read like a compromise document, and that is the point. They call for:

  • Advancing innovation and protecting the public: AI policy should strengthen American technological leadership while protecting public safety, civil rights and privacy. The release argues the United States will not win the AI race if the public does not trust that the technology is safe and works for them.
  • Supporting workers: job creation should be a core priority as AI spreads, with programs to support workers whose jobs change and protection from the harms of rapid deployment.
  • Clear and complementary federal and state roles: the federal government should handle national and international risks as AI is developed, while states keep the flexibility to protect people and govern how AI is used once it is deployed.
  • Safeguards matched to credible, evidence-based risks: rules should use clear, technically informed definitions and proportionate measures, reviewed regularly as capabilities change.
  • Consistency without weakening protections: federal and state approaches should cut duplicated and conflicting requirements wherever possible.
  • Competition: rules should not favor large companies or put unnecessary obstacles in front of smaller entrants.

The council is led by Maryland Governor Wes Moore, the association’s chair, and Indiana Governor Mike Braun, its vice chair, alongside North Carolina Governor Josh Stein and Utah Governor Spencer Cox. Moore, a Democrat, and Braun, a Republican, frame the effort as bipartisan by design — a point the release stresses repeatedly.

The preemption question underneath#

The release never uses the word “preemption.” But that is the fight it is really about. In March, the association summarized a White House legislative framework that, in its reading, called on Congress to preempt state AI laws that impose undue burdens on AI development. The governors’ principles answer that with a split: Washington takes the risks that cross borders; the states keep authority over how AI touches the people who live there.

The stakes are concrete. States have already passed their own AI laws — covering children, consumers and workers — and several are stepping up enforcement. Cox’s line in the release was that governors do not have to choose “between innovation and safety,” and that many states have already enacted policies that foster adoption while protecting children and consumers. The subtext: those laws are not going away quietly.

Moore put the same point more bluntly: “We agree that humans must stay in control, and we must protect our people.” Stein added that the governors are calling on the federal government “to act to protect the public, support innovation, and preserve appropriate state authority.”

Why the timing matters#

The governors timed the release for the transition. The principles were discussed in an interview that aired Sunday on Bloomberg This Weekend, and the release is explicitly addressed to the incoming Congress as it prepares a federal framework. The association’s chief executive, Brandon Tatum, said no federal AI policy would work without governors’ input — the states, in his words, are “on the frontlines” of AI deployment, running the schools, hospitals and services where the technology actually lands.

That framing matters because the alternative is already on the table: a single federal rulebook that would override state laws. The governors are offering a third option between that and the current patchwork — federal rules for how models are built, state rules for how they are used.

What to watch#

Three things. First, whether the incoming Congress takes up a federal AI framework in its first months, and whether it draws the federal-state line where the governors drew it. Second, whether any framework preempts state laws — the principles do not say which, if any, state laws should be set aside, and that silence is where the real negotiation will happen. And third, what the states do meanwhile: with enforcement already stepping up, governors do not need to wait for Washington to keep writing rules of their own.

Frequently asked questions

What did the governors actually release?

Six principles for governing AI at the state and federal levels, published November 22, 2026, by the National Governors Association’s bipartisan Governors Council to Secure American AI Dominance. The principles are meant to inform the incoming Congress as it writes a federal AI framework.

Does this settle whether federal law overrides state AI laws?

No. The principles never use the word preemption and do not name any state law that should be set aside. They argue Washington should handle national and international risks while states keep flexibility over how AI is used — a middle position between a single federal rulebook and the current patchwork.

Who wrote the principles?

The Governors Council to Secure American AI Dominance, led by NGA Chair Wes Moore of Maryland, Vice Chair Mike Braun of Indiana, North Carolina Governor Josh Stein and Utah Governor Spencer Cox.
