RUMOR: Alpöge and Buckmaster claim an unforced Navier–Stokes blow-up — paper and Lean code posted, certificate still to come

Hours after a claim that Claude helped prove the Riemann Hypothesis, the same mathematician has posted a second one — aimed at the other great unsolved problem in his sights. On Friday morning, Tristan Buckmaster, a mathematician at New York University, announced that he and Levent Alpöge of Anthropic have a proof of finite-time blow-up for the unforced Navier–Stokes equations, the fluid-dynamics problem at the heart of one of the Clay Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems. A 79-page paper and a Lean 4 formalization are posted online. Nothing has been independently verified — and the repository itself is upfront about what is missing.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 20, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge say they have proved that smooth solutions of the unforced Navier–Stokes equations can blow up in finite time — the negative answer to a $1 million Millennium Prize question. A 79-page paper and Lean code were posted on Friday, November 20. The key certificate the paper relies on has not been released yet, the computation behind it is not in the repository, and no independent expert has verified the claim.
  • Tristan Buckmaster announced the claim on Mastodon on Friday, November 20, 2026, linking a paper PDF for unforced Euler and a GitHub repository for unforced Navier–Stokes.
  • The repository holds the paper Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations by L. Alpöge and T. Buckmaster — 79 pages, dated November 20, 2026 — plus a Lean 4 formalization of about 2,270 files and 600,000 lines.
  • Buckmaster's own words on the announcement: the write-up is “unpolished and heavily LLM-assisted.”
  • The repository's README states the certificate whose hash the paper gives is not included and “will be released,” and that the rigorous computation behind it is not part of the formalization.
  • The claim follows the same pair's September announcement of blow-up for the Euler equations with smooth forcing, and OpenAI's September claim for forced Navier–Stokes — both of which drew criticism that the forcing, not the fluid, did the work.
  • A Reddit thread in r/singularity picked the claim up within an hour of the announcement; no top comment debunks it, and the only pushback is against calling the morning “the singularity.”

What was actually posted#

Buckmaster announced the claim on Mastodon on Friday morning, in a post that linked two things: a PDF for unforced Euler, hosted at NYU's Courant Institute, and a GitHub repository for unforced Navier–Stokes. The repository, created early Friday morning, holds the paper — Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations, by L. Alpöge and T. Buckmaster, 79 pages, dated November 20, 2026 — and a Lean 4 formalization of the argument.

Buckmaster did not dress the announcement up. “After last time, we couldn't risk waiting, so the Navier–Stokes write-up is unpolished and heavily LLM-assisted,” he wrote. “Fortunately, once the numerical profile is in hand, the proof follows a well-trodden computer-assisted path.”

The claim itself is stark. The Navier–Stokes equations describe how fluids move. A “blow-up” means a solution that starts out perfectly smooth develops a singularity in finite time — the velocity gradients run away to infinity, and the equations stop describing any physical fluid. The Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize question asks whether that can happen for the unforced equations, or whether smooth solutions always exist for all time. A verified blow-up would answer it: no, they don't always exist. It would also be worth $1 million — eventually.

Why “unforced” is the word that matters#

This is the second time in two months that these names have been attached to a blow-up claim — and the difference between the two rounds is a single word.

In September, Alpöge and Buckmaster announced finite-time blow-up for the three-dimensional Euler equations with smooth forcing, and OpenAI announced blow-up for the forced Navier–Stokes equations the same week. Both were real mathematical achievements, and both drew the same criticism: the forcing did the work. A paper published in October argued that OpenAI's construction builds the forcing function out of the singular solution itself, so the viscosity term — the very thing the Millennium problem is about — cancels out of the dynamics. The blow-up, on that reading, was an Euler blow-up wearing a Navier–Stokes costume.

Today's claim has no such escape hatch. “Unforced” means no external force is driving the fluid at all: the singularity, if the proof holds, forms out of the fluid's own motion, with viscosity present and unable to stop it. That is the version of the question the Clay Institute actually posed. It is also why the morning's second announcement may matter more than the first: the Riemann Hypothesis is the more famous problem, but the Navier–Stokes claim is the one with no forcing term to argue about.

What is — and is not — in the repository#

The repository is unusually honest about its own gaps, and the gaps are the story.

What is there: the 79-page paper, and a Lean package of roughly 2,270 files and 600,000 lines. The statement files give the definitions and state the theorems; a file called Solution.lean proves them from a library; comparator files check that the statements agree, permitting only the standard axioms. The README notes the package was patched on November 20 and that the patched version was not rebuilt when the repository was assembled — and that a full build “needs a great deal of memory and time.”

What is not there: the certificate. The paper gives a hash for a certificate — the numerical object that is supposed to witness the key computation — and the README says plainly that the certificate is not in the repository and that the paper says it “will be released.” The rigorous computation itself, interval arithmetic at 128 bits of precision, is not part of the formalization and not in the repository either. Nor is the source of the paper.

In other words, the machine-checkable skeleton is posted, but the numerical heart of the proof cannot be checked yet — by anyone. That is a thinner position than this morning's Riemann claim, where the Lean formalization is the whole argument. It is also the reason the claim is labeled a rumor here: the parts that would let an expert verify it are still, by the authors' own account, on their way.

One question the posted material does not answer is which AI model was involved. Nothing in the repository or the announcement names one. Reddit commenters have guessed; a guess is all it is.

What to watch#

Six things decide how big this becomes. First, the certificate: until it is released, the central computation cannot be checked. Second, whether the Lean package builds and the comparators pass — the fast, mechanical filter. Third, whether independent mathematicians confirm that the formal statements actually match the paper's theorems, rather than a subtly different claim. Fourth, whether a human-readable writeup appears: a 79-page paper exists, which is more than the Riemann claim has, but it is, by its author's word, unpolished and heavily LLM-assisted. Fifth, the companion Euler claim, posted as a PDF in the same announcement, which will face the same scrutiny. And sixth, the prize: the Clay Institute's rules require publication in a refereed journal and general acceptance by the mathematics community before the $1 million is awarded. Even if the proof holds up, that is years away. For now, the honest position is the one the repository itself states: the paper is posted, the code is posted, the certificate is not — and the problem is still open.
