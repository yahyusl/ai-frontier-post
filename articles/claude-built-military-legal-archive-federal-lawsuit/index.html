A Claude-built archive of 300,000 military board decisions is now evidence in a federal lawsuit against Trump and the Pentagon

A veterans’ legal nonprofit sued the President and the Pentagon on November 10 after building a searchable archive of 300,000 military board decisions with Claude. The complaint uses the archive to show the Defense Department’s own records site can’t find its own cases.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

On Tuesday, November 10, a small veterans’ legal nonprofit sued the President of the United States and the Pentagon in federal court — and the case is built on an archive that artificial intelligence helped assemble. The Institute for Constitutional Military Law filed suit in Springfield, Illinois, arguing the Defense Department has failed for years to make military review boards’ decisions public and searchable the way Congress requires.

The Institute’s founder says the archive at the heart of the case — a free, searchable collection of more than 300,000 board decisions — was built over 18 months with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant, using its Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 models. The complaint itself does not name Claude, but it leans on the archive heavily: its own search comparisons show the Pentagon’s official site missing cases the archive finds.

What the lawsuit asks for#

The complaint was e-filed at 8:46 a.m. on November 10 in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of Illinois, Springfield Division, as case No. 3:26-cv-03388-CRL-DJQ, assigned to Judge Colleen R. Lawless. The defendants are Donald J. Trump in his official capacity as President, the Department of Defense, the Departments of the Army, the Navy and the Air Force, the Department of Homeland Security, and each department’s secretary.

The Institute asks the court to order the agencies to produce the records it requested under the Freedom of Information Act, to make the boards’ final decisions and current indexes public, and to compel the summaries and subject index Congress required. It also asks the Navy to publish the names and votes of the board members who decide each case, or justify each deletion in writing, and for a declaration that the President has no power to suspend the law — a declaration only, not an order against him. It seeks no damages, only fees and costs, and asks the court to keep the case open with a compliance plan and progress reports until every decision is published with a summary and an index.

The archive that makes the case#

The Institute calls its collection the Reading Room: a free public archive of more than 300,000 decisions the boards have posted, collected, converted to searchable text and indexed by subject. According to the Institute, it was built with custom software, optical character recognition and several artificial intelligence systems, all directed by an attorney. It is live at readingroom.icml.us.

The complaint uses the archive as its own evidence. Searching the Pentagon’s site for the phrase “Presidential Clemency” returned 8 decisions; the Institute’s Reading Room found 31 covering the same posted decisions, including the board’s 2020 decision in the case of the veteran whose clemency petition the complaint discusses. A search for decisions about post-deployment health assessments returned 19 on the official site and 33 in the Reading Room.

The founder’s own numbers, posted with the announcement, are larger still: he says a Pentagon search for post-traumatic stress disorder returns 211 records, while his archive returns 13,085 from the Navy board alone, and at least 44,023 across the Army, Navy and Air Force boards against 11,600 on the official site. Those figures are his, not the court’s, and they have not been independently audited. But the underlying point is narrower than the numbers: the archive holds the boards’ own published decisions, converted and indexed rather than generated — which is why the complaint can cite it as proof that a searchable index is practicable, not as a new source of law.

Why the Pentagon’s own site is the problem#

In December 2024, Congress required that, from October 1, 2026, the information the correction boards publish “shall include a summary of each decision, to be indexed by subject matter.” On October 1 the Defense Department’s site carried no summary of any decision and no subject index, and the complaint says it carries none today.

The site’s problems run deeper than the missed deadline. A Government Accountability Office report from July 2025 found it was missing about 84 percent of the Army correction board’s liberal-consideration decisions closed from January 2018 through March 2024, and about 81 percent of the Air Force board’s. The site has looked the same since at least January 2005: decisions sit in folders named only by branch, board and year, and the Army correction board’s folder for 2010 lists 9,247 files on a single page, with no summary and no index. Searching by docket number sometimes finds the decision, sometimes finds nothing, and sometimes returns another veteran’s decision.

The Institute sent nineteen Freedom of Information Act requests between September 23 and 30, 2026. Not one agency produced a single record until November 5, when the Board for Correction of Naval Records released job descriptions for its senior staff and the slideshows it uses to train board members. The Defense Department agreed in July 2024 to replace the site, could not give an anticipated date in April 2025, and told the accountability office in July 2026 that a new site with metadata and case summaries was planned for October 2026. None had launched by October 1.

What Reddit made of it#

The story surfaced on r/ClaudeAI, where the founder — a Marine Iraq veteran, now a lawyer and the Institute’s founder — posted it on Monday evening. The thread drew more than 1,100 upvotes and 57 comments. One commenter supplied a CourtListener docket link corroborating that the case exists; another asked how much of the archive’s output the models might have hallucinated, a question the founder did not answer in the thread. Asked who paid for the work, he replied that he had no legal department or budget, and that volunteers and a data analyst helped.

What to watch#

First, whether the court moves quickly. The Institute wants the case kept open until every decision is published with a summary and an index — a compliance remedy that would take the Defense Department well past the deadline Congress already set.

Second, whether the Defense Department’s replacement website finally appears, and whether it meets the standard the Institute’s archive already sets. The complaint’s argument is, in effect, that a nonprofit with volunteers and AI tools did in 18 months what the department has not done in twenty years.

And third, whether this becomes a pattern. AI-built public-interest archives — collections that make government records searchable rather than generating new content — are a different use of the technology from chatbots and copilots, and they invite a different kind of scrutiny: not whether the model is eloquent, but whether its index is complete and its text faithful. The complaint’s search comparisons are a start on that audit, not the end of it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Institute for Constitutional Military Law?

A nonprofit founded by attorney Tyson Manker that represents servicemembers and veterans before the military review boards free of charge. Its members in the lawsuit include veterans and families in Michigan, North Carolina, California, Florida, Minnesota and Connecticut, and the National Veterans Council for Legal Redress.

Did Claude write the lawsuit?

No. The AI systems built the archive — collecting, converting and indexing the boards’ published decisions under an attorney’s direction. The complaint is a legal filing by the Institute’s counsel, and it does not mention Claude; the Claude detail comes from the founder’s Reddit post.

What happens next in the case?

The case is open in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of Illinois, Springfield Division (No. 3:26-cv-03388-CRL-DJQ, Judge Colleen R. Lawless). The Institute asks the court to keep it open with a compliance plan and progress reports until every board decision is published with a summary and a subject index.
