Lean and Mathlib certify Alpöge's Riemann proof: ‘We have full confidence’

The bodies that maintain Lean and its mathematics library have formally certified Levent Alpöge's Riemann Hypothesis proof as correct — closing the verification question that hung over the November 20 claim, while the human-readable proof remains unwritten.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 21, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: The Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative — the organizations behind the proof-checking software and the mathematics library Alpöge's proof is written in — published a joint statement on November 21 certifying that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct. All four items on the community's verification checklist are satisfied. What the certification does not do: explain the proof, which no human has yet read, or settle the Clay Institute's prize process.
  • Who certified it: the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, in a joint statement on the Lean community blog, November 21.
  • The verdict: full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct; both the statement and the proof are certified.
  • The four checks: correct statement (Mathlib's own definition), clean compilation (dozens of independent reproductions), right target (two independent comparison tools), valid proof (Lean kernel, con-leche, con-ron, plus independent checkers).
  • What is still open: the human-readable proof (250,000+ pages if written out), the generalized hypothesis as a separate certification, and the Clay prize process.
  • The context: the claim landed November 20 as a four-page note and a 17-million-line Lean library; this statement closes the verification question our earlier coverage left open.

One day after Levent Alpöge said he had proved the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude, the two organizations that maintain the machinery the proof is built on have answered the question everyone was asking: is it correct? Their answer, published Saturday morning as a joint statement, is yes — with full confidence, and in writing.

The statement comes from the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, the bodies behind Lean, the proof-checking software, and Mathlib, the mathematics library the proof is written in. It is the closest thing this story has to an official verdict, and it closes the verification question that hung over Friday's claim: the proof compiles, it proves the right statement, and independent checkers agree it is valid.

What the two bodies actually said#

Alpöge, a mathematician on Anthropic's technical staff, released the proof on November 20 as a Lean library of 17 million lines of code, alongside a four-page note on his personal website. Over the following day, members of the Lean and Mathlib communities set about checking it. The joint statement says all four items on the community's verification checklist — drawn up days earlier by the Xena Project — are satisfied, and certifies both the statement and the proof as correct.

The wording is unambiguous, and that matters: the first coverage of the claim, including ours, had to hedge every sentence with “claimed” and “pending verification.” The hedge can now come off the verification question, even as it stays firmly on the question of understanding.

The four checks, one by one#

1. The statement is the right one. The Riemann Hypothesis as formalized lives in Mathlib itself, under the name RiemannHypothesis, and has been vetted by experts in Lean and number theory. That detail carries weight: the people who wrote the proof did not get to write the definition it is judged against.

2. The library compiles. By now, dozens of people have independently compiled the codebase and reproduced the same raw proof export, with no errors or build failures. A proof that only compiles on its author's machine would have been a red flag; this one compiles everywhere it has been tried.

3. The code proves the correct statement. The community added a file that isolates the statement of the hypothesis and imports only Mathlib, then compared that statement against the theorem Alpöge's library actually proves using two independent tools, comparator and con-leche –compare. Both confirmed the theorem proved is exactly Mathlib's statement — ruling out the classic failure mode where a formal proof quietly proves something weaker than advertised.

4. The proof itself is valid. The exported raw proof was checked by Lean's official kernel, by the con-leche and con-ron checkers, and by several independent checkers contributed by third parties and written in different programming languages. The con-leche and con-ron checkers come with a formal proof, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof — which answers the worry that a flaw in Lean's own logic could have been exploited. And the checks ran on an inert export of the proof, out of reach of any hypothetical malicious code in the proof sources. Andrew Sutherland of MIT, who ran some of the first independent checks, maintains an extensive overview of the verification effort.

What certification does not settle#

A machine-checked proof is not a human-understood one, and the statement is careful not to pretend otherwise. Written out in ordinary mathematical prose, Alpöge's argument would run to more than 250,000 pages. Nobody has read it — including Alpöge himself, who has said he intends to simplify the argument and explain it properly. Terence Tao, in his first public verdict, warned that digesting it will be slow. The statement itself closes by looking forward to the mathematical activity that will explain the new ideas in the proof.

There are two further limits worth stating plainly. First, the certification is framed around the Riemann Hypothesis itself. Alpöge's note also claims the generalized hypothesis, which covers Dirichlet L-functions and is the stronger statement; the joint statement does not separately certify every generalized version. Second, the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved for its $1 million prize process, which requires publication in a refereed journal and two years of acceptance — a 17-million-line Lean library is a long way from that.

One footnote on completeness: a few of the slowest independent replays were still running at the last public refresh of Sutherland's verification page around midday UTC — the lake comparator's end-to-end run among them. The certification rests on the checks that have finished, including the kernel and the formally verified checkers, not on those stragglers.

What to watch#

First, the human-readable write-up. Alpöge has said he will work on understanding and explaining the argument, and expects it to shrink considerably. Until that exists, the proof is a verified black box: correct, but not yet mathematics anyone can learn from.

Second, the two sibling claims from the same circle. A Stanford postdoc's all-human proof, rushed out hours after the Claude claim, and a Navier–Stokes blow-up claim from Alpöge and a collaborator are both still unverified — and neither is covered by this certification.

Third, the Clay Institute. Its prize rules were written for papers, not Lean libraries, and the gap between “certified correct by the Lean community” and “accepted by the mathematical community” is the story of the next months. The verification question is closed. The understanding question is just opening.

Frequently asked questions

Does this mean the Riemann Hypothesis is proved?

As a formal statement checked by machine, yes, according to the two bodies that maintain the proof-checking software and library it was written in: the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative certify that both the statement and the proof are correct. As a piece of mathematics humans understand, not yet — no one has read the 17-million-line argument, and the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved for its prize process.

What did the verification actually check?

Four things: that the statement of the hypothesis in Mathlib is the right one; that Alpöge’s library compiles cleanly (dozens of people reproduced the same proof export); that the code proves exactly Mathlib’s statement (confirmed by two independent comparison tools); and that the proof itself is valid (checked by Lean’s official kernel, the con-leche and con-ron checkers, and several independent checkers written in different languages).

What about the generalized Riemann hypothesis?

Alpöge’s original note claims the generalized hypothesis too, which covers Dirichlet L-functions and is a stronger statement. The joint statement’s certification is framed around the Riemann Hypothesis itself; it does not separately certify every generalized version.
