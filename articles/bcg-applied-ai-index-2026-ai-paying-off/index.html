BCG's Applied AI Index 2026: nearly half of companies now generate real value from AI

BCG's survey of 1,330 CxOs finds nearly half of companies now generate real value from AI — up from just 5% last year. The new bottleneck isn't capability, it's governance.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · September 30, 2026 · 6 min read

For most of this year, the enterprise AI story was a shrug: spending climbed, pilots multiplied, and returns stayed stubbornly thin — last year BCG itself found just 5% of companies capturing substantial AI value. Its new Applied AI Index 2026, released today, reads like a rebuttal: nearly half of all companies now generate real, measurable value from AI, and the winners are no longer just the elite few.

The study surveys 1,330 chief executives and senior leaders across more than 20 sectors. Its argument: the binding constraint has moved. The question is no longer whether AI delivers — it's whether companies can govern what they're deploying. Value numbers jumped, spending doubled in under a year, agents are about to get real authority, and the controls to manage them are nowhere close.

The middle moved up#

In 2025, BCG found only 5% of companies capturing substantial AI value while everyone else got minimal returns. The 2026 edition splits the world into four tiers: 7.5% "future-built" — AI woven through strategy, operations, and the P&L — and 41% "scaling," generating real value across multiple functions. Together that's roughly half the corporate world, past the "AI doesn't pay" debate. The rest: 47% "emerging," 4.5% "stagnating."

The consequential finding is who benefits: future-built companies report 2.3 times the shareholder return of laggards, 2.4 times the revenue growth, and 2.8 times the EBITDA growth — and the scaling cohort already delivers 1.8 times the shareholder return. Value is no longer confined to a handful of AI-native giants, nor to any one industry: every one of the 20-plus sectors in the study contains at least one future-built or scaling company.

AI is no longer an IT line item#

AI spending has roughly doubled in under a year to about 3.3% of revenue — up from roughly 1.7% in late 2025 — and more than 80% of it now sits outside the enterprise IT budget. AI has stopped being a technology line item; it's a business operating expense. As BCG's Michael Grebe warns, companies that still treat AI purely as an IT cost are underestimating both what they're spending and what it could return.

What separates the leaders looks like management, not technology. Future-built companies are 3.5 times more likely to fund one multiyear AI program (61%, versus 17% of laggards) than to scatter pilots. And 95% measure AI against P&L metrics or close proxies — firms that track value through the P&L realize three times as much of it (3.6% versus 1.2%). The winners redesign workflows end to end instead of bolting chatbots onto old processes.

The real gap is control, not capability#

Here's the number that should keep boards awake: 42% of companies expect AI agents with genuine decision-making authority operating inside their firms by 2030 — and only 5% say they have full controls for that world today. Agentic AI is expected to account for roughly 40% of all AI value by 2030 — a huge prize flowing through systems almost nobody is ready to supervise. The required controls aren't exotic — oversight, rollback gates, security, audit trails, cost guardrails — but building them is, per BCG's Jeff Walters, the defining challenge of the next two years. "The responsibility sits with the companies deploying these systems."

Independent research backs the warning: Parker & Lawrence Research, published the same day, found 72% of financial institutions now use high-autonomy AI in at least one risk or compliance domain — and 81% of those have no AI incident-management procedures at all. The autonomy is arriving before the oversight.

The regulatory clock is ticking too: Italy's AI safety law enters into force today, and the White House's self-policing accord was signed Monday. Governance is moving from best practice to law.

Work reshaped, not deleted#

On jobs, the study is blunt but not apocalyptic: workforces are expected to shrink by roughly 10 to 15% by 2030, with pressure landing hardest on middle management, not senior experts. Seven in ten leading firms are already retraining staff, and the share of the workforce in dedicated AI roles is expected to roughly triple — from 7% to 22% — by 2030. Reskilling matters more than headcount cuts.

What to watch#

Three caveats belong on the record. First, this is a self-reported survey of executives — the people with the strongest incentive to say their programs are working. Second, BCG sells AI transformation consulting, so the index sits adjacent to its business; the methodology (1,330 senior leaders) is substantial, but it's worth knowing the messenger. Third, "nearly half" blends the transformed elite with the merely improving; there's a big difference between future-built and still-scaling.

Watch the control gap, because that's where this story goes next. If two in five companies hand agents real authority by 2030 with one in twenty having full controls today, the incident curve — and the litigation around it — will tell the tale. Watch earnings calls for P&L-level AI disclosures; the leaders already talk that way. And watch the scaling cohort: either it climbs into the future-built tier, or it stalls.
