Trump administration weighs forcing US firms to disclose their Chinese AI use

The Treasury Department and the White House science office have discussed requiring companies like Airbnb to post their use of Chinese AI models on their websites — a ‘name and shame’ push that stops short of a ban.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

The Treasury Department and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy have discussed the idea, which would force companies such as Airbnb — which uses Alibaba’s Qwen model in its customer-service agent — to post the information on their websites. The Trump administration is considering requiring American companies to say publicly when they use Chinese AI models, Reuters reported on Wednesday — a disclosure rule meant to push businesses away from Chinese technology without actually banning it.

The plan has been discussed by the Treasury Department and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, according to three people familiar with the matter. Under it, companies would have to post the information on their websites. Airbnb is the example Reuters names: the company uses Qwen, a model built by China's Alibaba, to run its AI customer-service agent. It is a proposal under discussion, not a decision — Reuters reports it is not clear whether the idea is gaining traction inside the administration, or whether it would ever be written into a formal rule.

What the plan would require#

The mechanism is simple: transparency, enforced by publicity. A company using a Chinese model would have to say so on its own website, where customers, investors and rivals could all see it. Nothing in the proposal as reported would stop a company from using the models — the pressure is reputational, not legal. Reuters describes the approach as a ''name and shame'' campaign.

Airbnb, the company named in the report, pushed back on the framing. It told Reuters its AI activity ''runs overwhelmingly on U.S.-origin models'' and that it uses ''only a limited number of China-origin models, all of which are open-source and run only through approved U.S.-based service providers, keeping data and operations separate and protected.'' The White House, the Treasury Department and Alibaba did not respond to Reuters' requests for comment, and neither did the Chinese Embassy in Washington.

Why disclosure instead of a ban#

The choice of disclosure over restriction is deliberate. A ban on Chinese models would anger American industry, which has embraced them, and Beijing, which would treat it as an escalation. Disclosure lets the administration act against Chinese AI without writing a rule that forbids the models.

The reason squeezing is on the agenda at all is that the models are winning on merit as much as on price. Chinese open-weight models — systems whose core components are publicly available for anyone to download and adapt — are cheaper than proprietary tools from OpenAI and Anthropic and perform well enough for many jobs. In October, Chinese AI models were the three most popular by token usage on OpenRouter, the marketplace that tracks model usage.

China hawks inside and around the administration fear the adoption is a trap: that Beijing is using models like Moonshot AI's Kimi, DeepSeek and Qwen to hook American companies so they become dependent on Chinese AI. The irony, which Reuters notes, is that the US government is itself a user — a government website deployed an Alibaba model in September to let users search proposed federal regulations, even though the FBI had accused the model's developer of copying Anthropic's technology.

The pressure behind it#

The disclosure idea is landing in a political climate that has turned sharply against Chinese technology in a matter of days. Revelations about Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang's affair with an alleged Chinese spy have fuelled renewed calls in Washington for a tougher stance on China, and put a spotlight on cases of Nvidia AI chips being smuggled to China that went unpunished.

The administration's rhetoric has been escalating for months. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in July that the US could sanction Chinese AI companies if they were found guilty of stealing American models' intellectual property through distillation — training smaller models on the output of larger ones. In April, Michael Kratsios, the director of the White House science office, accused China of stealing US AI labs' intellectual property on an ''industrial scale.'' But officials have so far stopped short of restricting or punishing Chinese AI companies. Disclosure would be the first concrete step in that direction that does not involve a ban or a sanction.

Congress is already moving on the same track. Republican Representatives Andrew Garbarino of New York, who chairs the House Homeland Security Committee, and John Moolenaar of Michigan, who chairs the House select committee on China, launched an investigation this year into American companies' use of Chinese AI. They sent letters to Airbnb as well as Anysphere, the maker of the AI coding tool Cursor, whose Composer 2 model was built on a version of Kimi, and to DoorDash, whose co-founder said the company routes some tasks in an internal system through Kimi. Moolenaar welcomed news of the administration's deliberations, telling Reuters that ''disclosure is an important step in managing the potential risks posed by the diffusion of Chinese open-weight models in the United States.''

What to watch#

Three things to watch. First, whether the idea gains traction inside the administration — and whether it ever reaches a formal rulemaking process, which is where a disclosure discussion becomes a disclosure requirement. Second, whether disclosure actually changes behaviour: open-weight models can be downloaded and run anywhere, so a notice on a company website may not move a business that picked Qwen or Kimi on price and performance. And third, whether American open-weight alternatives close the gap — Meta's Muse Glimmer, Nvidia's Nemotron and Reflection AI's Beam are the domestic options Reuters names, and their adoption is the other half of any strategy to wean US companies off Chinese models.

If the rule does come, expect a fight over scope: which companies it covers, which models count as Chinese, and what counts as ''use'' when a model is downloaded, fine-tuned and run on American servers.

Frequently asked questions

Would the plan ban Chinese AI models?

No. As reported by Reuters, the idea is disclosure, not prohibition. Companies would have to say publicly that they use Chinese models, but nothing in the proposal would stop them from using them.

Which companies would have to disclose?

Any US company using Chinese AI models. Reuters names Airbnb, which uses Alibaba’s Qwen model in its customer-service agent. Congress has separately asked Airbnb, Anysphere and DoorDash about their use of Chinese models.

Is the plan a done deal?

No. Reuters reports it is not clear whether the idea is gaining traction within the administration or would be enshrined in a formal rulemaking process. The White House, the Treasury Department and Alibaba did not respond to requests for comment.
