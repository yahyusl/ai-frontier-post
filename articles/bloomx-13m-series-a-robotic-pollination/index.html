BloomX raises $13M: the AI that tells farmers exactly when to pollinate

Israeli agtech startup BloomX has closed a $13 million Series A to scale its AI-timed robotic pollination — machines that buzz, shake and electrostatically gather pollen for the crops honeybees won't touch, with the timing picked by environmental AI models.

MD Marcus Doyle Senior Technology Writer · October 9, 2026 · 4 min read

GMO Internet Group and Google Cloud today launched a joint "AI Promotion Project" that puts Gemini Enterprise and Google Workspace in front of about 6,000 of the Japanese conglomerate's employees — with instructions to stop treating AI as a chat tool and start rebuilding business processes around AI agents. The announcement, dated October 9 on GMO's own news feed, is the clearest signal yet that Japan's internet giants are competing not on model quality but on who can rewire their entire workforce around agents first.

The project has four pillars. First, an "Agentic Workflow" migration: roughly 6,000 GMO partners (the company's term for employees) get Gemini Enterprise, and the group will redesign workflows on the assumption that AI agents carry the work — backed by a group-wide hackathon meant to grow in-house AI talent. Second, a "Data Agent" common foundation built on BigQuery and Looker that unifies the group's data so agents make decisions on correct numbers rather than siloed ones. Third, "Agentic Commerce": new EC and payments services in which agents infer a user's intent and proactively propose and execute services. Fourth, "Agentic Defence", which uses Google Cloud security technology to sharpen detection and response for AI-era threats.

The four agentic pillars

The framing matters. GMO didn't announce a pilot or an internal chatbot. Each pillar is named for what the agent does — workflow, data, commerce, defence — and the company explicitly positions the project as moving past "chat with AI" into process redesign. Data comes before flash: unifying group data on BigQuery and Looker is the second pillar precisely because agent decisions are only as good as the data behind them, a lesson most enterprises are learning the hard way.

The commercial angle is the most ambitious. GMO's core businesses are internet infrastructure, security, payments and online shopping — the EC/payments side is where "Agentic Commerce" gets built. Agents that understand user intent and continuously propose and execute services would put GMO in direct competition with the agent-driven shopping push from Google itself, which has been weaving its own agents into commerce flows.

Why GMO is the one to watch

Japan's big internet players are turning agent adoption into a competitive sport. SoftBank has reportedly built more than 2.5 million AI agents across all its employees, and Rakuten Group has rolled out its own "Rakuten AI 3.0" platform. GMO's own receipts are unusually concrete: the group says generative AI saved it an estimated 1.5 million work hours in 2024, and it has publicly committed to becoming "Japan's most hyper-automated company group" by November 30, 2027 — using AI agents across the board.

The physical half of the story is already under way. On October 6, GMO opened the "GMO Humanoid Lab Shibuya Showroom", described as one of Japan's largest R&D facilities dedicated to humanoid robots, run through its GMO AI & Robotics trading arm. The project announcement points to a next step: fusing Google's Gemini Robotics with the group's robotics know-how to carry the screen-based agent economy into the physical world. GMO calls that the "second half of the internet revolution".

The risk is the same one every company faces: agents that touch real money and real systems need the data foundation and governance in place first, not after. GMO is at least building it in that order — shared data definitions on BigQuery and Looker, security capabilities from Google Cloud's Wiz, CodeMender and Mandiant tooling. Whether 6,000 employees can turn a Workspace mandate into redesigned processes, rather than 6,000 new chatbots, is the experiment worth watching through the November 2027 deadline.

Israeli agtech startup BloomX has closed a $13 million Series A to scale its robotic pollination systems, led by Elah Fund with Rothschild, OurCrowd, Sadot — the agtech fund of Israel's Granot growers' organization — plus existing shareholders and new strategic investors from agriculture. Dealroom and today's funding roundup put it among the day's five qualifying announcements. Unusually for a Series A, some of the money comes from the customers themselves: growers tied to Granot kibbutzim including Magal, Horashim and Eyal invested separately and plan to be among the first to deploy BloomX's flagship YAHAV2400 orchard robot in their fields.

The problem BloomX attacks is timing — the most unforgiving variable in farming. Avocados and blueberries, its two core crops, are poorly served by honeybees, which dislike avocado nectar and can't buzz-pollinate blueberry blossoms the way bumblebees do. A grower can do everything right all season and still lose the harvest to a pollination window nobody controlled. BloomX's answer splits into two pillars: AI models that ingest environmental conditions to pinpoint the exact pollination window for each day of the flowering season, and crop-specific machines that do the physical work at that moment.

The machines that mimic bees

The hardware is unabashedly bio-mimetic. For blueberries, BloomX mounts pre-calibrated mechanical arms on electric vehicles that vibrate plant stems — reproducing the "buzz pollination" bumblebees perform with their wing muscles, shaking pollen loose onto the flower's stigma. For avocados, a "collector device" carries an electrostatic charge that strips sticky pollen from one variety's flowers and deposits it on another, forcing the cross-pollination honeybees rarely manage. The company has also widened its blueberry partnership with Robee over the past year and introduced YAHAV2400, its orchard pollination robot, in several territories.

The $13 million will go toward deepening work with existing customers, accelerating YAHAV2400 adoption, moving into new crops and geographies, and — the interesting line in the announcement — turning pollination itself into a managed service that scales across commercial farming. Dealroom ranks the round around the 77th percentile of comparable Series A deals by size; BloomX disclosed no valuation or individual commitments.

Why the growers are co-investing

Founder and CEO Thai Sade framed the raise as validation from the people who would know: "The people who understand the realities and challenges of commercial agriculture firsthand are choosing not only to invest in BloomX, but also to bring our technology into the field." It is a clever structure — investor-customers de-risk the go-to-market because they are the market. Granot is one of Israel's largest agricultural organizations, so a cluster of its kibbutzim deploying the robots doubles as a live reference deployment for the rest of the world's avocado and blueberry regions.

The longer bet is that pollination is just the first timing-critical operation AI will schedule. Everything BloomX built — environmental sensing, per-day operational windows, machines that execute at exactly the right hour — generalizes to spraying, thinning and harvesting, where being a day early or late is the difference between a crop and compost. Whether "pollination as a managed service" scales beyond Granot's orchards is the number to watch.
