Would a design team actually ship it? AtelierBench launches to grade AI against real client briefs

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 20, 2026 · 5 min read
TL;DR: SILVERSIDE and Hyde launched AtelierBench, a benchmark that grades AI models the way a design studio would: real client briefs, expert judging, and the multi-step workflows designers actually use. Its launch finding: today’s models still fall short of professional design standards.
  • Who: SILVERSIDE, an AI innovation company, and Hyde, an AI startup focused on model sovereignty for enterprises.
  • What: AtelierBench, described as the first benchmark that evaluates AI against the real-world judgment and workflows of professional creatives.
  • When: Announced October 20, 2026, via a press release issued from San Francisco.
  • Method: Real client briefs, expert judgment-based scoring criteria, and the multi-step workflows designers actually use — not single images scored against short prompts.
  • Launch finding: AI models still fall short of professional design standards.
  • The three-question test: Can a model deliver an output, would a real design team actually ship it, and what did it cost to get there?

AI can generate an image in seconds. But could it do a professional designer’s actual job — take a client brief, make the hundreds of judgment calls in between, and deliver work a studio would ship? SILVERSIDE and Hyde announced AtelierBench on October 20, a benchmark built to answer exactly that, and their launch finding is blunt: current models don’t clear the professional bar.

What AtelierBench actually tests

AtelierBench is described as the first benchmark designed to evaluate AI against the real-world judgment and workflows of professional creatives, built jointly by SILVERSIDE — an AI innovation company — and Hyde, an AI startup the release describes as bringing model sovereignty to enterprises.

The method is the differentiator. Instead of scoring a single image against a short prompt or crowd taste — how most AI benchmarks work today — AtelierBench tests models against real client briefs, expert judgment-based scoring criteria, and the multi-step workflows designers actually use. The framing question behind it: can AI meet the professional bar, rather than simply generate creative output?

Why existing benchmarks miss the mark

The founders are explicit about what’s broken in current evaluation. “Most AI evals are built around problems with a right answer,” said Anirudh Sanga, co-founder and CEO of Hyde. “Creative work doesn’t have one, and that’s what makes it interesting.”

His pitch for AtelierBench’s honesty: it “captures the judgment that separates good design work from great: can a model deliver an output, would a real design team actually ship it, and what did it cost to get there? That’s a much more honest definition of ‘great’ at creative work, and once you can measure it, you can build models that are great at it.”

The launch verdict: not pro-grade yet

The headline finding from the launch is that AI models still fall short of professional design standards — despite the flood of impressive single-shot generations that dominate demos and social feeds.

“AI has gotten incredibly good at making things, but making something and knowing whether it’s good are two very different skills,” said PJ Pereira, founder of SILVERSIDE. “Creative people make hundreds of decisions between receiving a brief and delivering the final work. We want to understand whether AI can learn to navigate those decisions too.”

Why this matters

As AI takes a larger role in creative production, the question stops being whether a model can produce something striking and becomes whether it can be trusted with the job. A benchmark that grades the whole workflow — brief to shippable — is the first attempt to measure the part of creative work that clients actually pay for: judgment.

Whether AtelierBench becomes the standard yardstick will depend on adoption — the release doesn’t detail which models have been tested or publish a leaderboard. But the direction of travel is clear: the next wave of AI evaluation won’t be about right answers. It’ll be about taste.
