Nominal raises $240M at a $3B valuation to bring AI to hardware engineering

Nominal, the startup selling AI to the engineers who test aircraft, nuclear reactors, and robots, has raised $240 million at a $3 billion valuation — tripling its private-market value in eight months.

What we checked: Nominal's Series C announcement of November 9, TechCrunch's report the same day, and Unite.AI's coverage. The round size, valuation, investors, customer counts, and contract figures come from the announcement and TechCrunch. Nominal has not disclosed revenue, so no valuation multiple can be checked against the $3 billion figure.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 11, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Nominal announced a $240 million Series C at a $3 billion valuation on November 9, led by Lightspeed Venture Partners with Insight Partners co-leading. The valuation triples the $1 billion attached to its March Series B extension. The money funds its 'hardware intelligence' platform — software that ties test data to engineering context, plus its Nominal Analyst AI agent — and expansion into new industries and regions, including through acquisitions.
  • The round: $240 million Series C at a $3 billion valuation; Lightspeed led, Insight Partners co-led, with around 20 investors participating, including Sequoia, General Catalyst, Founders Fund, Lockheed Martin Ventures, and Salesforce Ventures.
  • The re-rating: a $155 million Series B extension at a $1 billion valuation in March 2026, led by Founders Fund, to $3 billion in November — a threefold jump in about eight months.
  • The customers: more than 80 commercial and government organizations, including five of the seven largest defense contractors. Anduril has more than 300 engineers on the platform.
  • The defense anchor: the US Air Force selected Nominal in October for a $71.2 million Strategic Funding Increase award supporting Collaborative Combat Aircraft capabilities.
  • The product: Nominal Analyst, an AI agent that investigates engineering data and returns analyses traceable to the underlying evidence; the platform runs in cloud, on-premises, air-gapped, and edge environments.

The check is large; the re-rating is the story. Nominal was worth $1 billion in March, when Founders Fund led a $155 million Series B extension. Eight months later, investors have put a $3 billion price on the same company — a bet that AI's next big market is not another chatbot, but the engineering teams building the physical machines AI will eventually run on.

Nominal was founded in 2022 and is headquartered in Austin, Texas. Its pitch is aimed at a corner of the economy that software has mostly skipped: the testing and validation of complex hardware, where a single aircraft program can generate years of telemetry, logs, video, and simulation data spread across legacy systems — and where the knowledge of what any of it means often lives in individual engineers' heads.

The March round was, by TechCrunch's account, a preemptive deal — an offer initiated by the investor rather than the startup. The November round is three times the size of that March financing and carries a valuation three times as high. What neither announcement discloses is revenue, so the $3 billion cannot be translated into any multiple; the market is pricing growth and positioning, not a published run rate.

What Nominal actually sells#

Nominal calls its product a hardware intelligence platform, and the framing matters because the company is selling context, not just models. The platform pulls together the data a hardware program produces — telemetry, logs, video, simulation — and ties it to the conditions, decisions, and requirements that give that data meaning, so engineers can build on previous work instead of re-running the same investigations.

That record is also what its AI is built on. Nominal Analyst, the company's first purpose-built agent, investigates engineering data and produces analyses whose sources and calculations stay available for review — a design choice aimed squarely at engineers who will not trust an answer they cannot audit. Engineers keep control of the decisions, reviews, and approvals that matter, and the platform is built to run wherever hardware is developed and validated: cloud deployments, on-premises infrastructure, air-gapped environments, and edge devices. Developers can also reach it through SQL, APIs, and MCP to connect existing tools and build their own workflows and agents on top.

Co-founder and CEO Cameron McCord, a former US Navy submarine officer and Anduril alum, put it this way in the announcement: “Hardware engineering is getting its AI moment, with real operational value that goes far beyond making existing workflows faster.”

The defense connection#

The fastest way to understand the valuation is to look at who already pays. More than 80 commercial and government organizations use Nominal, including five of the seven largest defense contractors — and the company's showcase numbers all come from that world.

At Anduril, more than 300 engineers rely on Nominal across programs, and analysis that once took five to six hours now happens in near real time, according to the company. A pilot at shipbuilder HII reduced certain analysis tasks from hours to minutes and cut some production test steps by roughly half. At Hermeus, the time between uploading data and reviewing it fell 80% while the company designed and built its Quarterhorse Mk 1 aircraft in seven months.

The defense work also brings government money. In October, the Air Force selected Nominal for a $71.2 million Strategic Funding Increase award to support Collaborative Combat Aircraft capabilities — a program that requires matching funds from outside the Air Force. Nominal's account of the problem is blunt: a modern aircraft program can involve primes, suppliers, and government test teams, yet critical test evidence still moves between them through zip files and email threads. Its pitch is a shared workspace where that evidence is exchanged against requirements while each organization keeps control of its intellectual property.

Where the money goes#

Nominal says the funding will support deeper products for engineers and engineering enterprises, agents and applications covering more of a machine's lifecycle — from requirements verification and planning to scheduling and fleet health — and expansion into new industries and regions, including through strategic acquisitions.

It has already started buying. This year Nominal acquired Fid Labs, whose team is developing its agents, and Pull Systems, extending the platform into automotive. The pattern is familiar from other vertical-AI winners: buy the adjacent capability, fold it into the platform, and sell the combined record back to the same customers.

What to watch#

Five things to track from here:

  • Whether the $3 billion holds up. With no revenue disclosed, the valuation is a bet on trajectory. The next financing — or any public filing — will be the first reality check.
  • Defense-to-industrial conversion. Nominal earned its credibility with primes and the Air Force. The question is whether that converts into automotive, energy, robotics, and advanced manufacturing, where the sales cycles and security requirements differ.
  • Analyst in the wild. The traceable-evidence claim is the product's differentiator. Watch whether customers beyond the showcase names adopt the agent for consequential analysis, or keep it on low-stakes work.
  • Acquisition integration. Fid Labs and Pull Systems were both bought this year; how quickly their teams and products fold into the platform will show whether the M&A is strategy or shopping.
  • Competition from the general-purpose side. Frontier models keep getting better at data analysis. Nominal's defense is that generic models do not carry an industry's proprietary data, workflows, and institutional knowledge — a moat that only holds if the platform keeps capturing it.
