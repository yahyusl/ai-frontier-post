Italy fines Meta €805 million for reading Android photo galleries without consent

Italy’s privacy regulator fined Meta €805 million after finding Facebook’s Android app read users’ photo galleries — photos, videos and metadata — for a suggestions feature without specific consent. The decision also sets a precedent other EU regulators can copy.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 16, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: Italy’s Garante fined Meta Platforms Ireland €805 million on November 14, 2026, finding Facebook’s Android app read photos, videos and metadata from about 20 million Italian users’ phones for a gallery-suggestions feature without specific consent. Meta has 60 days to ask for separate consent and switch the feature off by default — and the ePrivacy route Italy used lets other EU regulators bring similar cases without going through Ireland.
  • The fine: €805 million (about $887 million), roughly 0.47% of Meta’s worldwide turnover — the largest penalty the Garante has ever imposed.
  • The feature: gallery suggestions, live since 2023, surfaces photos from the phone while browsing Facebook; it was on by default, behind one app-wide permission and a vague notice.
  • The people: about 20 million Android users in Italy; the violation is still ongoing for users who haven’t updated the app or had already granted the permission.
  • The law: Article 122 of the Italian Privacy Code, transposing the EU ePrivacy Directive — access to what’s stored on a device needs free and informed consent, regardless of transmission or harm.
  • The deadlines: report started work within 30 days; separate consent and off-by-default within 60 days; report measures within 90 days.

On its face, the feature is a convenience: while you scroll Facebook, the app suggests photos from your phone that you might want to post. It has run since 2023, switched on unless you went looking for the toggle. The permission behind that convenience, though, reached much further than the feature itself.

Italy’s data protection authority, the Garante, announced the €805 million fine against Meta Platforms Ireland on Saturday, November 14, in Rome. Meta called the fine disproportionate and plans to appeal. But the more consequential part of the ruling may be the route Italy took to get there — one that sidesteps the EU’s usual one-regulator system, and that other national authorities can now copy.

A suggestions feature with the keys to the whole archive#

According to the Garante, the feature — labelled in Italian “Ottieni suggerimenti dalla galleria quando navighi su Facebook” — sat behind a single, app-wide gallery permission. That one grant served the suggestions feature and every other part of Facebook that handles media. Before the prompt appeared, the app showed a generic notice inviting users to “enable other features,” wording the authority says gave no hint that gallery suggestions were part of the deal.

Once granted in full, the permission let the app read the device’s entire photo archive, usually with no time limit — and, in the Garante’s words, neither the user nor the authority could verify what information was actually being used. The metadata mattered as much as the images: photo and video files typically record when they were taken and on which device, and can carry location coordinates. The decision does not say which fields the app read, or what happened to them afterwards.

Meta changed the flow after the consumer group’s complaint, but the Garante counted those changes as late, and limited to people who update the app. For anyone still on an older version — and, on the Italian text of the decision, anyone who had already granted the system permission — the violation is still ongoing.

Why Ireland couldn’t take the case#

Meta is headquartered in Europe through Meta Platforms Ireland, and under the GDPR cross-border cases normally run through a lead authority in the company’s home country — Ireland’s Data Protection Commission. Meta asked the Garante to step aside in its favour. The Garante refused, because the rule it applied carries no such mechanism.

Article 122 of the Italian Privacy Code transposes the EU’s ePrivacy Directive, which governs storing information on — and reading information from — a user’s device. Unlike the GDPR, ePrivacy has no one-stop shop: each national authority enforces its own transposing law directly. That distinction is the whole ballgame for this case.

It also explains a recent defeat. In March 2026, a Rome court annulled the Garante’s €15 million fine against OpenAI, ruling that Ireland’s commission had become OpenAI’s lead authority under the GDPR — without ruling on the alleged violations themselves. The Meta decision rests on a provision the Garante says falls outside that mechanism entirely. France has already used the same route: its CNIL fined Google €325 million in September 2025 over ads in Gmail and cookies set without valid consent, relying on the French counterpart of Article 122.

What Meta has to do now#

The fine is only part of the decision. Within 30 days Meta must tell the Garante what work it has started; within 60 days it must ask Italian users for separate, specific consent — explaining what the feature is for, that it involves gallery access, and how to switch it off — and keep the feature off by default, including for people who already granted the permission. Within 90 days it must report on the measures adopted. If the deadline passes without compliance, the feature cannot run for anyone without valid consent. Counting from November 14, those deadlines fall around December 14, January 13 and February 12.

In setting the amount, the Garante weighed four aggravating factors against one mitigating one: about 20 million people affected, a violation still ongoing, a previous 2019 measure against the company, and what it described as limited cooperation during the investigation — against changes made after the complaint, judged too late and too narrow. At about 0.47% of Meta’s worldwide turnover, the fine is the largest the Garante has ever imposed, though still well below the €1.2 billion the Irish commission imposed on Meta in May 2023 over transfers of European data to the United States.

The case began in July 2025, when the Centro Tutela Consumatori Utenti di Bolzano, a consumer protection centre, filed its report. The Garante then ran its own technical analysis of the app rather than relying on the complaint alone. Sixteen months later, it had a decision.

What the case didn’t cover — and what comes next#

Two questions sit outside the decision. The proceedings did not examine whether gallery content was used to train artificial intelligence models — Meta denies that it was — and the Garante says it may separately evaluate a newer feature, launched in 2026, that sends gallery content to Meta’s cloud for processing. If the same reading of Article 122 applies there, the cloud step would add a transfer off the device to an act of access that already requires consent on its own.

The precedent is the part other companies should be reading. Because ePrivacy carries no one-stop shop, any national authority in the EU can act against any app that reads information from devices in its country, wherever the developer’s European base sits. And the consent standard the Garante applied — one permission per purpose, explained in plain terms, off by default — reaches every app that asks for broad media access once and uses it for several features.

Meta called the fine disproportionate and plans to appeal. An appeal would test whether the Garante’s reading of Article 122 — the phone as part of a person’s private sphere — survives in court.

What to watch#

Three dates matter: the 30-day report to the Garante around December 14, the 60-day consent deadline around January 13, and the 90-day measures report around February 12. Watch whether other EU regulators pick up the ePrivacy route — France already has — and whether the Garante opens its separate case on the cloud-processing feature. And watch Meta’s appeal: if the fine stands, the “one permission, one purpose, off by default” standard becomes the price of reaching into a European user’s photo gallery.

Frequently asked questions

Did Facebook copy users’ photos or use them to train AI?

The decision doesn’t say that. The Garante found the app accessed photos, videos and metadata without specific consent; it did not examine whether gallery content was used to train AI models, and Meta denies that it was. The violation was the access itself, not what happened to the data afterwards.

Why didn’t Ireland’s regulator handle the case?

Under the GDPR’s one-stop-shop system, cross-border cases usually go to the lead authority where the company is based — Ireland, for Meta. But the Garante based its case on the ePrivacy Directive, which has no one-stop-shop mechanism, so each national authority enforces its own law directly. A Rome court annulled the Garante’s earlier €15 million OpenAI fine precisely because that one was a GDPR case.

What happens if Meta misses the 60-day deadline?

The feature cannot be switched on for any user who hasn’t given valid consent. Meta must also report to the Garante within 30 days on the work it has started, and within 90 days on the measures adopted.
