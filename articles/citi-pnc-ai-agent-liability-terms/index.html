Citi and PNC rewrite their terms: give an AI agent your bank login and the risk is yours

Citibank has rewritten the fine print on AI agents, and the message to customers is blunt: hand your bank login to a third-party AI assistant, and whatever it does with your money is your problem — even if the assistant disobeys you, and even if it gets hacked.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Citibank updated its Online User Agreement this week: customers who give a third-party AI agent access to their accounts are responsible for what the agent does — even when the agent acts against their instructions, and even if the tool is hacked and criminals use their credentials. PNC changed its terms the same day, Citizens warned customers by email, and Wells Fargo's version takes effect November 24. Federal consumer protections may still limit what the banks can enforce, but no court or regulator has tested that yet.
  • The change: Citi's November Online User Agreement says that if a customer gives a third-party AI agent access, the bank will treat the agent's actions as authorized — even when they conflict with the customer's instructions.
  • The sharp edge: if the AI tool leaks the customer's credentials or is hacked, Citi says the customer is responsible for what criminals do with the account, except where applicable law says otherwise.
  • The company: PNC changed its terms on November 18; Citizens emailed customers this week; Wells Fargo's updated agreement takes effect November 24. JPMorgan and Bank of America moved in October.
  • The carve-out: the new liability applies to third-party agents. AI tools the banks provide themselves are exempt — Citi's terms note its own agents can be used without the extra liability.
  • The limit: federal Regulation E caps what a customer can be made to pay for an unauthorized electronic transfer, and a bank agreement cannot override that cap. Whether an agent's actions count as 'authorized' has not been tested in court.

The update to Citi's Online User Agreement landed this week, and Citi is not acting alone. PNC rewrote its terms the same day, Citizens emailed customers with a similar warning, and Wells Fargo's new terms take effect on November 24. JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America moved first, in October. In the space of a month, the biggest banks in the United States have gone from blocking AI agents at the door to writing down, in legal language, who pays when an agent goes wrong.

What Citi actually changed#

The new language sits in a section of Citi's Online User Agreement devoted to third-party AI agents. It starts with a warning — customers are cautioned against sharing their credentials with outside AI tools — and then spells out the consequences of doing it anyway. Provide the login or otherwise permit access, and Citi will treat everything the agent does as if the customer did it themselves. That holds even when the agent's actions run against the customer's own instructions.

The second half is the part that has drawn the most attention. If the AI tool shares or leaks the credentials, Citi says later access using them will also be treated as authorized, with an exception only where applicable law says otherwise. A Citi spokesperson told TechCrunch the bank wants customers to understand the risks of sharing banking credentials or authorizing outside tools to reach their accounts, and that the update is meant to give greater clarity about those risks.

The industry is moving together#

Citi's move completes a pattern that has formed across the industry in about a month:

PNC's version takes a different route from Citi's. Rather than only assigning blame after the fact, it defines the permitted path: connections the bank itself provides, with defined accounts, duration and permissions. Handing an agent a password or a one-time code is prohibited, and so is using browser automation or screen scraping to work around the bank's controls. Break those rules, and PNC says the customer is responsible for the resulting access and activity, to the extent the law permits.

The catch: the fine print may not hold up#

There is an important qualification that the scarier summaries leave out: a bank's terms do not automatically erase federal consumer protections. Guidance from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau under Regulation E says an agreement cannot impose more liability for an unauthorized electronic transfer than the regulation allows. If a third-party service is hacked and a thief moves money without actual authority, that may still count as an unauthorized transfer — and the customer's liability for one is capped, and low if reported quickly.

The hard cases sit in the middle. If a customer intentionally gave an agent authority and the agent then exceeded its instructions, the dispute becomes much harder — the bank's position is that the customer authorized the access, and the regulation's protections for unauthorized transfers may not apply. Business accounts can carry fewer protections than consumer accounts. And as of now, no court or regulator appears to have tested how these clauses hold up against Regulation E, and no US agency has publicly weighed in. Whether the new terms survive a real dispute over a drained account is an open question.

Why banks are drawing the line now#

The timing is not an accident. AI agents are moving into personal finance just as the tools get good enough to be useful — and dangerous. Anthropic launched a personal finance tool earlier this month that is explicitly read-only: it can look at your accounts but, in Anthropic's own words, can't move, manage or invest your money. Google, meanwhile, has made its Gemini Tasks assistant free, with examples that include reaching into a utility account through a password manager, paying bills and using payment cards. Google says sensitive actions need confirmation and the agent runs in an isolated virtual machine. Those are real controls. They do not make handing a bank password to an agent a safe integration.

Banks also have a competitive reason to draw the line. An agent that can shop around for better rates, dodge fees or move money to higher-yield accounts threatens the margins banks earn from inertia — low-interest deposits, unnecessary fees. And the pattern is not unique to banking: AI labs promise agents will change how people engage with every service provider, and the providers keep answering 'hold on a minute.' The same pushback has shown up in ride-hailing and food service. Whoever controls the pathway between the agent and the account controls the relationship — and the revenue.

What to watch#

The next date on the calendar is November 24, when Wells Fargo's updated terms take effect. The bigger questions are whether any regulator weighs in on how Regulation E treats an agent's actions, and whether the first customer who loses money to a compromised agent takes the new clauses to court. On the product side, watch whether agent makers answer with read-only defaults and bank-approved integrations — the model Anthropic chose — or keep asking users for full credentials and hope the banks blink first. Until one of those things happens, the practical rule is simple: don't give an AI agent your banking password, passcode or a shared browser session. Use a bank-approved connection, prefer read-only access, limit the accounts and duration, and check what permissions the agent already holds.

Frequently asked questions

What did Citi actually change?

Its November Online User Agreement now says that if a customer gives a third-party AI agent access to an account, the bank treats everything the agent does as authorized by the customer — even actions that conflict with the customer's instructions, and even later access using credentials the tool leaked. The exception is where applicable law says otherwise.

If my AI agent gets hacked, am I really on the hook?

Under Citi's new terms, yes — the bank says the customer is responsible for what criminals do with leaked credentials. But federal Regulation E caps liability for unauthorized electronic transfers, and a bank agreement cannot override that cap. No court or regulator has yet ruled on whether an agent's actions count as authorized, so the question is untested.

What is the safe way to use an AI agent with my finances?

Don't share your banking password, passcode or a shared browser session. Use a connection your bank provides, prefer read-only access, limit which accounts the agent can see and for how long, and review the permissions it already holds. If you have already shared credentials, change them and revoke active sessions.
