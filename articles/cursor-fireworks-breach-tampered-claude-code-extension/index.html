Cursor traces its tampered Claude Code extension to the Fireworks AI breach — up to 5,000 users may have installed it

Two weeks after a malicious copy of Anthropic’s Claude Code extension turned up in its marketplace, Cursor has traced the intrusion to an access key exposed at inference vendor Fireworks AI — and says up to 5,000 developers may have installed the tampered build before it was pulled.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 15, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Cursor says its October 29 marketplace compromise began with an access key exposed at inference vendor Fireworks AI. The attacker copied historical coding-agent traces, found unredacted credentials in some of them, moved into Cursor’s internal tools, and planted tampered copies of Anthropic’s Claude Code extension (versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313) in the marketplace cache. Up to 5,000 users may have installed the malicious build during an 11-hour window. Cursor says the incident is contained and has moved all inference off Fireworks.
  • Investigation findings published November 13, 2026, two weeks after the October 29 disclosure.
  • Entry point: an access key Cursor issued to Fireworks, which Cursor says was exposed by Fireworks. The key opened a read-only bucket holding coding-agent traces, mostly from 2025, from sessions outside Privacy Mode.
  • Fewer than 0.05% of users had traces exposed; fewer than 0.01% of customers had API credentials exposed.
  • The tampered builds — Anthropic.claude-code 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 — were placed directly on the cache serving Cursor’s VS Code extension marketplace on October 29; at most 5,000 users requested them in about 11 hours.
  • Anthropic did not publish the tampered copies, and the malware never appeared on Open VSX or Microsoft’s Visual Studio Marketplace.
  • Cursor has moved all inference off Fireworks, revoked the key, rotated credentials, rebuilt the marketplace, and engaged external forensics experts.
  • Context: Fireworks disclosed two intrusions in October; the same breach exposed Notion AI chat transcripts, covered here on November 10.

Two weeks after a malicious copy of Anthropic's Claude Code extension appeared in its extension marketplace, Cursor has published its account of how the attack unfolded — and the trail leads back to a vendor. In an update posted November 13, the company says the intrusion began with an access key it had issued to Fireworks AI, its inference provider. The key was exposed at Fireworks, Cursor says, and an attacker used it to copy coding-agent traces, harvest unredacted credentials from them, and eventually plant tampered extension builds where developers would download them.

At most 5,000 users who requested the affected versions on October 29 may have received the malicious build, which sat in the marketplace cache for about 11 hours. Cursor says the incident is contained — the last attacker activity it has seen was on November 1, and it was blocked — and that it has moved all inference traffic off Fireworks.

How a vendor key became a marketplace attack#

Cursor's investigation pieces the intrusion together as a chain, each link opening the next. It starts outside Cursor's own network: an attacker obtained an access key Cursor had issued to Fireworks, and — in Cursor's words — the key was “exposed by Fireworks.” That key opened a read-only storage bucket the two companies used to share data, and the attacker copied coding-agent traces out of it. The traces were mostly from 2025, from sessions that were not running in Privacy Mode and that Cursor was permitted to use for model training.

A small number of those traces contained credentials that had not been redacted. Starting from those credentials, the attacker reached some of the SaaS tools Cursor uses, and from there got into archived non-production source code, support data and email, usage metrics, conversation summaries, and credentials users had sent to support. With a stolen credential pulled from that internal data, the attacker then placed the modified extension packages directly into the cache that serves Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace.

One attribution note matters. The claim that Fireworks exposed the key is Cursor's account: Fireworks' own security bulletins page, last updated November 9, does not mention Cursor, and it is not public which of Fireworks' two October intrusions exposed the key. Outside reporting links the initial vendor compromise to financially motivated cloud-credential thieves, but no threat group has been named with confirmed attribution.

What the tampered extension did#

The malicious builds were versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 of Anthropic.claude-code, placed in the cache on October 29. They never appeared on the upstream marketplace: Open VSX never contained the malware, and Microsoft says the packages were not distributed through its Visual Studio Marketplace or the official npm package either. Anthropic did not publish the copies, and Cursor stresses this was not a vulnerability in Anthropic's product.

For the developers who did install one, the build carried a persistent reverse SSH backdoor and a telemetry script. Injected JavaScript ran when the extension module loaded, planted an unauthorized SSH public key, and registered background persistence — a scheduled task on Windows and a LaunchAgent on macOS — while attempting to open a reverse SSH tunnel and send host details to a remote command server. Cursor worked with the cloud provider hosting that server to take it down on October 29, the same day it removed the packages, took the marketplace offline, and published its first security notice.

Cursor's advice to anyone who installed or updated to 2.1.312 or 2.1.313 from its marketplace during the window is blunt: uninstalling the extension alone may not be enough. The company published remediation scripts to check for the backdoor and clean it up, and says developers should audit workstations for unauthorized SSH keys and persistence tasks.

What was exposed — and what was not#

The exposure numbers are small in percentage terms and specific in scope. Coding-agent traces were exposed for fewer than 0.05% of users, and Cursor says it has notified each of them directly — if you did not receive a notification, your data was not in the traces. API credentials were exposed for fewer than 0.01% of customers.

What stayed untouched matters just as much. Prompts and code from Privacy Mode sessions were not affected. Neither were customer code repositories, the production systems that run Cursor, the Cursor app and its official updates, or any release signing keys — Cursor ships from a separate code-hosting system the attacker never reached. No other extension in the marketplace was affected; Cursor says it validated that all others were authentic and has reviewed and rebuilt the store. Live traffic and active inference requests were not exposed.

A breach with a widening blast radius#

Cursor was not the only company posting an incident report on November 13. Apify and Epoch AI published their own the same day, according to a roundup of the three reports — a sign that the Fireworks breach's blast radius is still being mapped, company by company. The same breach had already exposed some Notion AI chat transcripts, and Fireworks' own disclosures describe billing data, datasets, and unfinished model weights caught up in its October intrusions.

The pattern is the story. One inference vendor, shared by many AI companies, became a single point of failure: a leaked vendor key turned into copied training traces, the traces turned into credentials, and the credentials turned into a poisoned distribution channel. The agent traces themselves are the quiet lesson — logs kept to train models turned out to hold live secrets, because some had never been redacted. Cursor has since added secret scanning across its repositories and other internal data sources, and says it rotated every exposed credential it found — then all credentials across its internal systems as a precaution.

What to watch#

  • Whether Fireworks names Cursor in its own accounting — and which of its two October intrusions exposed the key.
  • Whether more Fireworks customers disclose impact, now that three companies have published reports tied to the same breach.
  • Cursor's rebuilt marketplace: whether cached extensions now get publisher-identity or signature checks before they reach users.
  • Remediation in the wild: whether the developers who installed the tampered builds run the cleanup scripts, since uninstalling alone leaves the backdoor behind.
  • The traces question: how much sensitive material still sits in agent traces and training buckets across the industry — and who is scanning them.
