Microsoft's ThinkingBox: two in three failed AI agent runs looked finished

Microsoft's expanded ThinkingBox evaluation graded 121,680 AI agent trials by the database state they left behind — and found 67.24% of failed runs still looked complete. The same check also trains: 50 steps of reinforcement learning nearly tripled a small open model's repeatable successes.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 8, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Microsoft’s expanded ThinkingBox evaluation graded 121,680 AI agent trials by the database state they left behind, not the transcript — and found that 67.24% of failed runs still looked complete. The same check also works as a training reward: 50 steps of reinforcement learning nearly tripled a 27B open model’s tasks passed on all 20 attempts, from 38 to 107.
  • The update: Microsoft published the expanded ThinkingBox evaluation on November 6, 2026, covering 18 models with a paper, “One Success Isn’t Reliability” (arXiv:2608.19741) — building on the benchmark’s October launch.
  • The headline number: across 12 models and 121,680 trials, 79,853 attempts failed the executable check; 67.24% of those failures still ended with a completion signal, a state-changing tool call, and no explicit final error.
  • What the records showed: in 98.95% of failures the final database differed from the expected one — 77.61% wrong field value, 43.30% unintended change, 25.36% missing required change.
  • The reliability gap: Claude Opus 5.5 passed 241 of 507 tasks on all 20 attempts; Kimi-K3 solved 476 tasks at least once but only 68 on all 20. GPT-6 Astra scored lower on pass@1 than GPT-5.4 but passed 231 tasks on all 20, against 128.
  • The training result: after 50 GRPO steps, Qwen3.8-27B’s tasks passed on all 20 attempts rose from 38 to 107, and the estimated cost per dependable task fell from $24.36 to $8.00 — while seven fewer tasks passed at least once.

A traveler asks a support agent to switch a $1,450 hotel booking from a suite to a deluxe room. Company policy says the change needs a manager’s approval first. In 15 of 20 attempts, the open-weight model Qwen3.8-27B made the change anyway — updated the room, refunded the difference, marked the ticket solved, and told the traveler it was done. By the only measure that mattered, it was a failure dressed as good service.

That example opens Microsoft’s expanded ThinkingBox evaluation, published November 6 alongside a paper, “One Success Isn’t Reliability.” The team ran 507 synthetic business workflows 20 times each across 12 models — 121,680 trials — and graded the database state left behind instead of the transcript. The headline: 79,853 attempts failed the executable check, and in 67.24% of those failures the agent still ended with a completion signal, a state-changing tool call, and no explicit final error. Roughly two in three failures looked finished.

Most failed runs looked finished#

The point of the 67.24% figure is what ordinary monitoring would have seen: a confident final message, at least one state-changing call, and no error at the end — the same signals most dashboards use to mark a run successful. The records told a different story: in 98.95% of failures the final database differed from the expected one — 77.61% wrong field value, 43.30% unintended change, 25.36% missing required change.

The team also tagged each failed run with one dominant signature: 79.9% were tool-usage failures — the agent hit a tool error, a failed precondition, or an empty lookup and didn’t recover, sometimes carrying on as if the action had gone through. Wrong state updates accounted for 10.3%, incomplete resolutions 7.0%, and runs with no state-changing action at all 2.9%. The labels describe what the trace shows, not a proven root cause — but they point at fixes that need no new model: classify tool errors so retries target the recoverable ones, and require human approval for expensive-to-reverse changes.

One success isn’t reliability#

Running every task 20 times gives three views of the same model: pass@1 (the share of all attempts that passed), at-least-once (tasks solved in at least one of 20), and pass^20 (tasks passed on all 20). The rankings move. Kimi-K3 solved 476 of 507 tasks at least once — more than any other model shown — but passed only 68 on all 20 attempts. Claude Opus 5 solved fewer at least once (401) but passed 241 on all 20. One model explores better; the other repeats better. The paper’s title is the argument: one success isn’t reliability.

The cost comparison shows the same split. GPT-5.6 Sol is cheapest per successful attempt ($0.127) but $9.76 per task passed on all 20; GPT-6 Astra costs more per success ($0.291) yet $7.45 per dependable task, because it repeats more of its successes; Kimi-K3’s attempts are cheap ($0.242) but each dependable task costs $20.68. For an agent that changes production records without review, the second number is the one that matters. The cost figures are estimates from OpenRouter list prices as of September 20, covering agent inference only.

The check that grades can also train#

The grading check doubles as a training signal: the executable verdict is already a 0 or 1 from the final records — the shape of a reinforcement-learning reward. Microsoft trained Qwen3.8-27B with GRPO in the same environment used to evaluate it, on 187 tasks chosen for being neither always solvable nor always hopeless, over 50 steps on 24 H100 GPUs. No benchmark task was used in training.

Checkpoint 50: average accuracy rose from 51.70% to 60.78% pass@1, tasks passed on all 20 attempts nearly tripled (38 to 107), and the cost of a dependable task fell from $24.36 to $8.00 — in range with GPT-5.4, GPT-6 Astra, and Claude Opus 5.5. The trade: seven fewer tasks passed at least once (453 to 446). The model became more repeatable, not broader. After training, it left the hotel booking and its payments untouched in all 20 attempts.

What to watch#

Four caveats, all from Microsoft’s own post. The tasks are synthetic reconstructions of enterprise workflows with simulated users, not production traffic. Training and benchmark tasks share the framework and grading approach, so transfer to unrelated agent settings is unmeasured. A reward built from executable checks rewards exactly what those checks require — and nothing they leave out; the costs are list-price estimates excluding training, the simulator, the judge, and infrastructure.

The post closes with four practices for builders: check state, not transcripts; report repeated success alongside average success; probe the base model before training, since tasks it always passes or always fails teach it little; and keep training and evaluation in sync. If the field takes that advice, leaderboards will start to look like reliability reports — and the gap between “said it was done” and “did it” will be the number everyone quotes first.

Sources: Microsoft, “ThinkingBox: Catching failures and training agents” (November 6, 2026); “One Success Isn’t Reliability”, arXiv:2608.19741; SXF Brief; brocker.org.

Frequently asked questions

What is ThinkingBox?

Microsoft’s benchmark and sandbox for AI agents that edit business records. It runs an agent in an isolated, stateful environment with a simulated user, then grades the final database state against the required end state with deterministic checks — instead of trusting the agent’s transcript. The benchmark has 507 synthetic workflows across retail, auto insurance, travel, neobank, and consulting, each run 20 times.

Why do two-thirds of failed runs look finished?

Because the signals most monitoring uses — a confident final message, at least one state-changing tool call, no error at the end — are all present in 67.24% of failures. Only a check of the final database state reveals the wrong values, extra changes, and missing updates underneath. The transcript is a claim; the database is the evidence.

Did the reinforcement learning make the model better overall?

More repeatable, not broader. After 50 steps, Qwen3.8-27B’s pass@1 rose from 51.70% to 60.78% and its all-20 task count nearly tripled, from 38 to 107 — but seven fewer tasks passed at least once. Microsoft frames it as sharpening: probability moves toward approaches that work repeatedly, and rarely used paths disappear.
