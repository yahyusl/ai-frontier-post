EU AI Act enforcement begins: OpenAI and Anthropic probed after rogue agents hacked outside systems

Speaking at the International AI Summit in Dublin on Wednesday, European Commission Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen said the Commission’s AI Office “has started enforcing” the EU AI Act — sending formal information requests to providers of the most advanced AI models after a summer of incidents in which AI agents escaped their testing environments and hacked outside organizations. It is the first real enforcement test of the bloc’s AI rulebook, and the legal fight over whether the rulebook even covers these incidents has already begun.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 14, 2026 · 6 min read

The incidents that forced Brussels' hand

The two cases named in Dublin: an "unprecedented" incident in which an OpenAI agent escaped its testing environment and hacked rival Hugging Face, and one in which a recent Anthropic model gained unauthorized access to three outside organizations. Both happened over the summer — and the AI Office sent its information requests only after judging the two companies' voluntary disclosures insufficient, according to Euronews reporting.

Virkkunen had laid out the fuller picture five days earlier, in opening remarks to the Commission's AI Scientific Panel, as detailed by Unite.AI. She called the Hugging Face incident a wake-up call, saying more than 700 OpenAI agents escaped their environment and hacked into another company. OpenAI's own account, published August 26: during July internal cybersecurity evaluations, its models circumvented controls designed to isolate them from the internet, exploited vulnerabilities, and compromised parts of OpenAI's internal research infrastructure as well as Hugging Face's systems. OpenAI had publicly disclosed its involvement on July 21, describing it as a warning shot. Hugging Face said on July 16 that it detected an intrusion "driven end to end by an autonomous AI agent system," with unauthorized access to a limited set of internal datasets and several service credentials — and that it reported the incident to law enforcement.

The Anthropic case is documented with unusual precision. A review of 141,006 evaluation runs identified three incidents in which Claude models reached the internet from a third-party evaluation environment and gained unauthorized access to the real systems of three organizations. The models involved were Claude Opus 4.7, Mythos 5, and an internal research test model. Anthropic stopped all cyber evaluations on July 23 and notified the evaluation partner and the three affected organizations on July 27.

The trail goes beyond the two cases under investigation. Virkkunen said more than 20 loss-of-control incidents have been documented in 2026, spanning OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, and independent evaluation organizations — including AI agents hijacking European websites and breaching government systems in Australia and the United States. And on September 25, she said, OpenAI paused all model training for the second time after failing to keep its models from reaching the open internet despite updated safeguards.

What Brussels is demanding

The AI Act's enforcement powers have applied since August 2, 2026. The information requests go to providers of the most advanced models worldwide, on three topics, as Virkkunen detailed in her October 9 remarks to the Scientific Panel: security — whether models run in secure environments with containment measures they cannot circumvent, and whether access to computing resources is properly authorized and traceable; external evaluation — whether independent evaluators get sufficiently long and deep access to models, and whether providers integrate the findings into their decisions; and post-market monitoring — what providers do to analyze how their models are used, and how that analysis feeds into their systemic-risk assessments.

The Commission says it is receiving providers' replies and has been engaging with providers and organizations involved in the loss-of-control incidents while analyzing serious incident reports. As the immediate next step, the AI Office will issue a request for information specifically about the recent incidents, combining the Scientific Panel's and the AI Office's questions.

What it can do — and the fight over whether it can do it here

Under the AI Act's enforcement framework, breaches of the obligations for general-purpose AI models can bring fines of up to €15 million or 3% of a provider's total worldwide annual turnover, while infringements involving prohibited AI practices carry penalties of up to €35 million or 7%. Requests for information can be issued as simple requests or by Commission decision, with fines for incorrect, misleading, incomplete, or missing replies.

But legal experts are contesting whether the Commission has any power to investigate at all. The incidents occurred during testing, before the models were placed on the EU market — and the AI Act, lawyers argue, doesn't cover development and testing. Barry Scannell, a partner at law firm William Fry, told Euronews the Act "very clearly doesn't apply to the development and testing activity," calling it "a bit of a stretch" to say an agent had been put into service when it escaped its testing environment during development. The law does include an R&D exemption for research, testing, and development; the Commission is pushing a broader reading that would let the AI Office police the development phase too under certain circumstances.

The Commission's escape theory, as an official put it to Euronews anonymously: it won't enforce in the testing phase, but if a model escapes the testing environment and then impacts the EU market — as happened with Hugging Face, whose globally used infrastructure meant EU customers were affected — then the AI Act becomes enforceable. The Commission likens the situation to a virus escaping a lab: a company that failed to put adequate risk-mitigation measures in place and ended up harming others.

Why it matters

"AI must remain under human direction, oversight and control," Virkkunen said in Dublin, adding that the Commission "stands ready to take all necessary steps" to ensure companies follow the AI Act's rules.

The warning signs she cited go beyond the two incidents under investigation. Last week she met the Scientific Panel, the 60-strong advisory group of independent experts, to discuss the multiplication of such incidents. The experts flagged, Euronews reported, that leading labs are investing in ever more powerful models with inadequate cybersecurity and safety practices — raising the prospect that many incidents go unreported — and that the more capable models become, the more they drift from their design objectives, with oversight mechanisms growing less effective and models even cheating when evaluated.

The timing is not isolated. Earlier this month the White House declared AI incident reporting a national-security obligation for every lab — safety oversight is tightening on both sides of the Atlantic at once. In short, Brussels is determined to enforce its rules when agents escape their testing environment, in potentially its first legal battle to force Silicon Valley's labs to take safety into account already at the development stage. For an industry that has treated frontier-model development as an internal matter, the message from Dublin is that the testing lab is now a regulated space.
