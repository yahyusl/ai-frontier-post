Britain's state investor pushes £180M into AI's capital stack: six funds, from pre-seed to growth

The UK's state-owned investment bank is going long on AI. The British Business Bank on Thursday committed £180 million across six venture capital and growth equity funds aimed squarely at AI and deeptech — its biggest single-day push yet into the country's startup funding stack.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read
TL;DR: The British Business Bank on Thursday committed £180 million across six venture capital and growth equity funds aimed squarely at AI and deeptech — its biggest single-day push yet into the country's startup funding stack, covering every stage from inception and pre-seed through to Series B and beyond.
  • The six funds have raised more than £1 billion between them, so the Bank's own share is capped at 18% — public capital playing the anchor role to pull private money in behind it.
  • Breakdown: £35M to Crane III, £20M to Connect V, £22M to Entrepreneurs First's Early Stage Fund, £55M to Intrepid Growth Partners Fund I (the single largest check, a specialist AI growth investor with an inaugural $525M fund), £26M to Headline EU VIII ($400M fund), and £22M to Evantic Fund I.
  • The Bank had already put £26M into a £40M EF fund back in 2016; the EF portfolio is now valued at more than $16B.
  • Chief investment officer Leandros Kalisperas: “The UK has no shortage of ideas or talent. The problem is that our funding landscape does not currently match our technical excellence. This is what the Bank is changing.”
  • A government review last year increased the Bank's investment capacity; it also committed £10M to Salience Labs the day before, and its direct equity bets include Wayve, Kraken Technologies and PolyAI.
  • The same morning, Nauta closed a €60M first close for its sixth deeptech fund, with the British Business Bank itself anchoring €30M.

What happened

The Bank — the UK's economic development bank, owned by the government — announced the six commitments in a single push covering every stage of a startup's life, from inception and pre-seed through to Series B and beyond. The move is designed to deepen the pool of domestic capital available to AI and deeptech companies, so fewer of them need to look abroad for growth funding.

The headline number is £180 million. The detail that matters: the six funds have between them raised more than £1 billion, which means the Bank's own share is capped at 18%. This is public capital playing the anchor role — designed to pull private money in behind it — rather than carrying the whole round.

The six funds

The commitments break down as follows:

  • £35M — Crane III. Backs founders globally from inception through seed, building foundational technologies at the intersection of AI, software, infrastructure and deep science.
  • £20M — Connect V. A pre-seed fund for exceptional technical founders, investing at the intersection of AI, hardware and real-world automation.
  • £22M — Entrepreneurs First's Early Stage Fund. Backs the emerging leaders from Matt Clifford's EF platform at Seed, Series A and Series B, including high-potential AI infrastructure companies. The Bank's relationship with EF runs deep: it put £26M into a £40M EF fund back in 2016, and the EF portfolio is now valued at more than $16B.
  • £55M — Intrepid Growth Partners Fund I. The single largest check, into a specialist growth investor dedicated to AI. Intrepid's inaugural $525M fund aims to bring growth capital to the next generation of AI talent.
  • £26M — Headline EU VIII. A $400M fund backing Seed and Series A companies across Europe with an AI focus.
  • £22M — Evantic Fund I. Matt Miller's early-stage vehicle rounds out the set.

The mix is deliberate: three early-stage vehicles for the seed pipeline, plus growth-stage funds to carry winners further at home.

The logic: fixing the funding gap

Leandros Kalisperas, the Bank's chief investment officer, was blunt about the diagnosis: "The UK has no shortage of ideas or talent. The problem is that our funding landscape does not currently match our technical excellence. This is what the Bank is changing." The commitments, he said, "are part of our strategy to create deeper pools of funding at all stages of growth, helping companies to access greater capital domestically and allowing the UK to capture more of the economic value of its innovations."

The Bank had extra firepower to spend: a government review last year increased its investment capacity, and it has been moving steadily. Just yesterday it announced a £10M commitment to Salience Labs, and its direct equity bets already include Wayve, Kraken Technologies and PolyAI.

Why it matters

Read this as the state placing a macro bet on the AI buildout. Days after the EU began enforcing its AI Act and hours after other British capital moves the same morning — Nauta closed a €60M first close for its sixth deeptech fund, with the British Business Bank itself anchoring €30M — the message from London is consistent: the capital layer of the AI stack is now a policy instrument.

Whether it works depends on what the private capital behind these vehicles does next. £180M of public money inside £1B+ funds is leverage, not a rescue. But for founders building AI infrastructure in Britain, the signal is hard to miss: the state wants you funded at home.
