NYT: Lutnick pushed a narrower AI chip rule that spares data centres tied to his family’s firm

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has pushed for a narrower rule on Chinese firms renting advanced Nvidia chips overseas — a rule that would spare facilities tied to Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm his sons now run. The Times found no evidence the ties shaped his decisions.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 16, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: A New York Times investigation published November 15 says Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has pushed for a narrower American rule on Chinese firms renting advanced Nvidia chips at overseas data centres — and some of the facilities the narrower rule would spare do business with Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm he ran for decades and his sons now lead. The Times says it found no evidence the ties influenced his decisions, and the Commerce Department says he has complied with all ethics agreements. The proposal is still under debate inside the administration.

  • Commerce’s narrower draft: cover only seven countries — Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa and Norway — apply only to future chip shipments, exempt facilities already filled with US hardware, and require notification rather than an automatic licence when Chinese customers train AI models.
  • The facility in the report: Bitdeer’s A201 data centre in Johor, Malaysia, due to open in early 2027 with hundreds of millions of dollars of Nvidia’s most advanced servers, expected to serve Tencent and projected to generate $1.7 billion over five years.
  • The Cantor connection: Cantor Fitzgerald was the designated sales agent for a Bitdeer stock offering of up to $1 billion this year and helped arrange one of up to $250 million in 2024; it also holds a stake in Bitdeer through its investment in the crypto company Tether.

Washington banned direct sales of America’s most advanced AI chips to China in late 2022. It did not ban Chinese companies from renting the same chips — at data centres in Malaysia, Thailand or Indonesia, running Nvidia’s best hardware, for as long as they keep paying. That gap is now the subject of a fight inside the administration, and a New York Times investigation published November 15 puts Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick at the centre of it.

According to the Times, Lutnick has pushed for a narrower version of the rule meant to close the gap, while some of the data centres the narrower rule would spare do business with Cantor Fitzgerald — the investment firm he led for more than three decades before joining the administration, and which his sons now run. The Times says it found no evidence that Cantor’s business dealings influenced Lutnick’s decisions. That distinction matters, and this story keeps it in view throughout: what the investigation documents is an overlap, not a finding of wrongdoing.

1. The rule at the centre of the fight#

The rule under debate is a replacement for the controls governing Chinese customers’ remote use of advanced American AI chips. The direct-sales ban has held since 2022, but the rental route around it has grown into a business in its own right: overseas facilities buy Nvidia’s most advanced servers, then sell Chinese firms the computing time. Administration officials disagree over how extensively to restrict that access, with Lutnick favouring the more limited approach, according to the report.

Commerce’s draft, as described by people familiar with the talks, would be narrower than what others in the administration want in four ways. It would cover only seven countries — Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa and Norway — instead of applying worldwide. It would apply only to future chip shipments, leaving data centres already filled with American hardware alone. Companies would have to notify the government when Chinese customers use their facilities to train AI models, but would not automatically need a licence. And the rule would start at a higher computing-power threshold, so fewer Chinese activities would be covered at all.

Each of those choices has the same practical effect: more of the existing rental business keeps running. Officials who favour the broader version argue the narrower one leaves the loophole mostly intact; Lutnick has resisted restrictions he argues could damage Nvidia’s overseas business or American trade relations. The proposal is not a completed change — it remains under debate, and the outcome will decide whether overseas cloud access stays a viable route around the direct chip-sale bans.

2. The facility in Johor#

The Times built its report around a facility in southern Malaysia known as A201, run by Bitdeer, a Singapore-based company in Nvidia’s partner network. The site, in Johor, is set to open early next year packed with hundreds of millions of dollars of Nvidia’s most advanced AI servers, and is expected to serve Tencent, the Chinese technology giant — a customer the Times describes as likely, citing someone familiar with the arrangement. Bitdeer’s Malaysian facility is projected to generate $1.7 billion over five years.

Cantor’s connection to Bitdeer is documented and recent. The firm acted as the designated sales agent for a Bitdeer stock offering of up to $1 billion this year, after helping arrange one of up to $250 million in 2024. Cantor also holds a stake in Bitdeer through its investment in the crypto company Tether. And Bitdeer is not the only overlap: Cantor previously provided financial services to Gorilla Technology, which is building data centres in Thailand and Indonesia — two more countries on the draft rule’s seven-country list.

The geography is the point. Malaysia is where the rental workaround is most visible: this desk reported just yesterday that the Malaysian government is drafting a data-centre disclosure law aimed at catching AI chip smuggling, after finding chips routed through Malaysian facilities were being shipped onward. A201 sits squarely in that story — a Malaysian facility, full of American hardware, serving a Chinese customer, under a rule that would decide how much of that business Washington tolerates.

3. What the Times did — and did not — find#

The investigation’s careful sentence is the one to keep. The Times says it “found no evidence that Cantor’s business dealings influenced Lutnick’s policy decisions,” and describes those dealings as a small part of Cantor’s business. Ethics officials say he has followed the rules. A Commerce Department spokeswoman told the Times that Lutnick makes policy decisions in the best interests of the United States, does not discuss department matters with his sons, and that they do not discuss Cantor business with him.

The formal position is also on the record. Lutnick recused himself from Cantor on joining the administration, though the White House granted him a limited waiver for certain decisions with a direct and predictable effect on the firm. He transferred his remaining Cantor ownership to his family as of October 2025. Federal rules still prohibit him from using public office for his own private gain or that of friends and relatives. Cantor declined to comment, and Bitdeer did not reply to the Times.

None of that settles the question the report raises, which is about appearance and process rather than proof: a cabinet secretary is shaping a rule that reaches companies his family’s firm has done business with, under a waiver that permits him to decide matters affecting that firm. The Times’ finding is that there is no evidence the overlap shaped the outcome. The open question is whether Congress and the public accept that as the end of it.

4. What happens next#

Three things to watch. First, the rule itself: it is still a proposal under debate, and the split inside the administration — seven countries versus worldwide, future shipments versus existing facilities, notification versus licensing — will be settled in the coming weeks. Second, the oversight track: Lutnick has agreed to testify voluntarily before a Senate panel examining his handling of chip diversion cases, and Congress is weighing a bipartisan bill on Chinese access to overseas computing. Third, the trip: the report lands days before Lutnick is expected to fly to China with President Trump, where chip policy is certain to be on the agenda.

The bigger pattern is the one this story shares with the Nvidia saga that has dominated the last week: the controls on advanced AI chips are no longer a technical argument about export lists. They are a political argument about who benefits from the exceptions — and the exceptions, whether a Malaysian data centre or a rental contract, are where the money is. The Lutnick report does not allege that the secretary traded policy for profit. It shows how narrow the distance has become between the man writing the rule and the firms it would spare — and asks whether that distance is narrow enough to matter.
