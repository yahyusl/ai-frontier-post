Disney's Character.AI bill: $180 million for the people, $20 million for the tech — a court filing prices the team at 9× the code

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 18, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Disney has budgeted about $180 million over three years to compensate the roughly 66 Character.AI employees it hired away from the bankrupt chatbot startup — nine times the $20 million it is paying for the company’s technology, according to a Delaware court filing summarizing its term sheet.
  • Who: Disney has made employment offers to 66 Character.AI employees, at an estimated annual compensation run rate of about $60 million, per a term sheet in the Delaware bankruptcy filing — first summarized by Bloomberg and reported by TechCrunch on October 18, 2026.
  • People bill: About $180 million over three years on compensation and retention, including a $30 million aggregate retention pool. Of the 66, 56 had formally resigned by the filing date; 10 were awaiting US work-visa updates.
  • Tech bill: A $20 million stalking-horse bid for a non-exclusive, perpetual license to the code behind the chatbot’s training, safety and serving systems, plus nine patents.
  • What’s left out: The app, the brand, the user data, the chats and the finished models stay in the bankruptcy estate. Rival bidders can top Disney’s offer until November 20; a sale is expected to close in late November or early December.
  • Context: Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 on October 12, 2026 with roughly 86 full-time staff. Its last venture round, led by Andreessen Horowitz, valued the company at $1 billion.
  • The ratio: $180 million for the people, $20 million for the technology — the team is priced at nine times the IP.

The numbers are in on what Disney thinks Character.AI is worth — and almost all of it is payroll. A binding term sheet filed in the company's Delaware bankruptcy case, summarized by Bloomberg and reported by TechCrunch on October 18, shows Disney has budgeted about $180 million over three years to compensate the roughly 66 Character.AI employees it hired away, including a $30 million aggregate retention pool. That is nine times the $20 million it bid for the chatbot company's technology.

The filing puts the group's annual compensation run rate at about $60 million. Of the 66 employees who received offers, 56 had formally resigned from Character.AI by the time the papers were filed; the other 10 hold H-1B, TN or F-1 visas and are waiting for the US government to update their work authorizations. TechCrunch reported that the filing describes the "vast majority" of those who received offers as having accepted them. Entertainment lawyer Corey Martin, who was not involved in the deal, told Business Insider the numbers "can be eye-popping, but it's not that unusual in this space."

$20 million buys the plumbing, not the house

The technology side of the deal is strikingly narrow. Disney's $20 million is a stalking-horse bid — a floor price other buyers can top — for a non-exclusive, perpetual license to the code behind the chatbot's training, safety and serving systems, plus nine patents. The app, the brand, the user data, the chats and the finished models stay behind in the bankruptcy estate, up for grabs by whoever else wants them. Rival bids are due by November 20, and the sale is expected to close in late November or early December; if no rival shows up, the Character.AI app itself is expected to go dark, taking its user-created characters offline with it.

From cease-and-desist to CTO

The hiring spree is the second act of a relationship that began adversarially. Disney had sent Character.AI a cease-and-desist letter over copyright infringement in September 2025. A year later it named Character.AI's chief executive, Karandeep Anand, as its first-ever company-wide chief technology officer, reporting to CEO Josh D'Amaro and overseeing technology, AI platforms, product and engineering.

Character Technologies had about 86 full-time employees on September 30 and filed its voluntary Chapter 11 petition in Delaware on October 12. Per the filing, the company was hobbled by high costs and "potential litigation-related liabilities and regulatory engagement": it faces lawsuits from families who allege teenagers were harmed after relationships with its chatbots, settled several child-safety suits with Google earlier this year, and faces government investigations over child safety.

Why it matters

Strip away the bankruptcy mechanics and this is a clean number on one of the industry's favorite beliefs: in consumer AI, the team is worth more than the code. Character.AI's last venture round, led by Andreessen Horowitz, valued the company at $1 billion; in bankruptcy court, Disney is paying $180 million for three years of the people and $20 million, once, for a copy of the technology. As Startup Fortune put it: Disney isn't buying a chatbot — it's buying the team that knows how to build one.
