Heretic is over — at least under the hand that built it. Philipp Emanuel Weidmann, the developer behind the open-source tool that let anyone reshape a language model’s behavior without training it, archived the project’s repository on Saturday and posted a farewell note. The code stays public and the models built with it stay where they are, but there will be no new Heretic from its author, and no successor of his choosing.

The announcement arrived as a GitHub issue titled simply “Farewell.” Within hours, the news had spread to r/LocalLLaMA, where the thread reporting it drew hundreds of upvotes and more than a hundred comments — mostly thanks, and mostly grief, from people who had built their local-model workflows around the tool. For a project that only became widely known this autumn, the reaction was a measure of how fast it had become load-bearing infrastructure for the open-weights scene.

What Heretic was#

Heretic edited a model’s weights directly, without any training, to remove or reshape a specific behavior. You told it what to suppress and what to preserve; it tried combinations of edits, scored each one on both counts, and returned the best trade-off it found. The default target was refusal suppression — the technique the community calls abliteration — but the same machinery trimmed slop, sycophancy, overlong answers, and unwanted humor, and a plugin system let users define their own scorers and modifiers.

Its signature contribution was Arbitrary-Rank Ablation, or ARA, a modifier developed specifically for Heretic and shipped as the default. Behind it sat a search loop: a hyperparameter optimizer trying weight edits against configurable scores, including refusal rate and KL divergence from the original model, so the result kept the model’s capabilities while changing its behavior. A reproducibility system, the Heretic Grimoire, let models be rebuilt byte-for-byte from a 9-kilobyte manifest.

The numbers explain the reaction. Heretic held 43,604 stars and 5,601 forks on GitHub at the time of the archive, and it sat at number one on GitHub’s Python trending page earlier in the month. Two releases in eight days — version 2.0.0 on November 1, then 2.1.0 on November 8 — had pushed it into the open-weights mainstream, and thousands of models processed with it were published on Hugging Face. We ran it hands-on on November 18, taking a small Qwen model’s refusals from 15 out of 20 prompts to zero.

What the farewell says#

Weidmann’s note is short, and it gives no reason beyond the one sentence that opens it: as foreshadowed a while ago, he wrote, “my journey has now come to an end.” He said he was archiving the repository to preserve it in its current state and to keep issue and pull-request spam from turning it into, in his words, “a trash pile.”

Two decisions in the note stand out. First, the deliberate absence of a handover: “I am deliberately not naming a successor, nor am I suggesting any particular infrastructure,” he wrote, encouraging contributors to organize on Discord, Matrix, or elsewhere and “decide whether and how to continue the project without me.” Second, the framing of what the tool was for. “As AI continues to spread through your lives, you will find that controlling the models you use gradually transforms from an issue of Free Speech into an issue of Free Thought,” he wrote. “The fight for the ability to shape that lens yourself is nothing less than the fight for the humanity in you.”

He signed off: “Farewell, my friends. In times like these, one should be proud to be labeled a heretic.” The repository’s front page now carries a single line above everything else: “THIS REPOSITORY HAS BEEN ARCHIVED. See #601 for more information.”

What happens now#

Archived does not mean deleted. The code remains public, readable, and forkable — and with 5,601 forks already in existence, there is no shortage of starting points. Models already built with Heretic stay on Hugging Face, and the Grimoire manifests that let them be reproduced stay with them. What stops is maintenance: no new releases, no bug fixes, no compatibility work as new model architectures arrive, and no one triaging issues.

That last point is the practical one. Heretic’s value depended on keeping up with a fast-moving model landscape — new architectures, new quantization formats, new Hugging Face libraries. An unmaintained tool in that landscape rots quietly: it keeps working on the models it already supports and gradually stops working on the ones that come next. The community thread that broke the news named several smaller projects — obliteratus, abliterix, heretic-gguf — but none of them, the thread’s author noted, has Heretic’s reach or maintenance.

What to watch#

The first test is whether the community consolidates behind a single fork or scatters. Weidmann pointed contributors at Discord and Matrix rather than naming a home, so the next weeks will show whether a credible maintainer emerges or whether the project fragments across a dozen forks. Fork counts and commit activity on the leading candidates are the numbers to watch.

The second test is compatibility. The next wave of open-weight releases will reveal how quickly an unmaintained Heretic falls behind — and whether a fork can keep the ARA search loop working on new architectures. And the third is what happens to the Grimoire, the reproducibility library that made Heretic models portable: it only stays useful if someone keeps the manifests honest.

Whatever follows, the farewell marks a clean break for one of the year’s most consequential open-source AI tools — a project that put the power to reshape a model’s behavior into the hands of anyone with a gaming PC, and then, deliberately, left the question of who carries it next entirely open.

Frequently asked questions

What was Heretic?

A free, open-source tool that modified language models without training them, by editing their weights directly. Its default job was refusal suppression — removing a model’s tendency to refuse — but it could also reduce slop, sycophancy, and overlong answers. It had 43,604 stars on GitHub when its author archived it.

Why did the author archive it?

Philipp Emanuel Weidmann said in his farewell note that his “journey has now come to an end,” as he had foreshadowed earlier. He gave no further reason. He archived the repository to preserve it in its current state and to prevent issue and pull-request spam, and he deliberately named no successor.

Does archiving mean Heretic stops working?

No. The code stays public and forkable, models already built with it stay on Hugging Face, and the Grimoire reproducibility manifests stay usable. What stops is maintenance: no new releases, bug fixes, or compatibility work for new model architectures unless the community forks the project and carries it on.
