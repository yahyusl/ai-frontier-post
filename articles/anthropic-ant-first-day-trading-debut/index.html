Anthropic shares close 17% higher on first day of trading in record $81.3B IPO

Anthropic’s Class A shares opened at $111.50 on the Nasdaq and closed at $97.17, up 17% on the $83 IPO price, in the largest public offering on record. The debut makes the Claude maker the first frontier AI lab on the public markets — and sets the benchmark every other lab will now be judged against.

Five years after a group of OpenAI researchers walked out to build a safer lab, their company is now the most valuable debut in stock-market history. Anthropic’s shares began trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker ANT, closing at $97.17 — 17% above the $83 price set the night before — after a first day that swung from a 34% opening pop to a midday fade and back.

The offering raised about $81.3 billion, the biggest initial public offering ever, ahead of SpaceX’s $75 billion in June. Add $26 billion in parallel private placements to Google, Microsoft and Nvidia, and the total haul passes $107 billion — for a company founded in 2021 that most of the public had never heard of until Claude arrived in early 2023.

The numbers of the debut#

Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela, the company’s co-founders, rang the bell at 9:30 a.m. in Times Square, but the first trade came at 11:50 a.m., once Nasdaq’s opening auction for a listing of this size had cleared. The shares opened at $111.50, briefly touched $115.10, slipped as low as $92.72, and settled at $97.17.

More than 846 million shares changed hands, making ANT the most actively traded name on the Nasdaq — ahead of the 522 million SpaceX turned over on its opening day in June. At the opening price, Reuters put Anthropic’s market value at about $2 trillion, a level only a handful of listed American companies have ever reached.

Demand outran supply#

The deal was enlarged and repriced before it reached the market: Anthropic had marketed 825 million shares at $70 to $78, and ended up selling 980 million at $83, with the seven founders selling more than 23 million of their own on top. Retail demand was the largest for any IPO on record, yet only about 15% of the offering went to individual investors — below the roughly 20% targeted, still well above the typical 5–10%. Roughly half the institutions that placed orders went home empty-handed.

Analysts who opened coverage stayed above the close. Melius started at Buy with a $155 price target, Needham at Buy with $150, and the average target across five Buy ratings sat near $116.

What the listing changes#

Anthropic is the first frontier AI lab to list, and that matters beyond the ticker. Until now there was no way for ordinary investors to bet directly on a standalone model developer: the trade ran through chipmakers and cloud providers. Now there is a public price on a lab itself — a benchmark OpenAI, still private, will be measured against whether it likes it or not. Sam Altman said this week that OpenAI will go public “at some point”, after postponing plans that had pointed to this year.

Index funds will not add the stock immediately: the Morningstar US Total Market Index takes it after five trading days, the Nasdaq-100 after December 18, and the S&P 500 not for at least a year. About 15.5 billion insider shares are locked up until after Anthropic reports third-quarter results next year, and the underwriters have until November 20 to buy up to 147 million more shares — another $12.2 billion, which would take the IPO total to $93.5 billion.

The risks the market is pricing anyway#

None of this happened in a calm market: the Nasdaq is down more than 11% since early October, and on debut day the broader market fell more than 2%, with the Dow losing 1,101 points. The shares faded from their open even as the prospectus showed $33.24 billion of revenue in the nine months to September 30, up about 1,200% on the year before, and run-rate revenue of roughly $81 billion by the end of October.

The risks in the prospectus are not boilerplate, either. Anthropic spent part of this year in a standoff with the Pentagon over restrictions on military use of its models, and the department later labeled it a “supply chain risk”. Its own filing devotes dozens of pages to the possibility that people could lose control of AI systems, and to the prospect of political backlash — with data centers a hot-button issue in the midterm elections just held. Amodei used the bell ceremony to restate the pledge: the company, he said, would pace the training and deployment of its systems, “taking as long as is necessary to have confidence that they are safe and will remain under human control”, and would not bend to business or political pressure.

What to watch#

Three dates decide what happens next: November 20, when the offering closes and the underwriters’ option expires; December 18, when the Nasdaq-100 decision lands and index money starts to flow; and the first earnings report — no date set yet — the first test of that $81 billion run-rate against audited numbers rather than a prospectus. The next question is whether the business underneath can keep growing fast enough to justify a $1.5 trillion starting point.
