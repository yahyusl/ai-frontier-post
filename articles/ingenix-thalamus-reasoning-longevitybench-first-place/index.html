Ingenix Thalamus Reasoning tops LongevityBench: a 9B model beats Claude Opus 4.6 at aging biology

Ingenix, a Warsaw startup founded in 2023, announced in a release published at 3:00 AM ET today that its 9-billion-parameter Thalamus Reasoning model ranks first of 29 models on LongevityBench, the independent benchmark for AI in aging biology — ahead of Claude Opus 4.6 — and that it is open-sourcing a version called Thalamus Decision today.

What we checked: Ingenix’s press release via PR Newswire, November 9, 2026, 3:00 AM ET (08:00 UTC), and Insilico Medicine’s Cell study introducing LongevityBench (September 17, 2026).

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

The model reads methylation, expression and proteomics data directly instead of as text, and Ingenix is open-sourcing a version, Thalamus Decision, today.
  • Who: Ingenix, a Warsaw AI startup founded in 2023, led by CEO Piotr Surma.
  • What: Thalamus Reasoning, a 9-billion-parameter model for aging biology, part of Ingenix’s Biological Reasoning Engine.
  • When: announced November 9, 2026; the open-source version, Thalamus Decision, is released the same day.
  • Rank: first of 29 models on LongevityBench across 14 ranked tasks, mean rank 1.42; next best 4.42; best frontier model Claude Opus 4.6 at 9.85. First on 11 of 14 tasks.
  • Age prediction: DNA methylation age error of 3.36 years, vs 12.9 years for the best text-prompted model on the same Qwen3.5-9B base and 4.67 years for the Horvath clock; transcriptomics ordering accuracy 0.828 vs 0.671.
  • Next: a 95-billion-parameter model is in development.
The verdict: A 9-billion-parameter specialist just beat the frontier labs at reading aging biology — on a benchmark built so scale alone can’t win. The headline number is Ingenix’s own reporting, but the open-source release of Thalamus Decision is what makes this checkable instead of just claimable.

The numbers#

The ranking comes from LongevityBench, the independent benchmark for language models in cellular aging research. Ingenix says Thalamus Reasoning finished first overall across the benchmark’s 14 ranked tasks with a mean rank of 1.42 — the next best model scored 4.42, and the best frontier model, Claude Opus 4.6, scored 9.85. It also won 11 of the 14 tasks outright and beat the best non-LLM baseline on eight.

The sharpest single numbers are in age prediction. On DNA methylation data, Thalamus’s age error was 3.36 years, against 12.9 years for the best text-prompted model built on the same Qwen3.5-9B base — and better than the Horvath clock, the long-standing epigenetic age estimator, at 4.67 years. On transcriptomics ordering, it scored 0.828 against 0.671 for the best other LLM.

The benchmark itself is independently established: LongevityBench was introduced by Insilico Medicine in a Cell cover study published September 17, 2026, spanning 17 tasks across five biodata domains — clinical data, genetics, epigenetics, transcriptomics and proteomics. Its public leaderboard previously listed L-Qwen3.5-9B as the best overall system at 4.4 and Claude Opus-4.6 at 9.2, close to the figures in Ingenix’s release — which supports the direction of the claim, even though Thalamus’s own scores have not yet been independently verified.

How it reads biology differently#

The trick, Ingenix says, is what it calls Modality Fusion. Instead of turning biological measurements into text and asking a language model to read about them, Thalamus converts methylation, expression and proteomics data directly into mathematical vectors — the native format models use to read images — and reasons across those representations. Each biological profile it generates needs just four tokens; a standard LLM needs around 1.3 tokens per word.

That efficiency is the point. The model is built on Alibaba’s Qwen3.5-9B base — the same 9-billion-parameter foundation behind the benchmark’s previous best system — and Ingenix says no benchmark data was used in its training, so there was no contamination to flatter the score. Its chief executive, Piotr Surma, put it this way: “Ranking ahead of frontier labs and specialised models on an independent benchmark validates our approach of Modality Fusion. It shows that when a reasoning model reads biological data as it was measured, rather than as text written about it, meaningful results can be delivered.”

The approach lands in a busy field. Microsoft Research’s Quine is a world model of biology that already picks cancer drugs, and OpenAI’s BioBasicsBench tests AI on messy wet-lab evidence — but Thalamus is the first model to claim the top of the aging-biology leaderboard while reading raw omics data instead of text.

What open source changes#

Ingenix is open-sourcing a version of the model, called Thalamus Decision, today — November 9 — so academic and commercial researchers can inspect, reproduce and build on it. The company is also developing a 95-billion-parameter model of its own.

The team behind it is small and specific. Ingenix was founded in 2023 and is headquartered in Warsaw; its founding team came from Applica, the AI company acquired by Snowflake in 2022, whose TILT model contributed to the GPT-4 benchmark. The Biological Reasoning Engine is built for translational and clinical R&D decisions, and Ingenix says every output arrives with its reasoning trace — mechanism, evidence and caveats laid out alongside the conclusion. Moonshot’s Kimi K3.1 Pro, published as open weights last week, shows the same pattern: frontier-capable models arriving with their weights attached.

The catch#

Three caveats, stated plainly. First, the scores are Ingenix’s own, published in its press release — no independent verification of Thalamus’s LongevityBench results has been published yet, and the company has not released the per-task breakdown.

Second, the release cites 14 ranked tasks while the Cell paper describes 17 tasks in LongevityBench; which tasks were ranked and why is not explained. And third, a benchmark win is not a drug or a diagnosis. LongevityBench tests reasoning over aging data, not clinical outcomes — and the Cell study itself already showed compact fine-tuned models matching frontier systems on this benchmark. Thalamus extends that pattern; it does not invent it.

AFP take: The interesting thing here isn’t that a 9-billion-parameter model beat Claude — the Cell study already showed small specialists could do that on this benchmark. It’s that Ingenix is open-sourcing the model on the same day it claims the top spot, which lets researchers check the claim instead of taking it on trust. Watch whether independent reproductions confirm the 1.42 mean rank — and whether the 95-billion-parameter follow-up keeps the efficiency story intact.

Frequently asked questions

What is Thalamus Reasoning?

Ingenix’s 9-billion-parameter model for aging biology, part of its Biological Reasoning Engine, announced November 9, 2026. It ranks first of 29 models on LongevityBench according to Ingenix, and reads methylation, expression and proteomics data directly through a method the company calls Modality Fusion.

What is LongevityBench?

An open benchmark introduced by Insilico Medicine in a Cell study published September 17, 2026, designed to test whether AI systems can reason across five domains of aging biology — clinical data, genetics, epigenetics, transcriptomics and proteomics — rather than recall facts from training.

Is Thalamus open source?

Ingenix is open-sourcing a version called Thalamus Decision on November 9, 2026, for academic and commercial researchers. The company is also developing a 95-billion-parameter model.
