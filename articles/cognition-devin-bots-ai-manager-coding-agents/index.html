Cognition launches Devin Bots: AI managers that run teams of coding agents for weeks

Cognition's Devin Bots hand a whole engineering goal — not a task — to a persistent AI manager that plans, delegates, and keeps the context for weeks. The showcase is an Android app built in 44 days; the catch is that every number comes from Cognition itself.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 22, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Cognition launched Devin Bots on November 17, 2026: instead of handing one coding agent one task, you hand a persistent AI manager a goal — keep the Android app at parity with iOS, say — and it breaks the work down, runs teams of Devin agents in parallel, and only surfaces the decisions that need a human. Cognition calls it its biggest launch of the year. The headline proof is an Android app the company says a Bot built in 44 days, and the honest caveat is that every figure in that story is Cognition reporting on itself.
  • Launched November 17, 2026: a Devin Bot is a persistent agent with its own memory, tools, identity and permissions that leads multiple Devin coding agents in parallel, each running on its own cloud machine.
  • The shift is from task to responsibility: a task is porting one feature to Android; a responsibility is keeping Android at parity with iOS for as long as it takes.
  • The Android showcase, as Cognition reports it: a Bot created on September 28 built Devin's Android app in 44 days — 1,134 merged pull requests, 344,651 lines of Kotlin, 2,841 screenshot tests, 1,217 tickets closed, 183 human messages, none on 22 of the 44 days. None of it independently verified.
  • Early access with dozens of customers, including Decagon, Fireworks AI and Reducto; usage grew tenfold in two weeks, from a small base. No Bot-specific pricing published, no date for wider availability.
  • It lands in a race: OpenAI shipped Dots in late September and Anthropic shipped Duet in late October. Cognition's bet is that a manager built specifically around coding beats general-purpose agents that also do coding.

Devin started in March 2024 as an individual contributor: hand it a ticket and it writes the code. Devin Bots, launched November 17, 2026, sit one level up the org chart. You hand a Bot a goal, and it does the work a tech lead used to do — plan the project, split it into pieces, hand those pieces to a team of Devin agents, check the results, and come back to you only when a decision needs a human. Cognition calls it its biggest launch of 2026, and the timing is not accidental: the coding-agent market has moved, in about two months, from autocomplete to management.

The idea is simple to state and strange to sit with. The AI software engineer now has a manager, and the manager is also an AI. What makes the product different from just running more Devins is continuity: a Bot keeps its memory, its instructions and its goals across sessions that can stretch for days or weeks, so a project does not restart from zero every time someone opens a new chat.

A goal, not a task#

Cognition draws the line between a task and a responsibility. Porting one feature to Android is a task — bounded, done, forgotten. Keeping the Android app at parity with the iPhone app as both keep changing is a responsibility, and that is what a Bot is given. It investigates the goal on its own, delegates the work, evaluates what comes back, and decides what to pursue next. When it is blocked or a choice needs judgment, it surfaces a decision rather than guessing or stalling silently: a “Needs You” inbox collects those moments for a human to clear.

The mechanics are built for teams that actually ship. Each Bot has one chat for everything it owns, and it shows up in Slack as its own team member — several people can work with the same Bot, including over iMessage, and it can text or call. It picks models per stage of the work, leaning on cheaper ones for routine code, and Cognition trains its own models for software engineering and code review alongside models from outside labs. Each Bot can be given its own budget, measured against the results it delivers. And the Bots run on separate cloud computers, away from engineers’ laptops, so they never touch the passwords and keys stored there; administrators set which repositories and tools each Bot can reach, and network controls are meant to stop unauthorised data transfers even if a model follows malicious instructions hidden in content it reads.

Ready-made templates cover six engineering roles — a software factory that hunts bugs and small refactors, an Android engineer, a chief of staff, a latency optimiser, a product engineer, plus backlog, CI and release work — with a blank Bot for custom responsibilities. The safeguards, chief executive Scott Wu told Fortune, rely on rules companies set and monitor, “rather than relying on models themselves being smart enough to recognize a risky or malicious prompt.” Teams decide which changes need human review and which low-risk edits — changing a button’s colour, for example — can merge automatically.

The 44-day Android app#

The launch’s centrepiece is Cognition dogfooding its own product. On September 28, an engineer on the Bots team gave a Bot the job of building Devin’s Android app. Cognition says that 44 days later the team had an app in open testing on Google Play: 1,134 merged pull requests, 344,651 lines of Kotlin, 2,841 screenshot tests, 1,217 tickets closed. The humans involved sent 183 messages over the run — about one for every six merges — and on 22 of the 44 days they sent none at all.

Those numbers deserve their limits attached, because they are the whole story. They are Cognition reporting on its own project: no independent evaluation, no published merge or defect rates, no accounting of how much human review those 1,134 pull requests actually needed. Pull request volume is a measure of output, not of good software, and one game studio using the Bots reportedly went from roughly 50 to 1,900 weekly pull requests with a small human team overseeing the work — a striking figure that raises the same question. What was reviewed properly, what was merged, and what introduced bugs a human later had to clean up? Cognition has not published those numbers.

The most grounded signal in the launch coverage is not from Cognition at all. Citigroup chief executive Jane Fraser said on the bank’s October 13 earnings call that Devin is used by 14,000 engineers at Citi, and that it made in-house development more efficient — the bank has been in-sourcing work and cut tech vendor spend 30% year over year. That is the existing Devin product, not Bots, but it is the closest thing to a real-world result in the whole story: a large enterprise, a named executive, a specific headcount and a specific saving.

The review burden moves, it doesn’t disappear#

Here is the part that should temper the excitement. A Bot that assigns work to a team of Devin agents can produce a lot of code very fast. Somebody still has to read it. The work that used to be writing code becomes reviewing code, setting rules, and deciding what the Bot is allowed to merge on its own. For a small team, that might be a genuine win: one engineer directing a Bot that directs a team of agents. For a large team, it raises a question nobody has answered yet — what does code review look like when the volume of incoming code multiplies by thirty and the authors are all agents?

Cognition’s answer is rules: teams decide which changes need human review and which low-risk edits auto-merge. That is a sensible design. But rules are only as good as the people who write them, and the failure mode here is not a Bot that writes bad code — it is a team that sets the auto-merge rules too loosely and discovers the problem weeks later, in production, across a project the Bot has been quietly updating the whole time.

There are two more things we don’t know, and Cognition hasn’t said them yet. The first is pricing: nothing public about what a Bot costs, or how the per-Bot budget is charged. The second is availability: Bots are in early access with dozens of customers, and there is no public date for wider access. If you want to try one today, you probably can’t — and nobody knows what it will cost when you can.

Why it matters — and what to watch#

Bots did not launch into a vacuum. OpenAI shipped Dots around September 29, an always-on proactive agent inside Codex that runs teams of agents on development tasks. Anthropic followed around October 21 with Duet, a similar product connected to Claude Code. Cognition is now the third leg of the same bet: the next phase of AI coding is management, not autocomplete. The question is no longer “can an agent write this function” — it is “can a team of agents run this project while humans do something else.”

The three products differ in scope, and that difference is the whole competitive argument. Dots and Duet are general-purpose agents that also handle code. Devin Bots do coding and nothing else, coordinated around a coding agent that has been in production since 2024. If you believe the product layer matters more as models improve, Cognition’s focus is an advantage. If you believe the frontier labs will simply absorb this capability into Codex and Claude Code, it is a vulnerability. Both arguments are live, and the next year will settle which one was right.

The business behind the bet is large. Cognition was valued at $48 billion after a $2 billion funding round in September 2026, up from roughly $10 billion in 2025 and $26 billion in May. The company said its annualised revenue run rate passed $1 billion in September. Wu’s summary of the direction, given to Fortune, is the quote to keep: “Self-driving software is, I think, the direction that it’s going. But of course, it’s always up to the human where we’re driving to.”

Three things to watch: pricing, when it is finally published — the first real invoices will say more than any demo; whether the review burden eats the saving, which only shows up in customer stories Cognition does not control; and whether focus beats the frontier labs. If a coding-specific manager wins over general-purpose agents, Bots will look like the week software development became something you manage. If the labs absorb the capability, it will look like a very good demo of a product the platform underneath can give away.
