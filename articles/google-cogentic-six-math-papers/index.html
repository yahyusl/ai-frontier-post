Google's Cogentic agent posts six math papers, claiming proofs for problems open since 1964

Google Research has posted six new mathematics papers to its public GitHub repository, and every one of them was produced by an AI agent system rather than a human team. The batch, dated November 25, includes a claimed proof of a conjecture from 1964 and a claimed unconditional algorithm for factoring integers — the kind of results that, if they hold up, would count among the most striking demonstrations yet of machines doing research-level mathematics.

Google Research has posted six new mathematics papers to its public GitHub repository, and every one of them was produced by an AI agent system rather than a human team. The batch, dated November 25, includes a claimed proof of a conjecture from 1964 and a claimed unconditional algorithm for factoring integers — the kind of results that, if they hold up, would count among the most striking demonstrations yet of machines doing research-level mathematics.

The catch is the usual one for AI-generated mathematics: the papers have not been peer-reviewed, and the machine-checked proofs that would back them up have not been released. The papers say complete Lean 4 formalizations have been finished and will appear in companion submissions. Until then, these are claims — serious ones, from a real system with a published track record, but claims all the same.

What Cogentic is#

Cogentic is a multi-agent harness for automated proof discovery, built by researchers at Google Research and Google DeepMind and described in a paper posted to arXiv in September. It runs on Gemini 4 and works from a problem statement alone: an orchestrator hands out proof directions to a population of independent provers, specialized checkers attack each step adversarially, and confirmed results go into a persistent verified ledger that later rounds build on. The system is designed to need no expert hints — just the problem, and roughly a hundred Gemini calls for most problems, up to a thousand for the hardest. In its first outing it produced novel results on five open problems in online learning, auction theory and mechanism design, each checked by domain experts.

The six papers#

The November 25 batch covers number theory, game theory, communication complexity and graph theory. The six titles, as listed in the repository:

The folder now holds ten Cogentic papers in total, including earlier batches from October 30, November 18 and November 23. Google is publishing the system's output in the open, as it goes, rather than waiting for journals.

The two headline claims#

The factoring paper is the one that will draw the most attention, and the paper states its claim precisely. It constructs an unconditional, zero-error Las Vegas algorithm that returns the complete prime factorization of any integer, in worst-case expected time LN[1/3, 105] with deterministic workspace LN[1/3, 40]. Every prime factor is certified by primality testing and direct multiplication, and the argument rests on no unproven number-theoretic hypotheses. The paper also says a complete, Mathlib-only Lean 4 formalization of the whole proof has been finished and will be released in a companion submission — and that the paper and its formalization “were entirely obtained by Cogentic … using Gemini 4 as the base model,” with “no human interaction other than the initial prompt.”

The L[1/3] class matters because it is the same class as the best practical factoring method, the number-field sieve — but the sieve's speed is heuristic, assumed rather than proven, while the best previously proven bound sat at L[1/2]. Closing the gap between proven and practical factoring has been open for decades. The honest footnote: the proven constant, 105, is far larger than the sieve's roughly 1.92, so this is a theoretical landmark, not a faster way to factor numbers in practice — and certainly not a threat to encryption.

The edge-reconstruction paper claims to resolve Harary's 1964 Edge Reconstruction Conjecture for all finite simple graphs of linear edge density above an explicit threshold — graphs with at least 1,000 times as many edges as vertices — with a corollary for every graph whose minimum degree is at least 2,000. The previous strongest unconditional bound was Müller's from 1977. It carries the same statements as the factoring paper: a complete Lean 4 formalization finished, a companion submission to come, and no human interaction beyond the initial prompt.

What is not settled#

The papers are not peer-reviewed, and the repository itself carries the line “This is not an official Google product.” The Lean formalizations are the crux: the papers say they exist, but until the files appear, nobody outside Google can check them — and machine-checked proof is the whole reason to take an AI-generated proof seriously. The Reddit thread that surfaced the batch, on r/singularity, was enthusiastic but not uncritical: its top comment, with more than a hundred upvotes, pushed back on any “math is solved” reading, noting the thousands of open problems that remain. A second caution is baked into the papers themselves: their framing is asymptotic, about the class of the bound rather than the clock, and the factoring constant makes the algorithm impractical for real numbers.

Why it matters, and what to watch#

Even with every caveat attached, the batch matters for what it says about where AI research is heading. Cogentic is not a chatbot writing essays about mathematics; it is a system that produces full papers with formal proofs, and Google is now publishing its output in batches — ten papers and counting. The same week has already seen OpenAI claim an exact matrix-multiplication exponent of 9/4 and the machine-checked Riemann proof gain a human-readable reconstruction: three separate episodes in which AI systems are producing or verifying serious mathematics in public. Whether that is a coincidence or a pattern is itself worth watching.

What to watch: the companion submissions carrying the Lean formalizations; reactions from number theorists and graph theorists, who will now comb through the factoring and reconstruction arguments; and whether Cogentic's next batch arrives with independent expert verification attached from the start. If the proofs survive, the story is that an AI agent closed a 62-year-old conjecture from a single prompt. If they don't, the failure mode — where exactly the machine's reasoning broke — will be just as instructive.

Frequently asked questions

What is Cogentic?

A multi-agent harness for automated proof discovery, built by researchers at Google Research and Google DeepMind and described in an arXiv paper from September 2026. It runs on Gemini 4: an orchestrator assigns proof directions to independent provers, adversarial checkers test each step, and confirmed results accumulate in a verified ledger. It works from a problem statement alone, with no expert hints.

Are the six proofs verified?

Not yet. None of the papers has been peer-reviewed, and the Lean 4 formalizations the papers say are complete have not been released — they are promised in companion submissions. Until the formalizations appear or domain experts review the arguments, the results are claims, not settled mathematics.

Does the factoring result threaten encryption?

No, not in any practical sense. The result is about the proven asymptotic bound: an unconditional factoring algorithm in time L[1/3, 105]. The stated constant, 105, is far larger than the roughly 1.92 constant of the best practical factoring method, so the new algorithm would be far slower on real numbers. It closes a long-standing theoretical gap; it does not make factoring faster.
