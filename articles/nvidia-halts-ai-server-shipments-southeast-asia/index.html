Nvidia halts AI server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries as new US export rules bite

Nvidia has told server makers to stop shipping AI servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, days after new US export rules took effect. The halt targets the route Chinese AI firms are believed to use to reach advanced chips.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 27, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: Nvidia has halted shipments of AI servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, the Financial Times reported on November 26, a week after new US export controls took effect. Dell and Supermicro have told buyers their orders are on hold, and it is not clear whether the pause is temporary.
  • The halt: Nvidia instructed its manufacturing partners to stop shipping servers containing its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. Dell and Supermicro, which assemble Nvidia chips into finished servers, have told buyers in the four countries that their orders cannot be shipped.
  • The rule: The US Commerce Department announced an interim final rule on November 16, effective November 19, requiring licences for qualifying exports of advanced computing hardware to the four countries plus Brazil, which it identified as presenting an elevated risk of chips being diverted without authorisation.
  • The target: The controls also reach rented computing power: cloud providers have 90 days, until about February 17, to obtain licences by showing they do not serve restricted entities connected with China.
  • The stakes: A single GB200 NVL72 AI server rack costs nearly $5 million, and Malaysia has approved about RM385.7 billion in data-centre and AI infrastructure investment since 2021.
  • The context: Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned and was reinstated by the board on November 23, and is reportedly due before three House committees on December 1 to answer questions about chip exports.

Nvidia has told the companies that build its AI servers to stop shipping them to four Southeast Asian countries, days after Washington imposed new export licences aimed at cutting off a route Chinese AI developers are believed to use to reach advanced chips.

The Financial Times reported on Thursday that Dell and Supermicro — which assemble Nvidia's processors into finished servers — have told customers in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia that their orders cannot be shipped. Nvidia, Dell and Supermicro did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

It is not clear how long the halt will last. The FT's sources said Nvidia and its partners are still working out how to comply with the new controls. For buyers, the uncertainty is the problem: purchase orders that were expected to become installed computing capacity now have no dependable delivery date. Servers already running in regional data centres have not been ordered to shut down.

What the new rule does#

The pause follows a rule published by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, announced on November 16 and in force from November 19. It requires licences for qualifying exports of advanced computing hardware to or within Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, which the bureau identified as presenting an elevated risk of chips being diverted without authorisation — a government approval checkpoint rather than a blanket ban.

The rule also reaches rented computing power, the channel Washington is most concerned about: Chinese developers can pay for access to processors installed abroad without possessing a chip physically. Under the new measure, a US licence is required for certain arrangements in which an overseas data centre supplies more than 12 million in aggregate Total Processing Performance to companies connected with China, Macau or Country Group D:5 destinations. Covered cloud providers have 90 days, until about February 17, to obtain licences by demonstrating that they do not serve restricted entities.

Why these four countries#

The four countries have become favoured destinations for Chinese AI developers seeking access to Nvidia's most advanced chips, which cannot be exported to China directly. Renting capacity in a nearby data centre offered a workaround: the hardware sits outside China, but the computing power flows to whoever pays for it.

Singapore sits at the centre of the story in several ways at once. It is a destination covered by the licensing rule, a base for cloud companies, and a regional booking centre for Nvidia — the company recorded $104 billion of revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, up 72% in a year. It is also home to Megaspeed, a cloud company that rents computing capacity and has come under scrutiny over possible illegal diversion of advanced AI chips to China.

Malaysia has the most to lose from a prolonged pause. The country has approved approximately RM385.7 billion in data-centre and AI infrastructure investment since 2021. Officials in Putrajaya said the investments remain on track because major US computing companies operating locally can qualify for exemptions — but independent regional operators that rely on server deliveries from suppliers such as Dell and Supermicro face a different position, with hardware unavailable while they establish who needs a licence.

The market fallout#

Malaysian data-centre stocks fell sharply on November 26, with YTL Power among the decliners. Lenders have also started to pull back: work on a proposed $480 million loan to Aolani Cloud, a Singapore-based firm, for GPU purchases at a Malaysian data centre serving ByteDance was paused on November 18.

The disruption began before the public halt. At least two Nvidia server partners had already stopped shipments for new orders on November 20, citing legal caution and uncertainty over how the licensing process would operate. The exposure is substantial: a GB200 NVL72 rack costs nearly $5 million, so a manufacturer that builds the equipment and then fails to obtain the necessary licence could be left holding extremely expensive inventory.

The halt also lands in the middle of an unusual leadership episode at Nvidia. Jensen Huang resigned following reports of an affair with a suspected Chinese spy who had previously worked at Megaspeed, and the board reinstated him as chief executive on November 23. He is reportedly scheduled to appear before three House committees on December 1, where lawmakers are expected to question him about chip exports.

What to watch#

The first question is duration: whether the halt is a temporary pause while licences are sorted out, or a longer interruption. Watch for practical guidance from US officials on how to apply for the new licences — one cloud provider executive said federal officials had supplied none so far — and for the first licences actually being granted.

The second is the 90-day clock for cloud providers, which runs to about February 17. How strictly Washington polices rented computing power will decide whether the region's data-centre boom keeps its hardware. Brazil, covered by the same rule but not included in the reported halt, is the obvious test of how far the pause spreads.

What to watch: the first export licences granted under the November rule; whether Dell and Supermicro resume shipments; Huang's December 1 appearance before House committees; Malaysia's customs checks on re-exported servers; and whether the halt extends to Brazil.
