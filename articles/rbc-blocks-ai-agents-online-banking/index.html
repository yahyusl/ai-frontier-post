RBC blocks AI agents from online banking: Canada's biggest bank draws a line on agentic AI

Royal Bank of Canada has cut third-party personal AI agents off from its online banking, calling the risk to customers unacceptable — the first Canadian bank to do so, and the latest sign that banks will decide for themselves how far agents get into your money.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · October 29, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: RBC has blocked third-party personal AI agents from direct access to its authenticated digital properties, including online banking, and existing agent connections will stop working. The bank cites fraud, consumer harm and liability risks; staff were told the move follows a full security review. Other big Canadian banks are reported to be moving the same way, while the regulator is leaving the call to each bank.
  • What happened: RBC's customer notice, published October 27, 2026, blocks third-party personal AI agents from direct access to authenticated RBC digital properties including online banking.
  • What stops working: connections customers set up to let outside agents reach their RBC accounts. Customers themselves keep full access via the website, app, the bank's own tools and customer service.
  • Why, in the bank's words: agents need account access and may store passwords; as they grow more autonomous, the stakes of something going wrong rise from fraud and consumer harm to risks that could ripple through the financial system.
  • The internal message: executives Naim Kazmi and Erica Nielsen told staff the agents create "unacceptable customer risk" and that blocking work would begin immediately after a full security review.
  • The wider picture: major US banks had already begun turning agents away in October, and Bloomberg reported that BMO, TD and Scotiabank were also moving to restrict them.

If you have been letting an AI helper sign in to your Royal Bank account to review spending or move money around, that door is closing. Canada's largest bank has decided that outside personal AI agents may no longer connect directly to its authenticated digital services, online banking included, and links customers already set up will stop working.

The decision, published on October 27, makes RBC the first Canadian bank to draw this line, according to reporting by The Globe and Mail — and it lands in the middle of a much wider argument about who answers when an autonomous program acts with your money.

What changed#

The scope of the block is specific. It covers third-party personal agents reaching RBC accounts through authenticated channels — assistants that log in as you to check balances, analyse transactions or take actions on your behalf. It does not close your account, change your login, or touch the bank's own app and website, which keep working exactly as before.

RBC has not published a list of named products caught by the policy. Coverage has singled out Meta's Muse as the prominent example — notable, since chief executive Dave McKay had said on Bloomberg Television a week earlier that the bank was weighing a block on Muse specifically. A same-day staff message from technology and operations chief Naim Kazmi and personal banking chief Erica Nielsen said blocking work would begin immediately, and spokesperson Jeff Lanthier confirmed the policy.

Why RBC says it had to act#

The bank's public reasoning is simple. To do anything useful, a personal agent needs the keys to your accounts, and it may store your passwords to get there. While it is merely reading a statement, that is one level of exposure. Once it can move money or act across services on its own, a single misread instruction stops being an annoyance and becomes a loss.

RBC's notice puts it in terms of escalation: as these systems become more autonomous, the stakes of something going wrong climb from fraud and consumer harm to risks that could ripple through the financial system. The internal message was blunter, describing "unacceptable customer risk" and warning that agents can act beyond what customers expect, leaving the bank carrying liability for actions it never approved — liability no one has yet settled, in law or in contract, for the case where the agent, not the customer, pressed the button.

A door closing across North America#

RBC is not acting in isolation. It is the Canadian chapter of a story building all month on both sides of the border:

  • United States, mid-October: PYMNTS reported that four of the biggest US banks were turning agents away: Schwab blocking unauthorised agents, JPMorganChase's Jamie Dimon saying outside agents would not get in, Bank of America limiting agent access while it writes liability rules, and Citigroup not letting third-party agents log in or transact.
  • Canada, October 28: Bloomberg reported that Bank of Montreal, Toronto-Dominion Bank and Bank of Nova Scotia were also moving to restrict third-party agents. BMO's chief executive has separately warned Canadians against handing agents their banking logins, PINs, card numbers or social insurance numbers.
  • The regulator: the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions has warned that agentic AI introduces cybersecurity and operational risks, but is reportedly leaving each bank to decide rather than ordering a blanket ban.
  • The earlier precedent: in September, Amazon locked Meta's Muse out of its storefront. Different industry, same logic: the owner of an authenticated system gets to decide which outside agents are allowed inside it.

The pattern is clear: the institutions holding the accounts are setting the default to "no" and inviting agent platforms to negotiate their way back in on the institutions' terms.

What this means for the agent economy#

The agent industry's pitch is that it moves past answering questions into doing things. Banking is the hardest test of that pitch, because money is irreversible, heavily regulated and deeply personal.

For agent makers, the implication is a fork in the road: keep reaching accounts via credential-sharing tools and watch the doors close one bank at a time, or negotiate official, permissioned access — scoped APIs, delegated authority a customer can revoke, and liability terms agreed in advance. For customers, the point is simpler: you can still get AI help with budgeting without handing an agent your bank login, and the bank's own tools are unaffected. A January PYMNTS Intelligence report put it well: broad adoption needs "payments-grade" trust — a bar RBC says today's agents do not meet.

What to watch#

Three questions will decide whether this is a pause or a permanent settlement. First, will RBC or any peer publish criteria for approved agent access? A published standard would turn a blockade into a market. Second, will the restrictions converge, or will some banks compete by offering controlled agent access as a feature? Third, will regulators bless a standard for delegated agent authority, as open banking did for data access, or will each bank keep improvising its own perimeter?

For now, the message from Canada's biggest bank is blunt: your agent can advise you about your money, but it cannot be you at the bank. The agent economy will have to earn its way back inside — with permission, with limits, and with someone clearly responsible when things go wrong.

Frequently asked questions

Can I still bank online with RBC?

Yes. The block covers third-party personal AI agents connecting directly to RBC's authenticated digital properties. Customers can still use RBC's website, mobile app, the bank's own digital tools, and its customer service team.

Does the block cover every AI tool?

No. It targets outside personal agents that sign in to your accounts on your behalf. RBC has not published a list of named products, though coverage has singled out Meta's Muse as the prominent example of the kind of agent affected.

Are other Canadian banks doing the same?

Bloomberg reported that Bank of Montreal, Toronto-Dominion and Bank of Nova Scotia were also moving to restrict third-party agents. Canada's banking regulator, OSFI, has warned about agentic AI risks but is leaving the decision to each bank rather than ordering a blanket ban.
