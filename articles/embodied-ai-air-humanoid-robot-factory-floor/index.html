Embodied AI AIR humanoid robot learns factory jobs by watching human operators

A humanoid robot that learns its job the way a new hire does — by watching someone do it first. Swiss robotics startup Embodied AI unveiled AIR on November 6 at Humanoid Europe 2026 in Munich, a wheeled humanoid built to work on the factory floor as it is, next to the team already there.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 6, 2026 · 5 min read
TL;DR: Embodied AI unveiled AIR on November 6, 2026, a wheeled humanoid robot built to work safely beside factory teams. It learns each job from a human operator's demonstrations — typically within a month — using a tendon-driven design whose light arms give way on contact. The robot went from research to industrial humanoid in nine months and is already being tested with global manufacturers.
  • Who: Embodied AI, a Swiss robotics company and EPFL spin-out, headquartered in Lausanne with an office in Rome.
  • What: AIR, a wheeled humanoid robot for factory floors, unveiled November 6, 2026 at Humanoid Europe 2026 in Munich.
  • Safety: Tendon-driven arms with the motors, gearboxes and electronics in the torso, so the arms carry almost no mass and give way on contact; designed under ISO/TS 15066:2016, with CE marking planned for 2027.
  • Learning: AIR starts every job under a human operator's control; every run is recorded and the AI stack learns from the demonstrations until AIR can do the task itself, typically within a month.
  • Proof: Its AI stack reached 98% reliability on a cable-handling task against a 90% goal, where five funded startups tested before it met none of the targets.
  • Timeline: Built in 9 months; already being tested with global manufacturers, with signed contracts with major OEMs and plans for scaled production.

A humanoid robot that learns its job the way a new hire does — by watching someone do it first. Swiss robotics startup Embodied AI unveiled AIR on November 6 at Humanoid Europe 2026 in Munich, a wheeled humanoid built to work on the factory floor as it is, next to the team already there.

The timing is driven by a labour gap. As many as 1.9 million US manufacturing jobs could go unfilled by 2033, according to Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute. Some 22% of manufacturers plan to use physical AI, such as humanoid robots, within two years — more than double today’s 9% — according to the Manufacturing Leadership Council. But Deloitte also found that more than 80% of factory task hours are expected to stay human-driven. The opportunity, Embodied AI argues, is in helping those people, not replacing them.

Safety is built into the body, not bolted on in software

AIR is designed with a tendon-driven architecture. The motors, gearboxes and electronics sit in its torso, and its arms are light and moved by tendons, so they carry almost no mass and give way on contact. It runs on wheels rather than legs, because factory floors are flat and wheels are more stable. The point, the company says, is that safety comes from how the robot is built, not from software holding it back. AIR is designed under ISO/TS 15066:2016, the standard for collaborative robots, with CE marking planned for 2027.

That matters because most humanoids today are still demonstrations, and most industrial robots are built for heavy-duty tasks and work behind cages, away from the people who know the job best. AIR is built for the high-dexterity tasks that are still performed manually — the repetitive work that changes too often for a conventional robot to be worth programming.

Learning on the job

To learn a job and guarantee factory uptime, AIR starts every task under the control of a human operator, so it is productive from its first shift. Every run is recorded, and Embodied AI’s AI stack learns from those demonstrations until AIR can do the task itself — typically within a month. The operator then stays on hand to cover for anything the robot hasn’t seen before. What one robot learns, the next can use.

The stack is already proven in paid factory deployments, in the company’s two-arm industrial cell. When a leading consumer electronics manufacturer tested five funded startups on a cable-handling task, none met its targets. Embodied AI met them all, reaching 98% reliability against a 90% goal.

Nine months from research to industrial humanoid

Embodied AI went from research to an industrial humanoid in nine months, and says it signed contracts with major OEMs globally with just a fraction of the spending other robotics companies needed to get this far. AIR is now being tested with global manufacturers, and the company is starting plans for scaled production.

“Hallucination of AI models is acceptable in the digital space because humans review, reject, or correct the output before acting on it. In robotics, however, incorrect actions have immediate and irreversible physical consequences. When AI is used to taking physical actions, errors can cause severe damage or endanger human lives. Safety and reliability are non-negotiable requirements of physical AI to enter the real world at scale. These are the principles that guided the design of AIR,” said Francesco Stella, co-founder and CEO of Embodied AI.

AIR’s arm design grew out of Stella’s PhD at EPFL, the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne. Stella, 28, previously also researched at MIT CSAIL. He co-founded the company with CTO Kai Junge and COO Max Polzin, both robotics PhDs with backgrounds at Cambridge, ETH Zurich and EPFL. The company is backed by Faber, which led its first round, along with TechShop, Look AI Ventures, Plug and Play, and Vento.

A crowded factory floor

AIR arrives as embodied AI moves from demos to deployments. Cainiao opened its warehouses this week to train embodied AI robots, and Serve Robotics is selling its delivery-robot stack as a physical AI service. Agility’s Digit humanoid is already working in GXO warehouses. AIR’s pitch is different: a robot that goes onto the factory floor as it is, learns from the people already there, and is safe because of how it is built. The unveiling is at 16:00 CET today; whether manufacturers buy that pitch will show in the contracts that follow.
