OpenAI brings computer-use agents to healthcare — GPT-6 Astra rated safe on 99% of 325 EHR tasks

OpenAI is moving its computer-use agents into hospitals and clinics. Announced on November 16, the launch lets GPT-6 Astra work inside the electronic health records and payer portals that care teams already use — drafting orders, preparing prior authorizations, closing out referrals — while a clinician reviews the result. Physicians who assessed the system rated 99% of 325 reviewed tasks clinically safe.

Healthcare runs on software that does not talk to itself. A clinician recommends an MRI; the record lives in one system, the insurer's portal in another, and someone in between retypes the same facts by hand. OpenAI's answer, announced on November 16 at the HLTH26 conference, is to let an AI agent do the retyping: computer-use agents that click, scroll and type inside the applications care teams already use.

The agents are available now through the OpenAI API, ChatGPT for Healthcare and ChatGPT for Clinicians, powered by GPT-6 Astra — with a cheaper sibling, GPT-6 Luna, for high-volume administrative work. The pitch is not diagnosis. It is paperwork: the referrals, authorizations and discharge plans that consume hours of clinical and administrative time.

What the agents actually do#

A computer-use agent works the way a person at a keyboard does: it reads the screen, moves the cursor, clicks buttons and types into forms, moving between applications when a workflow demands it. OpenAI's demo, built on synthetic patient data, walks through the pattern. A patient messages about lower back pain; the agent reviews visit notes, physical therapy records and prior imaging in the electronic health record, drafts an MRI order, an encounter note and a reply for the clinician to review — then uses the same records to prepare the prior authorization request in the insurer's portal for a specialist to check.

The distinction that matters is where the agent stops. Drafts go to clinicians for review; key actions stay with humans. Organizations can add approval steps, restrict which applications and records an agent can touch, and keep an audit log of every action. Patient data stays under the organization's control and, OpenAI says, is not used to train its models; HIPAA-covered use requires a Business Associate Agreement and the required security settings.

The safety numbers — and what they don't prove#

OpenAI's headline number comes from its own evaluation of GPT-6 Astra on EHR inbox work — the multi-step tasks clinicians handle in their inboxes, shaped with physician input. Reviewers judged whether inaccuracies, omissions or actions in the model's work could plausibly lead to patient harm. They rated 99% of 325 reviewed tasks safe, and rated the work “very helpful” or “extremely helpful” in 93% of 646 assessments.

The administrative side was tested separately, on HealthAdminBench — an independent benchmark from Stanford researchers covering prior authorizations, appeals and medical-equipment orders. Astra earned 94.3% of available rubric points; Luna earned 91.8% while costing 8.5 cents per task, 98% less than Astra, both at maximum reasoning effort.

Read the numbers for what they are: physician assessments of the model's work, not measured patient outcomes. The 1% of tasks not rated safe is the number to watch — Karan Singhal, OpenAI's head of Health AI, told Fierce Healthcare that researchers found no case in that group where the model created a specific, plausible risk of patient harm. But the evaluation is OpenAI's own, and independent replication is what would turn a vendor result into a settled one.

What the first partners are doing#

Four organizations are named as the first to build on the capability. CVS Health is the furthest along: one production agent cleared a months-long document-sending backlog over a weekend, and another handles the system steps behind tens of thousands of specialist consults a day. CVS is also open-sourcing its agent kit, built with OpenAI's Agents SDK, so other health systems can repeat the setup.

Cigna Healthcare is using the agents to assemble member context for care managers who previously navigated more than ten separate clinical and administrative systems; the agent pulls benefits, coverage, providers and prior interactions together in seconds. Abridge is building discharge coordination — agents working concurrently on medication access and home-health arrangements while the clinician keeps the decisions that need judgment. Ambience is focused on governance: its prior-authorization agent stops and asks the clinician when the record is ambiguous, and health systems can pause any agent and review a step-by-step log. Assort, a startup, is closing the referral loop inside Athena's EHR, completing the final step in the interface where no API reaches.

The second half of the launch#

The agents arrived with a companion update to ChatGPT for Healthcare: stronger clinical search — how the product finds, evaluates and cites medical evidence — and more than 20 new healthcare plugins, covering scheduling, referrals, records and insurance tools. The combination is the point: the agent can act inside existing software, and the plugins give it sanctioned routes into the systems it needs. It also follows OpenAI's September integration with Epic, which brought authorized patient context into ChatGPT — the groundwork this launch builds on.

What to watch#

Three things decide whether this becomes infrastructure or a demo. First, independent evaluation: the 99% and 94.3% figures are OpenAI's, and hospitals will want their own numbers before letting agents touch live records. Second, deployment reality: the launch partners are large, well-resourced organizations with governance teams; smaller clinics are where the paperwork burden is heaviest and the oversight thinnest. Third, the audit trail: Ambience's stop-and-ask design is the model to watch, because the first time an agent files a wrong authorization at scale, the question will not be whether the model was 99% safe — it will be who caught the 1%.

For now, the honest summary is narrower than the headline: a real product, available today, aimed at the least glamorous and most expensive part of healthcare — the paperwork between the decision and the care. The safety numbers are encouraging and self-reported. What happens in production, under audit, is the story from here.
