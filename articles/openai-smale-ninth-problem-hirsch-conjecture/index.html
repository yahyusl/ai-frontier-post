OpenAI claims to have answered Smale’s ninth problem — and disproved the polynomial Hirsch conjecture

On November 24, OpenAI added nine manuscripts to its public mathematics repository on GitHub — and two of them claim to settle problems that have stood open for decades: a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, which OpenAI frames as an answer to Smale’s ninth problem, and a construction it says disproves the polynomial Hirsch conjecture. Like everything else in the batch, neither claim has been peer-reviewed, and the formal verification that accompanies them covers only part of what the papers assert.

The Lean formalization checks the linear-programming result only for rational data, and the real-number case Smale’s problem asks about is not machine-checked. Nothing in the batch has been peer-reviewed.
  • What happened: on November 24, 2026, OpenAI added nine manuscripts to its public GitHub math repository, in two families: seven on tensor geometry, two on linear programming.
  • The linear-programming claim: a deterministic algorithm whose step count is bounded by a polynomial in the numbers of variables and constraints alone — not in the bit length of the numbers involved. OpenAI presents it as answering Smale’s ninth problem.
  • The Hirsch claim: for every sufficiently large n, a bounded simple rational polytope with exactly n facets and a diameter that grows faster than every fixed power of n — which would kill the polynomial version of the Hirsch conjecture.
  • Also in the batch: a claimed proof of the Erdős–Rado fixed-petal sunflower conjecture, with a constant base independent of the set size.

A week that began with two labs racing to claim the same proof ended with one of them posting nine more. On November 24, OpenAI added nine manuscripts to its public mathematics repository on GitHub, two of which claim to settle problems open for decades: a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, and a construction that supposedly disproves the polynomial Hirsch conjecture. It is the latest volley in a remarkable month for AI-assisted mathematics — and, like everything else in it, it arrives as a claim, not a verdict.

The ninth problem, in plain terms#

Linear programming — maximize a linear objective subject to linear constraints — sits underneath airline scheduling, supply chains, and portfolio construction. In 1979, Leonid Khachiyan showed it could be solved in time polynomial in the number of bits needed to write the problem down — but the running time depended on the size of the numbers, so a gap remained. Nimrod Megiddo asked whether the dimensions alone could bound the number of arithmetic steps; Robert Tarjan raised the same question for real-number data; and Stephen Smale put it ninth on his 1998 list of mathematical problems for the 21st century.

OpenAI’s paper, “Strongly polynomial linear programming,” claims to close that gap. Its main theorem says that for arbitrary real data, a deterministic algorithm returns one of three certificates — an optimal primal-dual pair, a feasible point with an improving ray, or a short certificate of infeasibility — and the step count is bounded by a polynomial in the numbers of variables and constraints. The paper says the same approach also covers convex quadratic programming and deterministic turn-based mean-payoff games.

Two caveats matter. The paper does not present its method as a faster practical solver, and it lists open questions about its own approach. And the Lean formalization is narrower than the headline: it proves the result for rational data, while the real-number case Smale’s problem asks about is stated in the paper but not machine-checked.

The polytope that walks too far#

The companion paper targets a different old question. The Hirsch conjecture, proposed by Warren Hirsch in 1957, said that the diameter of a polytope — the largest number of edge-steps between any two vertices — is at most the number of facets minus the dimension. The diameter matters because the simplex method walks from vertex to vertex along edges; a bound on the diameter is a bound on how long that walk can be.

Francisco Santos disproved the original linear bound in 2010 with a 43-dimensional polytope with 86 facets. But the polynomial version — which only asked for some polynomial bound in the number of facets — stayed open. OpenAI’s paper claims to finish it off: for every sufficiently large n, a bounded simple rational polytope with exactly n facets, dimension growing slower than any power of n, and a diameter growing faster than every fixed power of n. Lean proves the refutation for bounded simple rational polytopes.

Sunflowers, and the rest of the batch#

The two optimization papers are only part of the November 24 drop. The batch also claims a proof of the Erdős–Rado fixed-petal sunflower conjecture: a sunflower is a group of r sets whose pairwise intersections are all the same, and the claim is that any large enough family of sets of size at most k must contain one with r petals, with a base that depends only on r. The classical bound grows like k factorial, so a base independent of k is the claimed advance. Lean proves that such a constant exists, though the paper’s explicit formula for it remains unformalized.

The rest of the batch is tensor geometry, including the matrix-multiplication exponent claim — that the exponent is exactly 9/4 — which we covered separately, and two papers OpenAI says subsume its own November 23 preprint claiming VP ≠ VNP. Anthropic posted its own VP ≠ VNP claim the same day.

What has actually been checked#

As of November 25, no named mathematician was on record assessing any of the November 23 or 24 papers. OpenAI calls the manuscripts “raw research data” from an unreleased internal model, and says some unformalized results “could have issues.” The caution has precedent: on October 7, the repository withdrew three Hodge conjecture manuscripts after a sign error and revised fourteen others. For its October 26 claim about the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a working group led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi, and Ryan Williams agreed to examine the proof; no comparable review group has been named for the new papers. Neither lab announced these results in its newsroom. And the Lean code has a limit worth stating plainly: a computer can check every step of a formalization, but no machine checks that the formalization says what the paper claims it says.

Why it matters, and what to watch#

If the linear-programming result holds, it closes a question that has structured optimization theory since 1979 — the gap between “polynomial in the size of the numbers” and “polynomial in the shape of the problem.” If the Hirsch refutation holds, it ends the last version of a conjecture that has shaped how theorists think about the simplex method’s worst case. Neither changes practice tomorrow: the paper is not a faster solver, and the Hirsch construction lives in dimensions far beyond real problems. What to watch: whether a review group forms, and whether Lean’s coverage extends to the real-number case. The bottleneck is no longer producing candidate answers. It is checking them.

Frequently asked questions

What is Smale's ninth problem?

It asks whether linear programming can be solved by an algorithm whose number of arithmetic steps is bounded by a polynomial in the numbers of variables and constraints alone — the shape of the problem — rather than in the number of bits needed to write the numbers down. Stephen Smale put it ninth on his 1998 list of mathematical problems for the 21st century. OpenAI claims its November 24 paper gives such an algorithm.

What is the Hirsch conjecture?

Proposed by Warren Hirsch in 1957, it said the diameter of a polytope — the most edge-steps between any two vertices — is at most the number of facets minus the dimension. Francisco Santos disproved that linear bound in 2010 with a 43-dimensional example. The polynomial version, which only asked for a polynomial bound, stayed open until OpenAI's November 24 claim.

Have these results been verified?

Not by peer review. Lean formalizations check parts of the claims — the linear-programming result for rational data, the Hirsch refutation for bounded simple rational polytopes — but the real-number case of the linear-programming claim and several quantitative bounds are not machine-checked, and no outside mathematician had publicly assessed the papers as of November 25.
