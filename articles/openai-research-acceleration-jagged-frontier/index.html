OpenAI says its coding agents now finish 46% of two-to-four-hour research tasks on their own

OpenAI’s second report on AI-assisted research finds its coding agents completing nearly half of multi-hour research tasks with no human stepping in — but the gains are jagged, and the hardest engineering work still belongs to people.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: OpenAI’s coding agents now complete 46% of two-to-four-hour research tasks with no human intervention, up from 23% in March — but the gains are uneven: agents ace monitoring training runs (79%) and stumble on writing inference and serving code (25%). Researchers are using agents 11 times more than in January, and the median researcher now spends the equivalent of $2,400 a day on them.
  • Zero-intervention success on tasks estimated at two to four hours of human work rose from 23% in March to 46% in October; for four-to-eight-hour tasks it rose from 13% to 35%.
  • By task (Aug 1–Oct 18): monitoring training runs 79%, answering technical questions 72%, launching training runs 35%, building eval, RL and agent environments 29%, writing inference and serving code 25%.
  • Median researcher daily agent use reached $2,400 at API list prices in October, up 62% from September; the 90th percentile hit $23,000, up 105%.
  • Agent workdays per researcher rose from 0.5 to 5.7 — an elevenfold increase; by mid-October more than two-thirds of researchers averaged a daily peak of at least four concurrent agents.
  • Safety & Alignment saw the largest absolute increase in documented research workweeks, up 77% from January to September.

OpenAI's researchers are no longer just building AI models. Increasingly, the models are doing part of the building. In a report published November 18, the company says its coding agents now finish 46% of research tasks estimated to take a human two to four hours — with no person stepping in. In March, that figure was 23%.

The report, 'Research acceleration: The jagged frontier,' is OpenAI's second public accounting of how agents are reshaping its own research organization, updating a September analysis. It is also the company's latest argument that AI labs should publicly track indicators of recursive self-improvement — AI accelerating the development of future AI — while insisting the disclosure is not a plan to pursue full RSI.

The headline numbers: 46%, up from 23%#

OpenAI's core measure is zero-intervention success: whether an agent finished a delegated task without a person stepping in, as recorded in the company's internal logs. For tasks estimated to require two to four hours of human work, that rate rose from 23% in March to 46% in October. For four-to-eight-hour tasks it rose from 13% to 35% over the same period.

The activity measures point the same way. Logged experiments per active experimenter averaged 1.68 times the 2025 baseline over the four weeks ending October 18, and lines of code changed per active contributor kept climbing sharply through 2026. On OpenAI's internal version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, its models' frontier has risen 16.4 points a year since August 2025, against a 14-point industry trend.

The jagged part: brilliant at watching, weak at building#

The report's framing word is 'jagged' for a reason: the averages hide a sharp split. Agents are strong at watching and talking about research, and much weaker at the engineering that turns research into working systems.

The breakdown covers August 1 to October 18. OpenAI warns that the weaker categories could become bottlenecks to overall research gains even as the headline numbers climb: if agents can monitor a training run but not write the code that serves the resulting model, the loop stalls at the last step.

Usage is surging — and the heaviest users are pulling away#

The other headline is how much researchers now lean on these tools. At API list prices, the median researcher's daily agent use reached $2,400 in October, up 62% from September. At the 90th percentile it reached $23,000 a day, up 105%. Measured in time rather than money, agent workdays per researcher rose from 0.5 to 5.7 — an elevenfold increase — with pretraining and posttraining teams averaging roughly eight agent workdays per researcher per calendar day.

Concurrency has become the norm: by mid-October, more than two-thirds of included researchers averaged a daily peak of at least four agents working at once, and more than 10% averaged at least sixteen. In a survey of 101 research staff, the median respondent said they would trade 40% of team headcount or 30% of team compute to keep frontier coding agents for three months — and estimated their team would need three months to produce a month's work without them. In a separate late-October survey, 27 of 28 respondents chose or leaned toward the latest internal model over a mid-career human research engineer assisted by a 2024 model.

The caveats OpenAI flags itself#

OpenAI attaches a significant qualification to its own numbers. As agents grew more capable, researchers changed which tasks they delegated within each duration bucket — so part of the 23%-to-46% rise may reflect researchers getting better at picking tasks agents can finish, not agents getting better. The figures are the company's internal estimates, not an independent evaluation, and the estimates of how long a skilled person would take on each task still need more validation. A GPT-6 Astra agent assessed a sample of the coding-agent logs used in the analysis.

The ideas behind the models are still human, by the report's own account. Researchers said people came up with every idea they named as most important to the GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.2 Astra releases, with models helping to implement them. One pretraining researcher credited Codex with identifying key insights while working through an idea together — the closest the report comes to a model originating research direction.

One more shift stands out: Safety & Alignment saw the largest absolute increase in documented research workweeks of any area, up 77% from January to September, with growth picking up after OpenAI's response to the July Hugging Face incident. For all the agent usage, internal deployment of Codex, ChatGPT and the API still accounts for only 2% of the company's total research compute.

What to watch#

Three things decide whether this report ages as a milestone or a curiosity. First, the weak categories: inference and serving code at 25% is the bottleneck to watch — if that number climbs, the whole loop accelerates; if it stays put, the jagged frontier stays jagged. Second, whether other labs publish comparable indicators. OpenAI explicitly hopes its report sustains shared measurement norms across the industry. Third, the governance question the report is really about: whether transparency on RSI indicators becomes a norm labs are held to, or a one-off disclosure. OpenAI is careful to say its reporting should not be read as an intention to pursue full recursive self-improvement. The numbers, though, are now on the record — and the next update will be judged against them.

Frequently asked questions

What does “zero-intervention success” mean?

Whether an agent finished a delegated task without a person stepping in, as recorded in OpenAI’s internal logs. OpenAI estimates the rate for each task-duration bucket; it is the report’s core measure of how far agents can be trusted to work alone.

Does this mean AI is now doing OpenAI’s research?

No. The report says humans remain central: people came up with the ideas behind the GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.2 Astra releases, with models helping to implement them, and performance is highly uneven by task — 79% on monitoring training runs but 25% on writing inference and serving code.

What is recursive self-improvement, and why is OpenAI tracking it?

Recursive self-improvement is the process by which AI accelerates the development of future AI systems. OpenAI argues labs should be required to track and publicly report indicators of it, while saying this reporting should not be read as an intention to pursue full RSI.
