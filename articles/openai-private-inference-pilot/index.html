OpenAI starts testing Private Inference: your prompts stay sealed while its models work

OpenAI has begun testing Private Inference with a small group of business customers, running their prompts on isolated hardware that even OpenAI cannot inspect — the next step in a privacy push now spanning every frontier lab.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 26, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: OpenAI is testing Private Inference with a small group of business customers: prompts and responses are processed on isolated, verifiable hardware using confidential computing, so OpenAI itself cannot inspect them. A companion system handles safety reviews without giving OpenAI staff access to the content. No supported models, pricing, or general-availability date have been announced yet.
  • What it is: Private Inference, part of OpenAI Private Intelligence, processes business prompts on isolated hardware with confidential computing and verifiable controls.
  • Who has it: a small group of business customers, announced by CEO Sam Altman on November 25, 2026. It is listed as an alpha with an API preview.
  • What is new: the protection covers data while the model is computing on it — not just data at rest or in transit, which existing controls already cover.
  • The catch: no supported models, pricing, or general-availability date yet; OpenAI says it is still scaling up the confidential-compute capacity the feature needs.

OpenAI has started testing a feature that answers the question every cautious enterprise has asked since the first API key was issued: who can see what we send the model? For a small group of customers trying Private Inference, the answer is supposed to be nobody — not even OpenAI. Chief executive Sam Altman announced the pilot on X on November 25, saying stronger privacy protections would make frontier AI useful for a lot more work. The feature sits inside OpenAI Private Intelligence, the company’s privacy framework for business use, and it is the most concrete step yet toward a model that can work on sensitive material without being able to read it.

The timing matters. A day earlier, Anthropic opened its own enterprise privacy system, Enterprise Frontier Safeguards, to public beta. Google is building a similar approach under the name Private AI Compute. Privacy — long a compliance checkbox — has become a competitive front among the frontier labs, and the prize is the work that regulated industries have so far kept away from AI entirely: health records, financial data, proprietary research.

What Private Inference actually changes#

Most enterprise AI privacy to date has covered data at rest and in transit: encryption, retention settings, regional storage. Private Inference targets the gap in between — the moment the model is actually computing on the prompt. It runs queries on specially isolated hardware using confidential computing, with controls OpenAI says can be independently verified. Prompts and responses stay protected while the model processes them, so the company running the model cannot inspect the content it is working on.

That is a different promise from the one businesses already get. OpenAI does not use business-product inputs and outputs for training by default. Private Inference is about processing, not training — and the distinction matters, because the most sensitive workflows were never primarily blocked by training fears. A bank’s customer files, a hospital’s patient notes, a law firm’s case material: the blocker was that sending the data to a third party meant the third party could, in principle, read it. Private Inference is designed to remove that “in principle.”

Safety checks that never see your data#

A privacy feature that blinds the provider creates an obvious problem: how do you catch abuse if you cannot see what is being processed? OpenAI’s answer is a companion system, Zero Data Retention with Private Safety Processing. Flagged interactions are encrypted and stored in the customer’s own cloud, not OpenAI’s. The review itself runs inside a hardware-attested runtime with human access disabled, and what comes out of it is limited to predefined safety signals and operational metadata.

Customers keep the encrypted records for at least 30 days, can connect storage on AWS, Azure, Google Cloud or Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, and can add a customer-controlled encryption layer through Enterprise Key Management. Revoking the customer’s key blocks decryption of retained records — though it does not erase records or undo processing that has already happened. The design is a bet that safety review can be automated and attested rather than read, and that enterprise customers will trust the attestation.

Why now — and what is still missing#

Private Inference is not new as a concept. OpenAI introduced Private Intelligence at its DevDay in September and described Private Inference then as a preview coming in the fall. Altman’s post turns the preview into a live test with real customers — but the test is small, and OpenAI has said nothing yet about which models it supports, what it will cost, or when it becomes generally available. The company says it is scaling up the confidential-compute capacity the feature needs; the rollout is as much a hardware problem as a software one.

For now the feature is aimed at business customers, and organizations can register interest through OpenAI’s product page, where it is listed as an alpha with an API preview. Shekar, the OpenAI employee leading the effort, described the pilot as a first step toward “private intelligence for everyone” and said the company was obtaining confidential-compute capacity for a much larger rollout.

What to watch#

Three things will decide whether this becomes a real product or a permanent pilot. First, independent verification: the whole pitch rests on hardware customers can check for themselves, and the first audits will matter more than any announcement. Second, breadth: which models get the treatment, and whether the privacy survives the agent workflows — tools, files, approvals — that businesses actually use. Third, the competitive answer: Google’s parallel effort and Anthropic’s safeguards mean enterprise privacy is now a three-way race, and the lab that makes the strongest verifiable promise at a workable price will set the terms for the rest. If Private Inference ships broadly, the quiet consequence is larger than any single feature: the most sensitive work in the economy stops being off-limits to frontier models.

Frequently asked questions

What is OpenAI Private Inference?

An alpha feature, part of OpenAI Private Intelligence, that processes business customers’ prompts and responses on isolated hardware using confidential computing, so the data stays protected while the model works on it — including from OpenAI itself.

How is it different from Zero Data Retention?

Zero Data Retention is about what happens after processing: for eligible API workflows, OpenAI does not keep the prompt or the response. Private Inference is about what happens during processing, keeping the content sealed while the model computes. The two are designed to work together.

When will it be generally available?

OpenAI has not announced a date, a price, or which models it supports. The feature is in alpha testing with a small group of business customers, and OpenAI says it is scaling up the confidential-compute capacity needed to expand it.

How does OpenAI check for abuse if it cannot see the data?

Through Zero Data Retention with Private Safety Processing: flagged interactions are encrypted and stored in the customer’s own cloud, and the review runs inside a hardware-attested runtime with human access disabled. Only predefined safety signals leave the runtime.
