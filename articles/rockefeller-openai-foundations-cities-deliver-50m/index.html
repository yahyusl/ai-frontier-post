Rockefeller and OpenAI Foundations launch $50M ‘Cities Deliver’ fund for AI in city hall — applications open today

The Rockefeller Foundation and the OpenAI Foundation launched Wednesday Cities Deliver: AI Solutions for Public Services, a $50 million initiative to fund frontier-AI projects built alongside city governments. The global open call runs from today through November 29, awarding grants of $500,000 to $2 million with the stated goal of reaching up to 100 million city residents.

This is philanthropy’s biggest bet yet that AI’s next frontier is the unglamorous one: permits, disease surveillance, emergency response. Through Cities Deliver, the two foundations will fund eligible nonprofit and civil-society organizations partnering with city governments on projects across public health, economic mobility, environmental resilience, and civic engagement. The money is managed by RF Catalytic Capital, an affiliate of The Rockefeller Foundation.

Two tracks, one bet: seed pilots and proven scale-ups

The call for proposals runs two tracks, as detailed in Unite.AI’s breakdown. Seed grants of $500,000 to under $1 million back early concepts ready for a first city pilot — refining the idea with city partners, building and testing a prototype. Scale grants of $1 million to $2 million go to solutions already piloted in a city with early results, funding expansion within that city or extension to another one.

Every grant runs 24 months: 50 percent disbursed up front, the rest released on a documented mid-term milestone, with no guarantee of follow-on funding. The support goes beyond money: a facilitated peer-learning cohort with webinars and mentorship, occasional AI product and engineering help subject to capacity, and access to both foundations’ networks of city leaders, funders, and technical partners.

Built on Cali, Cape Town, and Rio

Cities Deliver didn’t start from a blank page. It builds on a 2023 convening the foundations organized with more than 40 civic leaders from six continents, followed by early projects in three cities. In Cali, Colombia, Dengue.AI forecasts dengue outbreaks to help public-health officials target prevention — the city’s mayor says it can anticipate outbreaks up to three weeks in advance. In Cape Town, South Africa, an AI-powered messaging platform connects residents with local government. In Rio de Janeiro, a WhatsApp chatbot ties flood alerts and residents’ reports into the city’s emergency response.

Those three pilots get follow-on grants of their own: Rio’s flood-alert system expands to more residents and additional extreme weather events, Dengue.AI’s forecasting stretches from Cali to Cartagena, and Cape Town’s resident-engagement platform grows its reach in the city and beyond.

The rules: no governments, no election projects, no vendor lock-in

Eligibility is deliberately strict. Lead organizations must be nonprofits — including university labs and fiscally sponsored groups — or public benefit corporations; for-profits qualify only in limited cases where the project is charitable and incidental to their business. Governments cannot apply directly, the award goes to organizations rather than individuals, and projects related to voting or elections are explicitly excluded.

And the detail worth noticing: every project must include a meaningful frontier-AI component, but applicants may choose any models and products that fit — the call states no requirement or preference for OpenAI technology. For a program co-launched by the OpenAI Foundation, that neutrality is the tell.

The announcement frames the stakes with a striking figure: citing 2025 UN estimates, it says 45 percent of the world’s 8.2 billion people live in cities — and that city governments face serious barriers to adopting AI, from high implementation costs to uncertainty around data protection.

The timeline

Round 1 proposals — an eligibility screen plus a short video — are due November 29, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Eastern. Applicants learn in December whether they advance to interviews in January 2027; Round 2 proposals are due March 1, 2027, with final selections notified in April 2027. Questions about the call are due October 31, with revised FAQs posted November 4.

Dr. Rajiv J. Shah, president of The Rockefeller Foundation, framed the initiative as taking what works in Cali, Cape Town, and Rio and opening it to institutions everywhere. Anna Makanju, the OpenAI Foundation’s head of civil society and philanthropy, described it as pairing AI’s potential with deep local expertise.

Why it matters

This is the clearest signal yet that AI philanthropy is moving past chatbots and into civic infrastructure. The model — co-design with city governments, AI as a component rather than the product, sustainability baked into the application — is a direct answer to the pilot-graveyard problem that has defined government tech for a decade. If Cities Deliver’s first cohorts land, the template could travel well beyond three cities.

The open question is the same one that haunts every civic-tech grant program: what happens in month 25, when the 24-month clock runs out and the follow-on funding the program explicitly doesn’t guarantee has to come from somewhere else.
