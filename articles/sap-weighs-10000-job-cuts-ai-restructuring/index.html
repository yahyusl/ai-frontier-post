SAP weighs cutting at least 10,000 jobs in AI-first restructuring, Bloomberg reports

SAP is considering cutting at least 10,000 jobs, about 9% of its global workforce, as CEO Christian Klein reshapes the German software giant around AI, Bloomberg reported. The number is not final, and SAP says no decisions have been made.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 27, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: SAP is weighing cuts of at least 10,000 jobs, roughly 9% of its 110,000-plus workforce, as part of a restructuring built around AI, Bloomberg reported on Thursday. The figure is not final and could shift after talks with employee representatives; SAP says no decisions have been made.
  • The plan: SAP is considering eliminating at least 10,000 positions, about 9% of a global workforce of more than 110,000, Bloomberg reported, citing people familiar with the matter.
  • The status: nothing is final. The total could change after negotiations with employee representatives, and SAP declined to comment on whether cuts are planned.
  • The driver: an AI-first restructuring under CEO Christian Klein, who wants AI tools embedded across SAP's product lineup.
  • The scale: SAP is Germany's biggest company by market value, at about €226.67 billion ($248.49 billion).
  • The context: tech has accounted for 28% of US job cuts in 2026, with 171,501 tech layoffs through October — already more than all of 2025.

SAP is weighing the elimination of at least 10,000 jobs as it rebuilds itself around artificial intelligence, Bloomberg reported on Thursday — a cut of roughly 9% of the workforce at Europe's most valuable software company.

The report, citing people familiar with the matter, says the German software giant is discussing a restructuring that would reshape the business for the AI era. Reuters later corroborated the planned reductions, citing two people familiar with the matter. The final number has not been set and could change once SAP negotiates with employee representatives. SAP declined to comment on whether the cuts are planned.

What Bloomberg reported#

The cuts under discussion would hit at least 10,000 roles across SAP's global operations, which employ more than 110,000 people. Product and engineering and sales are the main focus, with every department told to find areas to shrink, according to the reporting. One source told Reuters that SAP's works council had not yet been informed of the plans.

The story surfaced on Reddit in r/ArtificialIntelligence on Friday morning, where the thread pairing the SAP report with Anthropic's API migration drew 80 upvotes and 35 comments within hours — a sign of how quickly the AI-jobs question now travels through the communities that build with these tools.

What SAP said — and didn't#

SAP did not confirm the cuts. A spokesperson told MarketWatch that the business environment "is changing rapidly through AI" and that the company continuously reviews how its organisation and workforce need to evolve, adding: "Beyond this, no decisions have been made regarding any further measures."

That is a careful non-denial, and it fits the shape of the reporting: a plan under discussion, not a decision announced. The number could still move in either direction once labour negotiations begin, and in Germany — where about a quarter of SAP's workforce is based — a works-council employment guarantee runs through 2028, which limits how fast any reduction can land there.

The shake-up is reported to reach the top as well. Sales chief Thomas Saueressig is leaving, and two more executive board members are expected to depart by March, leaving CEO Christian Klein with a substantially different leadership team heading into 2027.

Why now: the AI-first pivot#

Klein has made AI central to SAP's strategy, pushing to embed AI tools and agents across the company's product lineup. The restructuring would redirect resources and investment toward that AI-focused business — a smaller, differently shaped workforce for a company that leans on AI tools rather than headcount.

This is not SAP's first round. An overhaul launched in early 2024 was later estimated to affect between 9,000 and 10,000 positions, and in July 2026 the company froze most hiring and paused non-essential travel to free up money for AI development. The reported new round would be of a similar size — but this time explicitly framed as an AI rebuild rather than a cost exercise.

A brutal year for tech layoffs#

SAP is far from alone. Tech accounts for 28% of all US job cuts in 2026, more than any other sector, according to Challenger, Gray and Christmas figures cited by MarketWatch. Tech companies announced 171,501 layoffs through October — already more than the 154,445 the sector recorded in the whole of 2025 — and that tally does not even include Microsoft's voluntary exit offer to 60,000 workers.

The pattern extends beyond tech and beyond the US. Volkswagen is planning to slash 100,000 jobs and Thyssenkrupp is shedding 4,000 roles, as Germany's blue chips contemplate significant headcount reductions. What distinguishes the SAP case is the framing: the cuts are presented as a consequence of AI reshaping the business, not just a response to a downturn. Neither Bloomberg nor Reuters established that AI automation alone drove the planned reductions — the reports point to a broader restructuring as SAP redirects investment toward AI.

What happens next#

The next milestone is the works council: SAP has not yet presented the plans to employee representatives, and the scope remains subject to change. Watch for a formal announcement, the final number after negotiations, and whether the cuts concentrate in particular divisions or countries. The board changes by March will also signal how far Klein intends to take the reshuffle.

For the enterprise software industry, the episode is a marker. The company that sells AI transformation to the world's biggest businesses is now restructuring its own workforce around the same technology — and 10,000 jobs is the price tag under discussion.

What to watch: SAP's formal statement to employee representatives; the final headcount figure after labour talks; which divisions absorb the cuts; and whether other European software firms follow with AI-linked restructurings of their own.
