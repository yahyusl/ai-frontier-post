Trump launches America.gov: an AI-powered front door for every federal service

The federal government now has one official AI chatbot for everything from passports to Medicare — powered by Google’s Gemini and Elon Musk’s Grok, and backed by an executive order. Here’s what launched, what it changes, and what to watch.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · September 29, 2026 · 5 min read

The U.S. government rolled out its biggest AI deployment yet on Tuesday: a chatbot that promises to be the single front door to every federal service. America.gov greets visitors with a simple invitation — ask anything — and answers questions about passports, benefits, and Medicare with citations to official sources.

President Donald Trump unveiled the site at a daylong event at the Andrew W. Mellon Auditorium in Washington, D.C., and signed an executive order making America.gov the “unified digital front door” for federal information and services. The site runs on two of the most advanced AI models on the market: Google’s Gemini and Elon Musk’s Grok.

What you actually get#

The homepage opens with a search bar and the line “Whatever you need from the government, start here.” Visitors can ask things like how to book a campsite at a national park, how to get a passport for their child, or how to replace a Social Security card — and get a short, plain-language answer with a link to the right form. The bot also redirects people who land on the wrong page. The site is free, carries no advertising, and doesn’t require users to log in.

Joe Gebbia — the Airbnb co-founder appointed U.S. chief design officer in 2025 to lead the National Design Studio — said the model is “trained specifically on one official source of truth: The United States Government,” drawing on government-vetted data from 29,000 websites. He put the daily audience at roughly 40 million Americans visiting a government website each day. The project was built by the National Design Studio together with the U.S. General Services Administration.

The models behind the bot#

Gebbia disclosed the model partners in a CNBC interview shortly before the site went live around 10 a.m. ET: “We have great partners behind the scenes,” he said, naming Google’s Gemini and SpaceXAI’s Grok — the company formerly known as xAI.

Google confirmed its role in a company blog post. Jim Kelly, vice president of federal at Google Public Sector, wrote that Google was proud to be named a technology partner, leveraging Gemini to help “more than 100 million people access critical public resources with greater speed and ease.” SpaceXAI did not respond to CNBC’s request for comment. Notably, the launch materials did not disclose what the government is paying, how long the agreements run, or how the two models divide the work.

The executive order#

The launch came with legal muscle. The executive order “Streamlining Access to Government Services Through America.gov,” signed at the event, establishes the chatbot website “as the unified digital front door to the federal government for every individual in the United States seeking federal information or services.” It directs federal agencies to integrate their public-facing services with America.gov “as soon as possible,” with the eventual goal of making “anything you might need from the United States government” accessible from the platform, Trump said at the event.

For now, the bot answers and points you to the right page. Phase 2, scheduled for 2027, goes further: users would be able to apply, enroll, and track progress directly inside the chat — passport applications, Medicare enrollment and plan selection, name changes after marriage, veterans’ benefits. CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz used the launch to tie the portal to his own agenda of decomplicating Medicare and moving providers toward outcome-based payments.

On the “why it matters” side: this is the largest real-world deployment of generative AI into U.S. government citizen services to date, and it puts the federal government among the very few public agencies running commercial frontier models in a public-facing service. It follows the GSA’s OneGov initiative and the USAi model-access program — America.gov is the most ambitious move yet, and it will set the template for how agencies scale or restrict the technology.

The risks are just as concrete:

  • Hallucinated officialdom. AI-generated answers about benefits, eligibility, and forms carry real consequences when they’re wrong. Citations to official sources help — but a confident wrong answer about Medicare eligibility is worse than a confusing right one.
  • Moderation is already a flashpoint. The Associated Press reported that within moments of the site going live, some answers changed: questions the tool initially answered later received a response saying it did not address political inquiries. Who decides what an official chatbot won’t say — and when — is now a live political question.
  • Privacy questions are pending. Gebbia said the site is not currently tracking conversations, but the planned phase-2 capabilities will inevitably change how the government handles what users type into the box.
  • Undisclosed terms. The price, duration, and division of labor between the Gemini and Grok deals are unknown. Those details will shape procurement norms for every agency that follows.

What to watch#

Five things will tell whether America.gov becomes infrastructure or a demo:

  1. Does the executive order get teeth? “As soon as possible” is a direction, not a deadline. Watch agency-by-agency integration announcements.
  2. Can the bot survive adversarial use? The AP episode suggests content moderation is already a live issue; the first scandal involving a bad benefits answer will be the real stress test.
  3. The money. The terms of the Gemini and Grok deals are still undisclosed. Budget documents and FOIA requests will fill that in.
  4. Phase 2’s privacy bargain. Applying for benefits inside a chat requires the government to handle deeply personal data through commercial models — a design and oversight challenge orders of magnitude harder than answering questions.
  5. The evening meeting. The launch landed hours before Trump gathered AI executives — including Dario Amodei, Greg Brockman, Jensen Huang, and Elon Musk — at the White House to discuss AI oversight. An official AI chatbot and a debate over how to govern AI arriving on the same day is not a coincidence; it’s the whole argument.
