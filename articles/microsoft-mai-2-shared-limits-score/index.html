Microsoft answers OpenAI's shared-limits call: MAI-2 Base scores 0.819 on OpenAI's own test

Mustafa Suleyman backed Jakub Pachocki's proposal for shared perplexity limits — and disclosed that Microsoft's in-house MAI-2 Base scores 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's evaluation set, in the same ballpark as GPT-5.6 Sol.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 23, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Microsoft AI chief Mustafa Suleyman endorsed OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki's November 22 proposal for shared perplexity limits, and disclosed that Microsoft's in-house MAI-2 Base scores 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's evaluation set — run with OpenAI's permission, on the same tokenizer, putting it in the same ballpark as GPT-5.6 Sol. The number is self-reported and narrow, but it is the first public answer to Pachocki's call, and no other frontier lab had followed suit as of Monday.
  • The disclosure: MAI-2 Base at 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's evaluation set; GPT-5.6 Sol's base model sits in the same ballpark, at about 0.83 on OpenAI's own curve.
  • The endorsement: Suleyman posted on X on November 22: “We fully support OpenAI's transparency initiative,” committing to share pre-training progress “on a common held-out evaluation set.”
  • The model: MAI-Base-2, a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 80 billion active parameters; Microsoft says pretraining completed October 15 after 38 days on 16,000 Nvidia GB200 GPUs.
  • The context: Pachocki's essay “Perplexity and Intelligence” (November 22) proposed perplexity as the shared yardstick, with common limits labs would not cross until monitoring catches up. We covered it yesterday.
  • The caveat: one self-reported pretraining metric on OpenAI's internal codebase snapshot — not a task benchmark, and not independently verified.

OpenAI's call for shared AI limits has its first answer — and it comes with a number. Microsoft AI chief Mustafa Suleyman publicly backed chief scientist Jakub Pachocki's proposal over the weekend, then disclosed where Microsoft's own frontier model stands: MAI-2 Base scores 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's own evaluation set, in the same ballpark as the model behind GPT-5.6 Sol. It is the first time a rival lab has put a score on OpenAI's scoreboard — and a quiet declaration that Microsoft can build at the frontier itself.

The speed of the answer is the story. Pachocki published his essay, “Perplexity and Intelligence,” on Sunday, proposing that frontier labs publish their pretraining scores, agree on shared evaluation and red-teaming norms, and commit to common development limits they would not cross until monitoring tools catch up. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman called it “the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.” Within a day, Microsoft had not only endorsed the idea but handed over a number to prove it means it.

The first answer to Pachocki's call#

Suleyman's endorsement came as a quote post on X on Sunday, November 22. “We fully support OpenAI's transparency initiative,” he wrote. “We're committed to sharing our pre-training progress on a common held-out evaluation set. With OpenAI's permission, we report MAI-2 Base on their evaluation set. Its log-perplexity is 0.819, putting it in the same ballpark as the GPT-5.6 Sol base model. Our model uses the same tokenizer as OpenAI, so the numbers are directly comparable.”

That last sentence is doing real work. Perplexity comparisons across labs usually break down because different models chop text into tokens differently; Suleyman stressed that MAI-2 shares OpenAI's tokenizer, which is what makes the 0.819 a like-for-like reading rather than an apples-to-oranges one. He was also candid about the limits of his own number, noting that access to OpenAI's test set is a special case and that future cross-lab comparisons may need bits-per-byte instead of perplexity.

As of Monday, no other frontier lab had followed suit. Anthropic chief Dario Amodei and Google DeepMind chief Demis Hassabis had not publicly mentioned the proposal, let alone posted a number of their own. A shared metric with two participants is a start, not a standard — but it is a start that arrived in under 24 hours.

What 0.819 actually means#

Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token in text it has not seen. Lower is better. Pachocki's argument is that this one number has tracked the arc of capability remarkably well: each drop has lined up with a jump in what models can do, and the jumps were not predictable in advance. OpenAI's published curve runs from GPT-3.5 down to its newest models:

On that yardstick, MAI-2 Base sits between GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol — frontier-adjacent, but behind OpenAI's newest public model, GPT-6.2 Astra, at about 0.76. And the number measures pretraining only. It says nothing about how useful a model is at real tasks, and nothing about the post-trained products people actually use: Copilot runs a tuned, safety-shaped descendant of a base model, not the base model itself. A good pretraining score is a signal, not a verdict.

Why Microsoft is disclosing now#

Microsoft has spent years as OpenAI's biggest backer and, in many investors' eyes, its dependent. A model that scores 0.819 on OpenAI's own yardstick says Microsoft can build at the frontier itself, not just rent it — and that changes the negotiating picture between the two companies, whatever the fine print of their partnership says.

The model behind the number is substantial. MAI-Base-2 is a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 80 billion active parameters, whose pretraining Microsoft says completed on October 15 after 38 days on 16,000 Nvidia GB200 GPUs. Suleyman also told employees internally that MAI-Base-2 outperforms the model behind GPT-5.6 while using about a quarter of the training compute, according to Business Insider — Microsoft's own claim, not independently verified, and worth treating as such. A single number on someone else's test does not settle which lab is ahead.

What it does settle is the direction of travel. Microsoft is now disclosing frontier-model numbers in public, on a shared yardstick, and backing a proposal that would make every lab do the same. If the shared-limits idea catches on, the frontier race gets a public scoreboard — and Microsoft has just put its first number on it.

The caveats, stated plainly#

Three cautions, all worth stating before anyone treats 0.819 as a league table. First, the figure is self-reported by Microsoft, measured on OpenAI's internal codebase snapshot — nobody independent has checked it. Second, perplexity is a pretraining measure, not a task score: reasoning stacks can add large amounts of capability without moving base loss at all. Third, there is still no agreement of any kind. Pachocki proposed common limits; two labs have now published numbers. That is a conversation, not a pact, and the hard part — a numeric limit, a date, a signatory list — has not begun.

What to watch#

Three things. First, whether other labs answer — a shared yardstick needs Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta to publish comparable numbers, and silence from all three so far is the proposal's biggest weakness. Second, whether “common limits” ever take concrete form: a number, a date, a list of signatories. Third, independent evaluation of MAI-Base-2: a third-party benchmark would turn a self-reported pretraining score into something the market — and the field — can weigh. Until then, 0.819 is a first move, not a final answer.

Frequently asked questions

What is perplexity?

A measure of how well a language model predicts the next token in text it has not seen, expressed here as log-perplexity (cross-entropy loss). Lower means better predictions. OpenAI has tracked the number internally since GPT-3.5 and published its measurements for the first time on November 22, 2026.

What exactly did Microsoft disclose?

That its in-house base model, MAI-2 Base, scores 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's evaluation set, run with OpenAI's permission. Because MAI-2 uses the same tokenizer as OpenAI's models, Microsoft says the number is directly comparable with GPT-5.6 Sol, which sits in the same ballpark on OpenAI's curve.

Does 0.819 mean Microsoft has caught up with OpenAI?

No. It is one self-reported pretraining metric on OpenAI's internal test, not a measure of how useful a model is at real tasks, and it says nothing about post-trained products like Copilot. On the same curve, GPT-6.2 Astra sits at about 0.76, ahead of both MAI-2 Base and GPT-5.6 Sol.
