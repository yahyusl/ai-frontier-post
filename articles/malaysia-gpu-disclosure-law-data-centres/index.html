Malaysia plans a data-centre disclosure law to catch AI chip smugglers

Malaysia is drafting a law that would force data-centre operators to disclose how much power they use and how many GPUs they import — a paper trail aimed at high-end chips that arrive in the country and then quietly leave it.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 15, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Investment, Trade and Industry Minister Johari Abdul Ghani said on November 12 that data-centre operators would have to report their electricity consumption, the GPUs they import, the operator’s identity, its hyperscale tenants, and each facility’s location. The backdrop is a building boom — 117 approved projects worth 385.7 billion ringgit, 55 already running — and a pattern the government says it has seen: parties renting Malaysian data-centre space, importing high-end GPUs, and shipping them onward, possibly in breach of US export controls. No draft has been published and no penalties announced, so for now the plan is an announcement, not a law.

  • The power bill: an operator booking 100 megawatts but using 50 would still pay for the unused half. Asia Daily reports the connected-load charge has risen from RM8.50 to RM89.64 per kilowatt.

Malaysia has approved 117 data-centre projects worth 385.7 billion ringgit, and 55 of them are already running. The government now says the boom has a leak: high-end graphics processors that arrive for Malaysian facilities and then leave again, possibly on their way to buyers that US export controls were meant to keep them from.

The fix, announced on November 12 by Investment, Trade and Industry Minister Johari Abdul Ghani, is a disclosure law. Operators would have to tell the government how much electricity their facilities consume, how many GPUs they import, who operates each site, who the hyperscale tenants are, and where each facility sits. The point is a paper trail: when US authorities ask where a given chip ended up, Malaysia wants an answer grounded in records rather than assurances.

What operators would have to disclose#

The disclosure package covers the full life of a facility, not just the approval stage. The minister said monitoring must continue after investment approvals are granted, so oversight would follow a project from construction through operation rather than stopping once the paperwork is done.

GPUs importedHow many accelerators entered the country, and for which facility
Electricity consumptionWhether large-scale computing hardware is actually running on site

Hyperscale tenantsWhose workloads the hardware is serving
Facility locationWhere the hardware physically sits

Writing the requirement into law gives regulators a basis to demand the numbers on a schedule, and a penalty when they are missing or wrong. Neither the draft nor the penalty has been published, so the enforcement teeth are still theoretical.

The pattern it is meant to catch#

The minister described the behaviour plainly: some parties have rented space in Malaysian data centres, imported GPUs, and then shipped them out again. The stated position is simple — chips imported for a Malaysian facility should run in that facility — and the disclosure regime is designed to create a record of exactly that pattern, operator by operator and tenant by tenant.

The context is the US export-control regime. Malaysia imposed controls in 2025 on the movement of US-origin high-performance AI chips, after pressure from Washington over their possible diversion to China. Tracking how many GPUs enter the country and who uses them is the enforcement half of that policy, and the government concedes it cannot be done with customs declarations alone.

One thread of the story is genuinely murky. Accounts differ on the government’s stance toward Speedmatrix, the Malaysian unit of Singapore-based Megaspeed, which has been linked in reporting to alleged re-exports of Nvidia chips: one account says the minister ruled out an investigation, another says a review is under way. The Digital Ministry is separately reviewing whether existing laws can be used against re-exports of sanctioned chips, and the minister acknowledged Malaysia lacks a specific law covering every movement of the hardware.

The power bill is part of the plan#

The proposal is not only about chips. The minister’s most concrete illustration was about electricity: a data centre that books 100 megawatts of capacity but uses 50 still pays for the other 50, because the utility has to reserve it. The government wants operators to state the capacity they actually need, so the grid is not holding space for projects that may not run for another one to three years.

That matters at the scale Malaysia is now operating at. The 117 approved projects carry a combined maximum power demand of about 23,500 megawatts. Asia Daily reports the connected-load charge has risen from RM8.50 to RM89.64 per kilowatt — roughly a tenfold increase — which makes overbooking capacity an expensive habit rather than a cheap option.

What the law cannot do yet#

For all its ambition, the plan has hard limits, and the reporting is candid about them. Disclosure is self-reported: a registry that records how many GPUs an operator says it imported tells the government what the operator believes, not what is in the racks. And reading accelerator counts off a power meter is unreliable — Nvidia’s H100 and H200 in SXM form carry a 700W rating while the B200 SXM is rated at 1,000W, yet neither necessarily holds its rated figure under real workloads.

The other limit is timing. No legislative text has been published, no commencement date announced, and no detailed reporting schedule set. Until the draft appears, it is impossible to judge the two design questions that will decide whether the law works: whether operators must disclose GPU models and serial numbers rather than bare counts, and whether inspectors will attend sites in person.

What to watch#

Three things. First, the draft itself: the disclosure categories, the penalties, and whether serial-level reporting or site inspections make it in. Second, the Digital Ministry’s review of existing law, which will show whether Malaysia can act against re-exports before the new legislation exists. And third, whether the model spreads: Malaysia and Singapore have both stepped up investigations into Nvidia chip re-exports, according to Tom’s Hardware, and a disclosure regime that works in one transit country is the kind of template its neighbours may copy.

Malaysia’s bet is that the cheapest enforcement tool is not a customs officer but a spreadsheet — one that records, facility by facility, what came in, what is running, and who is using it. Whether the spreadsheet survives contact with the operators it is meant to police is the question the draft will have to answer.

Frequently asked questions

What would Malaysia’s data-centre law actually require?

Operators would have to disclose how much electricity their facilities use, how many GPUs they import, who operates each site, who the hyperscale tenants are, and where each facility is located. It is still a proposal: no draft text, penalties, or start date have been published.

Why is Malaysia doing this now?

The government says some parties have rented space in Malaysian data centres, imported high-end GPUs, and shipped them onward — a pattern that could divert US export-controlled chips. Malaysia imposed export controls on US-origin high-performance AI chips in 2025, and its data-centre sector has grown to 117 approved projects.

Would the law prove chips were smuggled?

No. Disclosure is self-reported, so a registry records what operators declare, not what is in the racks. Power data is circumstantial too: nameplate wattage does not reliably map to GPU counts under real workloads. The law would create a paper trail, not proof.
