Cohere targets C$7B Series E at a C$35.7B valuation — Canada’s largest private tech raise ever

Toronto’s Cohere has announced the initial close of a Series E targeting up to C$7 billion at a C$35.7 billion valuation, led by Germany’s Schwarz Group with the Government of Canada among the new investors — a record-setting round for a Canadian tech company.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · October 29, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: Cohere has announced the initial close of a Series E targeting up to C$7 billion at a C$35.7 billion valuation — the largest private raise in Canadian tech history. Led by Germany’s Schwarz Group with the Government of Canada investing, the round bets that governments and regulated industries will pay for AI they can own and control.
  • The round: Series E, initial close announced October 29, 2026, targeting up to C$7 billion at a C$35.7 billion valuation.
  • Lead investor: the companies of Schwarz Group; new investors include Bell Ventures, Bullhound Capital, and the Government of Canada; NVIDIA, Inovia Capital, and Prosperity7 Ventures return.
  • The pitch: sovereign AI for regulated industries and governments — control over data, infrastructure, and deployment, including on-premises and air-gapped setups.
  • The growth claim: 165% year-over-year ARR growth at end of Q3 2026, and a customer base up 20x over the past year to 300 customers.
  • The open question: the full C$7 billion is a target, not a closed total, and the government stake’s terms were not disclosed.

Toronto-based Cohere has announced the initial close of a Series E funding round targeting up to C$7 billion, which the company says would value it at C$35.7 billion. If the round closes at its target, it would be the largest private financing ever raised by a Canadian technology company — and a striking vote of confidence in the idea of sovereign AI, the pitch that governments and regulated industries should own and control the systems they run on.

The distinction in the announcement matters. This is an initial close of a round targeting up to C$7 billion, not C$7 billion already in the bank. What is confirmed is the investor lineup, the valuation the full target would imply, and the direction of travel: a Canadian lab founded in 2019, once best known for its language models, now positioning itself as the independent alternative for enterprises and governments that do not want their AI strategy to depend entirely on a handful of US hyperscalers.

The round, and who is backing it#

The lead investor is the companies of Schwarz Group, the German retail conglomerate whose IT and digital arm, Schwarz Digits, has been pouring billions into data-centre capacity in Germany. New investors include Bell Ventures, Bullhound Capital, and the Government of Canada. Returning backers include Inovia Capital, NVIDIA, and Prosperity7 Ventures.

That mix is the story in miniature: a German industrial group leading, Ottawa investing directly, and a chip giant returning — less a conventional venture round than a coalition forming around AI infrastructure no single country or company fully controls. Schwarz Digits chief executive Christian Müller framed the investment as the next step after Cohere’s combination with Germany’s Aleph Alpha, with the goal of anchoring frontier capabilities in Schwarz’s STACKIT cloud.

Cohere chief executive Aidan Gomez called the raise “the largest private capital raise in Canadian history,” and pointed to the federal government’s participation as a sign of Canada’s standing in the global AI race. Prime Minister Mark Carney said the government’s equity investment would help Cohere scale from Canada, supporting jobs and computing infrastructure while giving Canadians a stake in the company’s success. Germany’s digital minister, Karsten Wildberger, also welcomed the round, citing German-Canadian cooperation on sovereign technology.

What the money is for#

According to chief financial officer Francois Chadwick, the funding will advance Cohere’s modelling and product roadmap, build fully verticalized AI products for regulated industries, and pay for the long-term compute infrastructure needed to train frontier-level enterprise models. The company also plans to keep scaling across North America, Europe, the Middle East, and Asia-Pacific.

The growth numbers published alongside the round help explain the appetite. Since launching North, its agentic AI platform, Cohere says it ended Q3 2026 with annual recurring revenue up 165% year over year, and that its customer base grew twentyfold over the past year to 300 customers, including RBC, TD Bank, Bell Canada, Fujitsu, PwC, and TKMS.

2026 has also brought expansion by acquisition: deals for Aleph Alpha, Reliant AI, and AISLE pushed Cohere deeper into sovereign AI, healthcare and life sciences, and cybersecurity, while North received its largest upgrade yet with North 2 and new models across generation, search, speech, code, and translation.

Why sovereign AI is having a moment#

Sovereign AI is, at its core, a procurement argument as much as a technical one: organizations want to decide where sensitive data is stored, how it is used, and who can access it — and to run models in private clouds, on-premises, or fully air-gapped environments when the stakes demand it. Rising compute costs, tightening regulation, and escalating cybersecurity risk have all pushed in the same direction, and Cohere cites a McKinsey projection putting the sovereign AI market at $600 billion.

That pitch lands differently depending on the buyer. For a bank or a ministry, control and compliance may matter more than raw benchmark scores; for a startup, price and capability usually win. Cohere’s bet is that the first group is large and growing fast enough to support a frontier-scale company — and that being the independent option is itself the selling point.

The counterweight is concentration: the biggest players command vastly larger compute budgets, and C$7 billion, while historic for Canada, is modest next to what hyperscalers spend in a quarter. Cohere’s answer is focus — security, deployment flexibility, and cost-efficient models — rather than a head-on race for the largest general-purpose model.

What to watch#

Three things will decide whether this round is a turning point or a high-water mark. First, the final close: how much of the C$7 billion target materializes, and on what terms, including the government stake’s size and conditions, which the announcement did not detail. Second, the compute: whether the money becomes owned infrastructure that lowers long-run costs, or mostly rents capacity from others. Third, the customers: whether twentyfold account growth becomes durable deployments inside regulated industries, rather than pilots that stall at procurement.

For Canada, the symbolism is already significant: a Toronto lab once dismissible as a research spinout is now raising at a scale no Canadian tech company has attempted privately before — with its own government as a shareholder. The harder test comes next: turning a record round into a company that can stand, on its own infrastructure, beside the giants it pitches itself against.
