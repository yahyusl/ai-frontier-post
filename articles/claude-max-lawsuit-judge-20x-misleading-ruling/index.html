Federal judge rules Anthropic must face Claude Max subscribers over 20x usage marketing

A federal judge in California has let a proposed class action against Anthropic go ahead, finding that the company's '5x or 20x more usage than Pro' pitch for its Claude Max plans is 'plausibly misleading' — and that the small print saying the multipliers apply per session may not save it.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 7, 2026 · 8 min read
TL;DR: A federal judge has let a proposed class action against Anthropic proceed, finding its '5x or 20x more usage than Pro' pitch for Claude Max plans is 'plausibly misleading.' The case turns on two different usage caps — per session and per week — and on whether a disclosure buried two clicks from the sales page counts. Nothing is proven yet: no class has been certified, and the court treated the plaintiffs' allegations as true only for the purpose of the motion.
  • The case: Kahn v. Anthropic PBC, No. 3:26-cv-05763-AGT (N.D. Cal.); order on the motion to dismiss issued November 6, 2026.
  • The claim: Max 20x delivers 6–8x Pro's weekly usage, not 20x; Max 5x about 3.5x, not 5x, according to the complaint.
  • The judge's line: the per-session disclosure sits 'two hyperlinks away from the primary sales pages' — like the back label on a shampoo bottle.
  • What survived: Smith's California consumer-protection claim, negligent misrepresentation, and breach of contract. Dismissed with leave to amend: Kahn's consumer claim, unfair-competition and false-advertising claims, and a good-faith claim.
  • What's next: an amended complaint is due by December 2, or Anthropic must answer the current one by December 16. Anthropic has not commented.

A federal judge in California has refused to throw out a proposed class action against Anthropic over how it sold its Claude Max subscriptions, ruling that the headline promise of '5x or 20x more usage than Pro' is 'plausibly misleading.' In an order issued on Friday, US Magistrate Judge Alex G. Tse of the Northern District of California let the case brought by two subscribers go ahead — putting the fine print behind AI subscription plans before a court for the first time.

The dispute is over what 'more usage' means. Anthropic sells three individual Claude tiers: Pro at $17 to $20 a month, Max 5x at $100, and Max 20x at $200. The plaintiffs, Karl Kahn and Solomon Smith, say the names promise five or twenty times Pro's usage — but their complaint, filed in June, calculates that for Claude's Sonnet 4 model, Max 5x delivers about 3.5 times Pro's weekly usage and Max 20x about six to eight times. Anthropic says the multipliers always applied to each five-hour session, not the week. The judge was not persuaded — at least not yet.

Per session, not per month#

Paid Claude plans carry two separate limits: a cap on each five-hour session and a cap on the week. According to the complaint, Max subscribers do get 5 or 20 times Pro's allowance inside a single session — but their weekly allowances are not 5 or 20 times higher. That gap is the whole case. The plaintiffs calculate that burning through Max 20x in one full five-hour window could eat 'a whopping 15%' of a subscriber's weekly allotment, and that Smith had to ration or postpone his usage after upgrading to Max 5x. Both are allegations, not findings.

Anthropic's numbers differ. When it renamed the plans on October 30 — Max 5x became Max, Max 20x became Ultra — the company said Max 20x's weekly limit had always been 10 times Pro's; only the five-hour limit was 20 times. The complaint's 6-to-8-times figures are the plaintiffs' own calculations for Sonnet 4. Which number a court accepts is now part of the case.

The shampoo-bottle disclosure#

Anthropic argued that customers should understand 'more usage' to mean more per session, because a help page says Max 5x 'provides 5 times more usage per session than the Pro plan.' Tse was unmoved: subscribers pay monthly, he wrote, so reasonable consumers could take the promise to mean 5x or 20x more usage per month. And the help page sits 'two hyperlinks away from the primary sales pages on claude.com' — a disclosure he likened to the back label on a shampoo bottle, which shoppers are not required to read.

That disclosure 'may persuade the fact-finder' later that the advertising is not misleading, the order says, 'but it is not a basis for dismissal.' Nor did an asterisk reading 'Usage limits apply' help Anthropic, because it 'says nothing about usage limits per session.'

What survived — and what didn't#

It is a mixed result. Going ahead: Smith's claim under California's Consumers Legal Remedies Act, negligent misrepresentation, and breach of contract. Dismissed, with a chance to refile: Kahn's consumer-protection claim, claims under California's Unfair Competition Law and False Advertising Law, and a claim that Anthropic broke an implied duty of good faith by changing limits during billing periods.

The split turns on who the plaintiffs are and what they want. The consumer-protection law covers personal purchases, not business ones, and the judge found Claude Code is plausibly a 'service' under it. Smith, a computer science student who used Claude for coursework and an IBM hackathon, counts as a consumer: 'Educational use is plainly not commercial use.' Kahn said only that he used Claude Code 'to accomplish computer programming tasks' — and, the order notes, 'he could be an entrepreneur who uses Claude Code to develop software for sale.' The unfair-competition and false-advertising claims fell because both men are still Max subscribers, so they cannot ask a court to change the marketing, and they did not explain why damages would not be enough. The contract claim survived because Anthropic's terms leave plan details to 'the order process,' which plausibly includes the sales pages promising 5x or 20x. And the good-faith claim failed because the same terms let Anthropic 'increase or decrease capacity limits' — as Tse put it, 'Anthropic didn't tighten usage limits as a bait-and-switch.'

The subscribers' verdict, so far#

The ruling landed on r/ClaudeAI in the early hours of Saturday, in a thread that quickly gathered about 190 upvotes. The top comments were a chain of subscribers saying the pitch had misled them, one after another. One commenter pushed back on the numbers, arguing the true values 'are and always have been 5x and 10x' — which is, in substance, Anthropic's position: 5x and 10x per week, with 20x applying only to the five-hour window. The complaint's measurements say otherwise. That disagreement is now a question for the court.

What happens next#

If the plaintiffs do not file an amended complaint by December 2, Anthropic must answer the current one by December 16. Anthropic has not commented. The claims are brought under California law, and the order does not say whether any class would reach subscribers outside the United States. No class has been certified and nothing has been proven: at this stage the court treats the complaint's allegations as true.

The timing adds a wrinkle: on October 30, Anthropic renamed the plans at the center of the case — Max 5x is now Max, Max 20x is now Ultra — and dropped the five-hour limit for verified Ultra subscribers. The weekly cap at the heart of the case remains, and the rename does not settle what the old names promised.

What to watch: whether the plaintiffs refile the dismissed claims by December 2, whether the case moves toward class certification, and whether other AI labs' subscription pages — which lean just as hard on multiplier-style promises — draw the same scrutiny. For now, the message is narrow but pointed: a headline number on a pricing page is a promise a reasonable subscriber can take at face value, and the explanation two clicks away may not be enough.

Frequently asked questions

Does this ruling mean Anthropic misled its subscribers?

No. The judge decided only that the claim is plausible enough to go forward. No class has been certified, nothing has been proven, and at this stage the court takes the plaintiffs' allegations as true. Anthropic's argument — that the multipliers were always per session — gets its hearing later.

What is the difference between the per-session limit and the weekly limit?

Claude plans cap usage twice: once per five-hour session and once per week. The plaintiffs say the 5x and 20x multipliers describe the session cap, while the weekly cap is much lower — about 3.5x for Max 5x and 6 to 8x for Max 20x, by their calculation. Anthropic says the weekly cap for Max 20x was 10x Pro's.

I subscribe to Max or Ultra. Does this case affect me?

Not directly, and not yet. There is no settlement, no certified class, and no claim form. The case is about how the plans were marketed, not about anyone's current service. The next dates to watch are December 2, when an amended complaint is due, and December 16, when Anthropic must answer.
