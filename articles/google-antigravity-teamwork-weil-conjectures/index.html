Google's Antigravity agent teams formalize the Weil conjectures and prove a 2013 open problem

Google's /teamwork multi-agent system, running on Gemini 4, formalized the Weil conjectures in 1.8 million lines of Lean, helped settle a 2013 information-theory conjecture, and rewrote a video decoder in Rust — faster than the original. Not all of it can be independently checked yet.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 14, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: On November 13, Google Research published results from Antigravity’s /teamwork, a system that coordinates teams of Gemini 4 agents against open problems in mathematics, chemistry and software engineering. The headline results: the 2013 Courtade–Kumar conjecture proved in full generality and formalized in 50 million lines of Lean; the Weil conjectures formalized autonomously in 1.8 million lines across 28,800 subagent invocations; and a Rust rewrite of Google’s AV1 decoder that runs 20–30% faster than the original. Google is opening the tools in Antigravity, with 10,000 academics offered six months of free access. One caveat: the Weil code hasn’t been published, so the headline number can’t be independently verified yet.
  • What: /teamwork, a multi-agent feature in Google’s Antigravity coding environment, running on Gemini 4 models (Argon, Carbon and Hydrogen).
  • Math: the Courtade–Kumar conjecture (2013) proved in full generality with researchers from Google, CUHK, USC and CMU, then formalized in Lean 4 as 50 million lines of code.
  • Weil: /teamwork-LEAP fully autonomously produced a machine-checked formalization of the Weil conjectures — about 1.8 million lines of Lean across 28,800 subagent invocations.
  • Beyond math: 95% top-1 and 100% top-3 molecule identification from NMR data; libgav1 rewritten from C++ into ~170,000 lines of Rust, 20–30% faster than the original.
  • Access: /teamwork and /boost are available in Antigravity now; 10,000 academic researchers are being offered six months of free access to the highest usage tier.

Google’s agent teams just handed in a remarkable report card. On Friday, Google Research published results from Antigravity’s /teamwork, a system that coordinates teams of specialist Gemini 4 agents against long-open problems: a 2013 conjecture in information theory, landmark theorems in pure mathematics, molecular structures in chemistry, and a video decoder rewritten in a safer language.

Taken at face value, it is the strongest evidence yet that multi-agent systems can do sustained scientific work. The caveat: the most eye-catching numbers come from Google’s own account, and the code behind the biggest one hasn’t been published.

A conjecture from 2013, settled and checked by machine#

The headline result is the Courtade–Kumar conjecture, posed in 2013. It asks: when a random string of bits passes through a noisy channel, can you squeeze it down to a single bit while keeping more information than the laziest possible method — just copying one input bit? The conjecture says no.

Researchers from Google, CUHK, USC and Carnegie Mellon proved it in full generality with /teamwork’s Long Proof pattern, which splits a long argument across separate agents. The proof itself appeared as a preprint in September; what the November post adds is the machine-checked formalization — the whole argument translated into Lean 4, a language that lets a computer kernel verify every logical step. Google puts that formalization at 50 million lines of code.

That number deserves a closer look. An independent check of the public repository found the 50 million lines are real — but only about 239,000 are proof a person could read. The rest — some 44,000 files — is generated certificate data the Lean kernel checks mechanically. The line count measures how hard the checker was worked, not how much mathematics was done. The audit trail is nevertheless strong: the repository records a clean rebuild with a byte-identical final artifact.

The Weil conjectures, formalized without a human in the loop#

The more startling claim comes next. Google says a framework called /teamwork-LEAP, built with Google Cloud AI and paired with Gemini 4 Argon, “fully autonomously produced an end-to-end, machine-checked formalization of the Weil conjectures” — the landmark results on counting solutions of polynomial equations over finite fields, proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974. It spans about 1.8 million lines of Lean, produced across 28,800 subagent invocations.

Formalizing a theorem means turning its proof into code a proof checker verifies line by line; once mathematicians confirm the formal statement matches the original problem, the proof only compiles if every step holds. The same system, Google says, also produced the first formalization of a recent proof of Hilbert’s tenth problem over general rings of integers — more than 919,000 lines of Lean — plus first formalizations of the Birkhoff conjecture (open since 1927) and the 3D Bochner–Riesz conjecture (open 50+ years).

Here the verification story is thinner: Google’s post links to Wikipedia pages, with no repository, no paper and no statement file. A week earlier, Axiom published its own Weil formalization under an open license — about 213,000 lines, produced in less than a day by its own account. Until Google publishes its code, nobody outside the company can say what theorem it states, which axioms it uses, or whether it rebuilds.

The same machinery, pointed at chemistry and code#

The math is only part of the report. In chemistry, Google organized 30 specialized agents around a physics-informed spectral simulator to determine molecular structures from nuclear magnetic resonance data — where a single misplaced bond can separate a working drug from an inactive look-alike. It identified the exact structure 95% of the time on complex benchmark molecules, with the right answer in its top three every time — nearly double a single agent.

In software, the same approach rewrote libgav1, Google’s AV1 video decoder, from C++ into around 170,000 lines of Rust, with exact bit-for-bit parity. The first port ran about four times slower than the original. Given profiling tools and a mandate to maximize speed, the agents closed the gap: the Rust version now runs 20–30% faster than the original C++ and hand-optimized assembly.

The design idea is the same throughout: pair parallel attempts with dedicated “falsifiers” — the Lean proof checker, unmodified test suites, simulators, hardware counters — that decide which candidates actually work. Each agent gets only the files and tools its step needs, and critics audit results from a clean context, without seeing the original producer’s reasoning.

What to watch#

Google is making /teamwork and /boost available in Antigravity now, and has opened applications giving 10,000 academic researchers six months of free access to its highest usage tier. More proof patterns and specialized workflows are promised in the coming weeks.

Three things to watch: whether Google publishes the Weil formalization — the difference between a claim and a checkable result; whether the field’s scoreboard moves from line counts to verified artifacts; and whether the review side spreads: ahead of STOC 2027, authors of 578 submissions opted in to Deep Review feedback, and 90% of surveyed authors rated it mostly or almost all factual, with over 30% saying it caught substantive proof bugs.

The through-line is not that agents can now do mathematics. It is that a system built around verification — checkers that cannot be charmed, critics that never saw the first draft — can keep a thousand parallel attempts honest. The next year will show whether that holds outside Google’s own lab.

Frequently asked questions

What is Antigravity’s /teamwork?

A multi-agent feature inside Google’s Antigravity coding environment. It coordinates teams of specialist agents — some propose solutions, others challenge them — while separate “falsifier” systems (the Lean proof checker, unmodified test suites, simulators, hardware counters) decide which candidates actually work. It runs on Gemini 4 models and is available in Antigravity today.

Did AI agents prove the Weil conjectures?

No. Pierre Deligne proved them in 1974. What the agents did, according to Google, was produce a machine-checked formalization: the proof translated into Lean code that a computer kernel verifies step by step. Google has not published that code, so the claim can’t be independently checked yet.

Can I try /teamwork myself?

Yes, in Antigravity, where /teamwork and the /boost coding pattern are available now. Google has also opened applications giving 10,000 academic researchers six months of free access to its highest usage tier.
