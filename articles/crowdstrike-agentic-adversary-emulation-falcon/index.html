CrowdStrike Agentic Adversary Emulation: an AI red team that attacks, hardens, then re-attacks to prove the fix

CrowdStrike unveiled Agentic Adversary Emulation on November 4, 2026, at Fal.Con Europe: an AI red team, powered by SafeMind, that attacks a customer's Falcon environment like an agentic adversary, chains weaknesses into paths to critical assets, and then re-runs the attack to prove each fix worked.

CrowdStrike unveiled Agentic Adversary Emulation on November 4, 2026, at Fal.Con Europe 2026 in Barcelona. Powered by SafeMind, CrowdStrike's family of purpose-built security models and harnesses, the new offering red-teams a customer's environment the way an agentic adversary would: chaining vulnerabilities and weaknesses into exploitable paths to that organization's critical assets, then driving prioritized remediations and re-running the attack to validate that each path is closed.

The pitch is that the adversary has already gone agentic, so point-in-time penetration tests and periodic assessments can no longer keep pace. CrowdStrike's 2026 Agentic AI Campaign report documented an agent chaining a single vulnerability into cluster-admin access and exfiltrating 726 databases, with no human at the keyboard. Adam Meyers, CrowdStrike's head of counter adversary operations, put it bluntly: “The agentic adversary uses AI to operate at a speed and scale no human can match. These are preventable gaps that go unprevented. Now defenders can find and close them before the adversary does. That is offense in the service of defense.”

Red Tempest, the offensive model

At the centre is Red Tempest, SafeMind's offensive model, which operates like an autonomous attacker. Its harness orchestrates thousands of steps to chain weaknesses, adapt around defenses, and pursue objectives — all inside controlled, customer-approved testing environments, not unrestricted attacks on production infrastructure. SafeMind is trained on Falcon sensor telemetry, expert-labeled threat intelligence, and frontline experience from CrowdStrike's analysts and incident responders.

The emulation is adversary-informed rather than generic. CrowdStrike says its intelligence covers more than 290 tracked adversaries, plus emerging agentic threats and newly disclosed vulnerabilities, grounding the simulated attacks in how real-world adversaries operate. Falcon environmental context then combines vulnerabilities, misconfigurations, and identity gaps into exploitable paths to critical assets — pinpointing, CrowdStrike says, where a single control can break the whole attack chain. That changes the prioritization question: instead of sorting thousands of findings by severity score, teams fix the weaknesses that actually connect into a working attack path.

Attack, harden, validate — on one platform

The loop is the product, not just the finding. CrowdStrike generates compensating controls to close each attack path — patches, configuration fixes, and new detections — and Red Tempest re-runs the attack to validate the fix. The company frames this as unifying purpose-built offensive and defensive AI, real-world adversary intelligence, and Falcon environmental context on one platform, from sensor to harness to model. Finding a path, in its argument, is not the same as closing it, and an offensive tool that cannot help defend the environment it just tested leaves the hardest work with the security team.

The launch also sits next to a parallel story in agent control: Leidos HeadWay Dispatch, unveiled the same day, governs agents from different vendors by letting operators block, approve, or auto-run their actions. CrowdStrike is attacking the problem from the other side — assuming agents, friendly or hostile, will act, and testing whether the environment survives them. It follows CrowdStrike's recent Falcon AI work, including the Falcon Guardian AI kill switch covered here on November 3 and its tracking of AI-driven intrusions such as VAPOR PANDA.

What is not ready yet

Agentic Adversary Emulation is available in early access for select customers through Project QuiltWorks partners — not as a general release. The announcement gives no general availability date and no public pricing, and CrowdStrike's own forward-looking statement cautions that unreleased services or features are still in development and subject to change, and that purchase decisions should be based on features currently available. Those are the right caveats for an offensive AI product: the value claim — that it finds real, closable attack paths rather than more findings — will only be proven when customers report what it found, what it fixed, and what the re-run confirmed.

For now, the announcement matters because it marks a shift in what red-teaming means. The test is no longer a scheduled exercise producing a report; it is a continuous, AI-driven loop of attack, hardening, and validation. If that loop works as described, the security question stops being “how many vulnerabilities do we have?” and becomes “which of them can actually be chained into a breach — and did our fix break the chain?”
