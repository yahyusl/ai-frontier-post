Canada commits C$162M to 10,000 AI work placements through Mitacs

Ottawa will spend up to C$162 million over five years to embed 10,000 students, recent graduates and post-docs inside Canadian businesses as AI help — the biggest talent bet yet under its AI for All strategy.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Canada is putting serious money behind a familiar diagnosis: its businesses know AI matters, they just don't have the people to put it to work. On September 25, AI and Digital Innovation Minister Evan Solomon announced that Ottawa will commit up to C$162 million over five years to fund 10,000 paid AI-related work placements through Mitacs, the nonprofit that brokers research partnerships between universities and industry.

The placements will send post-secondary students, recent graduates and postdoctoral fellows into Canadian companies — not to do busywork, but to find where AI could help, test it, and get it deployed. It's the most ambitious talent-pipeline move yet under Canada's National Artificial Intelligence Strategy, "AI for All," which aims to create 90,000 AI-related job and placement opportunities for young Canadians by 2031.

What the funding buys#

The money starts flowing in the 2026–2027 fiscal year and runs for five years. Crucially, the placements are co-funded — Mitacs' long-running model splits the cost with host organizations — which stretches the $162 million further than a straight grant program and keeps employers with skin in the game. In practice, that means companies get subsidized access to AI-capable talent, and young Canadians get paid, practical experience instead of another unpaid internship.

The participants are university and college students, recent graduates, and postdoctoral fellows, matched with Canadian businesses of all sizes. Their mandate is practical: help firms identify promising AI applications, pilot them, and convert early experiments into real productivity gains — then stay around long enough to make the changes stick.

ADOPT and AI+X: two tracks#

The placements run through a new Mitacs AI Advantage program with two complementary streams:

  • ADOPT — placements focused on adoption: pairing businesses with academic researchers and emerging talent to quickly test and deploy AI solutions across their operations.
  • AI+X — placements focused on innovation: co-funded research placements that apply AI to solve business problems and develop new technology across disciplines, including agriculture and healthcare.

One track is about getting today's AI into today's companies; the other is about building new AI applications with them. Mitacs CEO Stephen Lucas framed it as embedding young, highly skilled Canadians directly into businesses so SMEs can adopt AI with confidence.

The delivery vehicle isn't new, which is part of the point. Mitacs already partners with more than 200 post-secondary institutions, 170 innovation incubators and 12,500 companies across Canada, and says it has supported more than 4,200 AI-related innovation projects since 2020 — from language learning to surgical patient monitoring to intelligent radios. Ottawa is scaling an existing machine rather than building one from scratch.

Why Ottawa is doing this now#

Most national AI strategies obsess over compute and frontier models. Canada's is increasingly aimed at the other end of the problem: the small and mid-sized businesses that employ most Canadians and adopt AI slowly. The government's argument is that for many SMEs, the barrier isn't seeing AI's potential — it's lacking the skills, capacity and support to act on it. Rather than train consultants, Ottawa is embedding the talent directly inside firms.

The announcement also fills in a deliberate sequence. Canada launched its national AI strategy "AI for All" in June 2026; on September 9 it rolled out a National AI Literacy Initiative with Amii to help Canadians understand and use AI responsibly. Funding 10,000 placements is the natural next step: literacy tells people what AI is, placements put someone who knows AI in the room.

And there's a talent-retention angle nobody stated aloud. Young Canadian AI graduates are heavily recruited by US labs and companies; the pull of frontier-lab salaries across the border is a constant drain. Ten thousand paid placements inside Canadian companies is also ten thousand reasons to stay.

What to watch#

Placement counts are easy to hit; employment outcomes are not. The announcement sets targets for placements, not for what happens after — how many participants land AI jobs, and whether participating businesses keep using AI once the intern leaves. Mitacs will need to publish those numbers, not just the headcount.

Co-funding is a double-edged sword. It makes the program cheaper for Ottawa, but even a split cost can be a barrier for the smallest businesses — exactly the firms the program is meant to reach. Watch whether the placements skew toward larger companies that can afford their share.

And keep the five-year horizon in view. "AI for All" targets 90,000 opportunities by 2031; this program covers 10,000. The rest of the roadmap — and its funding — is still to come. For now, though, this is one of the largest single bets any government has made on AI work placements, and a signal that the AI talent race is increasingly being fought not over researchers, but over the people who can put the technology to work inside ordinary companies.
