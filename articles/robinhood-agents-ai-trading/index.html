Robinhood rolls out in-app AI trading agents to its 29 million customers

Robinhood Agents, unveiled at the company's HOOD Summit on September 29, lets anyone build an AI agent inside the app that researches markets and places trades. Autonomous trading just went mainstream — with approval required by default, model choices from OpenAI and Anthropic, and the same week economists started asking what happens when everyone's money moves itself.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · September 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Robinhood spent a decade teaching a generation to trade stocks from their phones. Now it wants to remove the trader from the loop entirely. At its HOOD Summit in Houston on Tuesday, the brokerage unveiled Robinhood Agents — AI agents built directly into the app that research markets, devise strategies, and place trades on a customer's behalf. Starting this week, roughly 29 million customers will be able to hand their portfolios to software.

This is not another chatbot that explains what a P/E ratio is. An agent takes action: buying and selling within limits the customer sets, running while they sleep. It is the first time autonomous trading has been packaged for a truly mass audience — and it landed the same week economists started warning about what happens when everyone's money starts moving itself.

What Robinhood announced#

Robinhood Agents lives inside the main Robinhood app. Customers name their agent, open a separate dedicated trading account for it, and pick an AI model from participating labs, including OpenAI. OpenAI's GPT-Luna model will be free to use through the end of the year. According to a Fortune demonstration of the product, the model menu also includes GPT-6 Sol and Anthropic's Opus 4.8.

The setup is deliberately pitched as no-code: Robinhood's announcement says anyone can "create an agent in just a few taps." Once configured, users give plain-English instructions. In the Fortune demo, those ranged from a simple "Buy $200 of Ford stock" to Loops — recurring routines such as checking the market every morning and trading when certain conditions are met, or running a continuous overnight strategy to hunt for opportunities while the user sleeps.

How the guardrails work#

The riskiest feature comes with the most conservative default. Trade confirmation is switched on by default: the agent must ask before executing. Users can turn that off for full automation, but the out-of-the-box setting keeps a human in the loop on every trade.

Other guardrails, as reported from the summit:

  • Dedicated accounts. Each agent gets its own separate trading account, ring-fenced from the rest of the customer's money.
  • No margin at launch. Agents cannot borrow on margin, capping the leverage they can deploy.
  • Customer-set limits. Users decide how much the agent may trade at a time.

That design matters, because the May pilot that preceded this launch showed how fast agentic trading scales. Since Robinhood let tech-savvy users connect their own agents in May, more than 150,000 customers have opened agentic trading accounts, and those agents now tap Robinhood's tools almost 30 million times a day. The new in-app version removes every remaining technical barrier to entry.

Why this matters#

Autonomous trading has been the private domain of hedge funds and quant firms for years. Robinhood just made it a consumer feature with a tap-to-create button. That is a genuine structural shift: when millions of agents can move money around the clock — and Robinhood also announced 24/7 weekend stock trading at the same summit — market structure, liquidity patterns, and the very idea of "market hours" start to look different.

The reaction on social media was immediate. An r/accelerate thread summed up the stakes in its title: "Robinhood just rolled out trading agents to millions — and investing may never be the same." The community's attention split between excitement at the capability and unease at the speed: 150,000 power users were already running agents; now the other 28.8 million get one-click access.

The risks#

The uncomfortable questions arrived on the same news cycle. Just two days earlier, Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok warned that if households let AI agents automatically shift their cash to the highest-paying accounts, banks could lose the cheap deposits they rely on for lending — an "agentic bank run" scenario we covered on September 28. Trading agents don't move deposits, but they normalize the same habit: delegating financial decisions to software that acts while you sleep.

There is also the leverage question. Alongside the agents, Robinhood announced crypto perpetual futures with up to 10x leverage on bitcoin and ether for eligible US customers, plus weekend equity sessions routed through Bruce ATS with a curated rollout planned for early 2027, subject to regulatory review. Autonomous agents plus leveraged products plus round-the-clock markets is a combination regulators will be watching closely — especially the question of what happens when approval toggles get switched off and agents trade through a flash event unattended.

What to watch#

Three things will decide whether this is a novelty or a turning point. First, pricing: GPT-Luna is free through year-end, but the economics of running frontier models on millions of trading loops after that are unproven. Second, incidents: the first unattended-agent trading disaster will do more to shape the rules than any launch-day guardrail list. Third, the competitive response: Schwab, Fidelity, and the rest of the brokerage industry now have a public benchmark — expect fast followers within a quarter or two.

Robinhood's shares closed Tuesday at $116.22 after the summit announcements. The market's verdict, for now, is enthusiasm. The real test comes the first time an agent does something its owner didn't expect — at 3 a.m., with approval turned off.
