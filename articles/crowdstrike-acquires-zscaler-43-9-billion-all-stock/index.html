CrowdStrike buys Zscaler for $43.9 billion in stock — the biggest cybersecurity deal ever, pitched as AI-era security

CrowdStrike will buy Zscaler in a $43.9 billion all-stock deal, uniting endpoint protection with zero-trust networking in a bet that securing AI agents is the next decade of cybersecurity.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 23, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: CrowdStrike announced on Monday that it will acquire Zscaler in an all-stock deal worth about $43.9 billion — the largest cybersecurity acquisition on record. Zscaler shareholders get 1.135 CrowdStrike shares per share, a premium of roughly 34%. The companies frame the combination as a single platform for securing AI agents; investors, for now, are less convinced, sending CrowdStrike shares down more than 8% in premarket trading while Zscaler rose more than 17%.
  • The price: about $43.9 billion, all stock — 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each Zscaler share, worth $260.41 at Friday's closing prices, a premium of roughly 34%.
  • The scale: the largest cybersecurity acquisition on record, per Reuters. The combined company starts with more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue, including roughly $3.9 billion from Zscaler.
  • The leadership: CrowdStrike CEO George Kurtz leads the combined company; Zscaler founder and CEO Jay Chaudhry will run the Zscaler business and join CrowdStrike's board.
  • The market reaction: CrowdStrike shares fell more than 8% in premarket trading; Zscaler shares rose more than 17%.
  • The timeline: expected to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028, subject to regulatory approval. The agreement carries a $1.4 billion breakup fee if Zscaler walks away under certain circumstances.

CrowdStrike said on Monday it has agreed to buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal worth about $43.9 billion, the biggest acquisition in cybersecurity history. The combination would join the two best-known names in their corners of the market — CrowdStrike's endpoint protection and Zscaler's zero-trust networking — under one roof, with the stated goal of securing the AI agents enterprises are now letting loose inside their systems.

Under the terms, Zscaler shareholders receive 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each share they own. At Friday's closing prices that values Zscaler at $260.41 a share, a premium of roughly 34% over its $194.82 close. CrowdStrike's existing shareholders would own about 84.1% of the combined company; Zscaler's would hold the remaining 15.9%. Both boards approved the deal unanimously, and the companies expect it to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028, subject to regulatory approval.

The pitch: security for the agent era#

The two companies are selling the deal as an answer to a shift in what security has to protect. CrowdStrike guards the devices on a corporate network — laptops, phones, servers. Zscaler, based in San Jose and serving about 8,000 enterprise customers, guards how those devices reach cloud applications, checking identity on every access rather than trusting anything inside the perimeter. Put together, the argument goes, they cover the full path an AI agent travels: from the endpoint where it runs to the data it touches across the network.

CrowdStrike chief executive George Kurtz put the pitch bluntly: “Security used to be about keeping the adversary out. Today, safety is required to let AI in. The network used to connect people to places. Now it connects agents to data.” He described the combined company as “cybersecurity’s operating system: a single control plane to deploy AI, control AI, and enable any agentic application to run safely.”

There is also a defensive logic. Cyberattacks are increasingly AI-driven, and the two companies' recent product work points the same way: CrowdStrike has been adding controls to stop rogue agents at the gateway, and Zscaler's zero-trust model is built for a world where the thing asking for access may not be a person at all. Whoever can credibly govern autonomous software — not just block malware — stands to capture the next decade of security spending, and CrowdStrike is paying $43.9 billion to be that vendor. The company says the deal expands its addressable market by $185 billion.

The market's verdict#

Investors, at least on day one, were not cheering — not for the buyer. CrowdStrike shares fell more than 8% in premarket trading, while Zscaler shares rose more than 17%. That is the classic pattern when an acquirer pays a fat premium in its own stock: the target's holders pocket the gain, the buyer's holders absorb the risk that the price was too high and the integration too hard. An all-stock price is only as good as the acquirer's share price, so the premium Zscaler holders actually receive will move with CrowdStrike's stock until the deal closes.

Both companies reported quarterly results alongside the announcement, and the numbers explain why each side could afford to be ambitious. CrowdStrike's revenue rose 28% year over year to $1.58 billion, with record operating cash flow of $665 million in what Kurtz called the company's best quarter yet. Zscaler's revenue rose 25% to $898.2 million. Neither company is buying from weakness; this is a deal between two growing businesses, priced accordingly.

What could stop it#

Regulatory approval is the main hurdle. A $43.9 billion combination of two of the biggest names in security will draw scrutiny in Washington and in Europe, and the long runway — the deal is not expected to close until the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028 — gives regulators and rivals time to weigh in. The agreement carries a $1.4 billion breakup fee if Zscaler walks away under certain circumstances, including to accept a rival offer. Bloomberg first reported the talks on Friday, so the market has had a weekend to price in the possibility; the formal announcement still moved both stocks sharply.

The leadership plan is settled: Kurtz leads the combined company, Chaudhry runs the Zscaler business and takes a seat on CrowdStrike's board, and the two companies will keep operating separately until closing. That structure is meant to reassure customers that nothing changes overnight — but the hard part of any deal this size is the integration that follows, and the two companies have more than a year to plan it.

What to watch#

Three things. First, the regulators' reception — whether antitrust authorities treat endpoint-plus-network as one market or two will shape the review. Second, CrowdStrike's share price: because the deal is all stock, a sustained slide would shrink what Zscaler holders actually receive and could reopen the price. Third, whether the AI-agent security story lands with customers. The combined company has to sell a unified platform, not two products stapled together — and rivals will spend the next year arguing that their version is already built.
