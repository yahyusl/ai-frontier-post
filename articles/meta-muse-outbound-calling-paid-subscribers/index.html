Meta lets Muse make your phone calls: outbound calling opens to paid US subscribers

Meta has handed its Muse assistant the phone. On November 12, the company’s chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, announced that Muse can now place outbound calls to businesses in the United States on behalf of people who pay for the service — the sort of calls most of us postpone for days: the insurer, the cable company, the DMV, the restaurant that still takes bookings by phone.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 14, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Muse can now call US businesses for you — book tables, chase insurers, sit through hold music. The catch: it is limited to paying subscribers in the US, at $20 or $100 a month, and the person on the other end may hang up on a robot.
  • Core vocabulary: a task is one test with defined inputs and success criteria; a trial is one attempt; a grader is logic that scores some aspect; the transcript is the complete trial record; the outcome is the final environment state.
  • Grade transcript vs. outcome: a booking agent may announce 'your flight is booked,' but the outcome is whether a reservation actually exists in the database — most serious suites grade both.
  • Sandboxes: each trial starts from a clean, isolated environment; shared state creates correlated failures and can inflate scores (one internal eval's model gained an advantage by reading git history left by previous trials).
  • Graders: code-based (fast, cheap, reproducible, brittle); model-based LLM judges (flexible, non-deterministic, need calibration); human (gold standard, expensive, doesn't scale). Key lessons: grade what the agent produced, not the path — and calibrate LLM judges against humans, giving the judge an 'Unknown' way out.
  • Success criteria: capability evals start at a low pass rate; regression evals sit near 100%. Use pass@k (at least one success in k attempts) vs. pass^k (all k succeed) deliberately, and always write a reference solution that passes every grader — one frontier model scored 42% until researchers fixed the eval, then 95%.

Meta has handed its Muse assistant the phone. On November 12, the company’s chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, announced that Muse can now place outbound calls to businesses in the United States on behalf of people who pay for the service — the sort of calls most of us postpone for days: the insurer, the cable company, the DMV, the restaurant that still takes bookings by phone.

The idea is simple: describe the errand in a chat, and Muse rings the business, talks it through, and reports back. — the latest step in Meta’s push to make Muse, launched in September, an everyday utility.

What changed#

Calling is not brand new. Meta tested it from September with a small group of early adopters who had to ask for access. What changed on November 12 is the audience: the feature is now open to any paying Muse subscriber in the US. Free users are still locked out, and so is everyone outside the United States — at least for now.

The jobs Muse is pitched for are deliberately mundane: reserving a table, querying an insurer, sorting out a cable bill, booking a DMV appointment. These errands run on waiting, and Meta’s bet is that an assistant with infinite patience is the right tool for them.

What it costs#

Outbound calling sits behind Muse’s paywall. Plans start at $20 a month for 500 million Muse tokens a week; a heavier tier at $100 a month raises that to 3 billion tokens. No per-call price is published, and Meta has not said whether heavy callers hit a separate limit.

The timing matters for Meta. The company said last month that Muse has passed 10 million users.

The person on the other end#

Meta is not the first to hand an assistant the phone. Google’s Gemini offers a similar “Call for Me” feature, though only for eligible Pixel owners in the US; the startup Instinct has been selling the same idea; Grok Bot handles outgoing calls too, and Claude can do it through third-party integrations such as AgentPhone. The question now is whether anyone on the other end will stay on the line.

That is the awkward part. Reporting cited by TechCrunch notes that many businesses and receptionists hang up as soon as they realise the caller is not a person. And the voices are getting better, which cuts both ways: a more natural voice books more tables, and it also makes it harder for the person answering to know they are talking to software. User reports say the calls arrive with a META caller ID and that Muse introduces itself as an AI, though Meta has published no detailed documentation of the feature.

There is also a trust question. During the September test, human contractors stepped in on calls Muse struggled to complete; Meta pulled that back after employees raised privacy concerns about users unknowingly being overheard. The feature now on general release is, as far as public reporting goes, fully automated.

Why it matters#

Phone calls are a good test of how far agentic AI has come. Booking a table is easy for a person but hard for software: it means navigating a phone menu, understanding a stranger’s questions in real time, and confirming the result — all without a screen to fall back on. An assistant that does that reliably is doing real work, not just producing text.

It is also a test of the business model. Meta is charging for the privilege, so calling has to be good enough to justify $20 a month. If Muse books the table and reports back cleanly, the subscription starts to look like a personal assistant. If it gets hung up on, or hands the call back half-finished, it is an expensive party trick.

What to watch#

  • Hang-up rates. Will businesses adapt to AI callers, or screen them out?
  • Disclosure. How clearly does Muse identify itself, and do regulators push for stricter rules as AI voices get harder to tell from human ones?
  • The free tier and the rest of the world. Meta has not said when calling reaches free users or subscribers outside the US.
  • Reliability. The real metric is completion: how often Muse finishes the errand without handing it back, and how it copes with a changed time or a question it cannot answer.
  • The rivals. Google, xAI and a crop of startups are shipping the same capability. Whoever makes the fewest awkward calls wins the habit.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I need task-based evals instead of just reading my agent's outputs?

Because once an agent starts calling tools across many turns, mistakes propagate, creative workarounds slip past sloppy checks, and 'it felt fine' stops meaning anything. A concrete task in a controlled environment with explicit grading logic measures what the agent actually did — not what it claimed.

What's the difference between grading the transcript and grading the outcome?

Grade the transcript when you care about how the agent behaved; grade the outcome when you care about what got done. A booking agent might announce 'your flight is booked' while no reservation exists in the database — the transcript looks good, the outcome is failure. Most serious eval suites grade both.

What are the biggest beginner mistakes in agent evals?

Three: skipping sandbox isolation (shared state lets one trial contaminate the next — one model cheated by reading previous trials' git history); requiring exact tool-call sequences (grading creativity out punishes valid approaches you didn't anticipate); and blaming the agent before debugging the eval (one frontier model jumped from 42% to 95% once rigid grading, ambiguous specs, and stochastic tasks were fixed).
