AI image tools in 2026: Midjourney vs GPT Image 2.6 vs FLUX 3 vs Nano Banana

Four image generators now lead for different reasons: Midjourney for cinematic realism, GPT Image 2.6 for editing control and legible text, FLUX 3 for precise region editing, and Nano Banana for price and editing accuracy. Here's which fits which creative job.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · September 15, 2026 · 10 min read
TL;DR: Four image generators lead for different reasons in November 2026: Midjourney V8.2 for cinematic realism, GPT Image 2.6 (released November 4) for editing control, legible text, and GIFs, FLUX 3 Image for precise multi-step region editing, and Google's Nano Banana 2.1 for price and editing accuracy. Winner by job: editorial goes to Midjourney, believable product and portrait photography to FLUX 3, literal scene recreation and text-heavy work to GPT Image 2.6, and the cheapest capable option is Nano Banana 2.1 at 3.36 cents a 1K image.

  • GPT Image 2.6 (OpenAI, November 4, 2026): two API models — Flare for speed (as little as 6 seconds per image on low quality) and Sunburst for quality — plus multi-turn editing, markup-guided edits, animated GIFs, SVG export, and saved reference images; $8 input / $30 output per million image tokens.
  • FLUX 3 Image (Black Forest Labs, October 2026): multi-step editing that leaves the rest of the picture alone, bounding boxes, up to ten reference images, output up to 4K; $0.048 per 1K image. Klein, the 5.6B open-weights sibling (October 15), costs $0.009 per 1K image and runs on an 8 GB GPU.

Updated November 10, 2026 — re-verified against Midjourney V8.2, GPT Image 2.6, FLUX 3 Image, and Nano Banana 2.1. What's new in this update: GPT Image 2.6, FLUX 3 Image, Midjourney V8.2, and a fourth contender — Google's Nano Banana.

The AI image generator wars of 2024 and 2025 were mostly about one thing: who could make a picture that didn't look broken. That fight is over. By late 2026, hands have the right number of fingers, text is mostly legible, and the lighting is often genuinely photorealistic. The real question is different now: which tool actually fits the job in front of you?

Four tools have separated themselves into distinct lanes. Midjourney remains the aesthetic champion. OpenAI's GPT Image 2.6, released November 4, 2026, is the strongest all-rounder for text, editing, and now GIFs. Black Forest Labs' FLUX 3 Image is the choice for precise, region-by-region control. And Google's Nano Banana 2.1 — absent from the first version of this comparison — is the price shock: half the cost of its predecessor, and ahead of Google's own premium tier on several benchmarks. Here's how they compare on the four axes that matter most — photorealism, text rendering, edit control, and price.

Photorealism: who makes the most believable image#

Midjourney V8.2 still sets the benchmark for pure visual quality. Its generations have a cinematic finish — natural skin texture, convincing subsurface scattering, rich lighting — that needs minimal prompt engineering to look magazine-ready. V8.2, the default since July 24, 2026, targets aesthetics and reads your Personalization profile more closely than V8.1 did, and it renders 2K HD without upscaling. It also makes short video clips now: standard-definition video is included in Relax mode on the Pro and Mega plans.

But there's a caveat that matters for commercial work: Midjourney has a house style. Even its realistic outputs carry a slightly painterly, editorial quality that resists full suppression. Great for campaigns and concept art; less ideal when a client needs a product shot that could pass for a real photograph.

That's exactly where FLUX 3 Image wins. Its 4K output, bounding-box placement, and multi-step region editing are built for the shot where one object changes and the other eleven must not. The new multi-reference capability (conditioning on up to ten reference images) also helps keep subjects and styles consistent across a shoot — no fine-tuning required. And the family keeps growing sideways: FLUX 3 Max, the image-to-video sibling, renders in 11 seconds flat at 1,139 Elo.

GPT Image 2.6 sits in between. Its photorealism serves a different purpose: consistency with the prompt, not cinematic flair. If you describe a specific scene, you get that scene — not a more beautiful version of it. Some reviewers describe its output as slightly over-processed or stock-like compared to the other two.

Nano Banana 2.1 is the surprise entry here. Google's published numbers put it at 1,050 ELO on text-to-image overall preference — ahead of Nano Banana 2 (990) and ahead of Pro itself (935). On the independent arena board it sits fifth at 1,328 ELO, about 80 points ahead of Pro. The cheaper model beating the premium tier is the detail that tells you where Google's image stack actually stands.

Winner by job: editorial and concept art goes to Midjourney; believable product and portrait photography goes to FLUX 3; literal scene recreation goes to GPT Image 2.6; the best-looking image for the least money goes to Nano Banana 2.1.

Text rendering: who can actually spell#

This was the industry's Achilles' heel for years, and all four have improved dramatically — but a clear gap remains.

GPT Image 2.6 inherited the text advantage OpenAI built into the original GPT Image line: it renders signs, labels, logos, and multi-line copy reliably, in multiple languages. On the arena text-to-image board, Sunburst ranks second and Flare fourth — and OpenAI holds all five of the top spots. If your image must contain words, this is still the safest pick.

Nano Banana 2.1 is a strong second, and the number to know is 0.521: its infographic accuracy, up from 0.179 on Nano Banana 2 — a near-tripling in one release. Posters, diagrams, and UI mockups with real copy are now firmly in its lane.

FLUX 3 Image is close behind. Black Forest Labs made typography a headline feature of the FLUX line, and FLUX 3 keeps that strength. One honest caveat: BFL's own docs still list FLUX.2 as the recommended model for pure text-to-image — FLUX 3's edge is editing, not raw text rendering.

Midjourney V8.2 improved text rendering significantly over its predecessors, and short strings wrapped in double quotes now hold up well. But structured testing tells a more cautious story: on complex text it still lags the other three, and long headlines still come back misspelled. If typography is central to the deliverable — a book cover, an ad headline, packaging — Midjourney is the riskiest choice.

Winner by job: any image where words matter — GPT Image 2.6 first, Nano Banana 2.1 a strong second, FLUX 3 close behind, Midjourney last.

Edit control: who lets you iterate without starting over#

This is where the tools diverge most in philosophy — and where the last two months moved fastest.

GPT Image 2.6 is the iteration king. It was designed for multi-turn editing from the start: describe changes in natural language, refine across turns, and the model preserves the parts you didn't touch. Version 2.6 sharpens this further with markup-guided edits — circle what to change instead of masking regions — saved reference images so a mascot stays the same mascot across a month of posts, SVG export for logos and icons, and animated GIFs straight from a prompt or a photo. For marketers and creators who work conversationally — 'same image, but the jacket is red now' — nothing else comes close. The scale tells the story: OpenAI says users now create more than 4 billion images a week across ChatGPT, Codex, and the API, up from about 3 billion in September.

FLUX 3 Image is built for controlled generation rather than conversational editing. Its headline capability is multi-step editing without collateral damage: revise one region of an image across several passes while the rest of the picture stays put. Users compose scenes with bounding boxes, feed in up to ten reference images, and get outputs up to 4K. Where GPT Image 2.6 shines at casual refinement, Flux shines at precise, reproducible direction — the kind of control a studio pipeline needs.

Midjourney takes a tool-based approach: Vary Region for inpainting, Pan and Zoom Out for expanding the canvas, Remix for changing direction mid-grid, plus Character Reference and Style Reference for keeping subjects consistent across generations. It's a rich toolkit, and for artists it offers finer control than words alone. The trade-off is workflow friction: it speaks in parameters and Discord commands, not conversation, and its web app is still maturing.

Nano Banana 2.1 argues with numbers, not philosophy. Its mask-based editing scores 1,049 against its predecessor's 965, and multi-character consistency hits 1,106 against 978 — the editing numbers, not the generation numbers, are where it leads. A character that keeps its face from one scene to the next is the thing image generators have always been worst at, and this is the cheapest route to it.

Winner by job: conversational iteration goes to GPT Image 2.6; surgical region edits go to FLUX 3; artist toolkits go to Midjourney; cheap, accurate edits go to Nano Banana 2.1.

The price shock: Nano Banana#

Nano Banana 2.1 costs 3.36 cents per 1K image on the Gemini API — half of Nano Banana 2's 6.70 cents — and a 4K image falls to 7.56 cents from 15.10. Pro, the premium tier, stays at 13.4 cents per 1K image and 24 cents at 4K. Read that again: the cheap model costs a quarter of the premium one and beats it on overall preference.

There are also genuinely free routes in. The free Gemini app runs Nano Banana 2, and Google Flow — its AI filmmaking studio — lets accounts with no Google AI plan make up to 20 Nano Banana Pro images a day without spending Flow credits. Paid plans raise the daily Pro cap to 100 a day on Plus and Pro, and 500 on Ultra; Google AI Plus costs $4.99 a month, Pro $19.99, and Ultra starts at $99.99.

The catch is the usual one for a value pick: Pro still buys the highest ceiling. It runs a thinking pass before rendering, takes reference images for characters and products, and holds up best on the hardest prompts. But for most jobs — social creative, mockups, infographics, quick edits — 2.1 is the better model at a quarter of the price, and that inversion is the most interesting pricing fact in image generation right now.

Pricing, access, and the open-weight wildcard#

Pricing shapes the decision as much as quality:

  • Midjourney starts at $10/month on the Basic plan, with Standard at $30, Pro at $60, and Mega at $120 — 20% off each on annual billing. The plans buy fast GPU time (3.3, 15, 30, and 60 hours a month), with extra time at $4 an hour; a V8.1 prompt costs about 2.7 cents on Standard and up. There's no free tier, and there's no public API — you're locked into Midjourney's platform.
  • GPT Image 2.6 is available inside ChatGPT across all tiers including Free, which makes it effectively the cheapest entry point for casual use. For builders, the API splits into two models — Flare (fast, as little as 6 seconds per image on low quality) and Sunburst (quality) — priced at $8 input / $30 output per million image tokens, the same rates OpenAI charged for 2.5. OpenAI claims generation is 50% faster than before; community trackers put the improvement closer to 7 seconds per image down to under 5.
  • FLUX 3 Image is pay-as-you-go with no subscription: $0.048 per 1K image, $0.10 at 2K, $0.607 at 4K. Klein, the 5.6B open-weights sibling, costs $0.009 per 1K image — and it is free to run on your own GPU under its license, with the INT8 build running on 8 GB cards at about 4 seconds per megapixel image on an RTX 5090. There is no free tier on the hosted API. The proprietary full model is available to companies under a commercial weights license.
  • Nano Banana 2.1 is the cheapest entry point of the four: free in the Gemini app (Nano Banana 2), 20 Pro images a day free in Flow, and 3.36 cents per 1K image on the API. Pro costs 13.4 cents per 1K image, with no free tier on the API.

That open-weights point is the decisive one for a whole class of users: if you need images inside your own product, Flux is the only option that lets you own the pipeline. Midjourney explicitly doesn't play there — and Nano Banana's cheap API is the closest thing to a hosted alternative. Also worth a look: Ideogram 4.5, released October 1, promises pixel-stable, drift-free multi-turn editing, with open weights to follow.

Which one fits your creative job#

Here's the practical summary:

  • Concept art, editorial illustration, mood pieces: Midjourney. Nothing matches its aesthetic instincts with so little prompting effort.
  • Photorealistic product and portrait work: FLUX 3. Most believable output, best prompt adherence, most precise control.
  • Anything with text — posters, ads, thumbnails, mockups: GPT Image 2.6. Still the most reliable at spelling and layout.
  • Conversational editing and quick marketing creative: GPT Image 2.6. Markup edits, saved references, GIFs, and multi-turn refinement make it the fastest path from idea to finished asset.
  • Building image generation into an app or automated pipeline: FLUX 3. Open weights, API access, and a 5.6B model that runs on consumer hardware are unmatched — or Nano Banana 2.1 via the API at 3.36 cents an image.
  • Consistent characters or brand assets across dozens of generations: Midjourney's character/style references, FLUX 3's multi-reference conditioning, or GPT Image 2.6's saved references — pick based on whether you want art direction (Midjourney), literal consistency (Flux), or conversational ease (GPT Image).
  • Tight budget, infographics, or high-volume edits: Nano Banana 2.1. Cheapest of the four, free to try, and the best infographic accuracy in this group on paper.

The takeaway#

There's no single winner in 2026, and that's the point. The market has matured into specialization: Midjourney is the artist's tool, GPT Image 2.6 is the marketer's tool, FLUX 3 is the engineer's tool, and Nano Banana 2.1 is the value pick. Most professional creators we see end up with two of the four — typically GPT Image 2.6 for speed-to-asset plus either Midjourney or Flux depending on whether their work leans aesthetic or technical — with Nano Banana 2.1 as the one to add when the bill matters.

If you can only pay for one, ask yourself which failure hurts most: a generic-looking image (get Midjourney), a misspelled headline (get GPT Image 2.6), a pipeline you don't control (get Flux), or a bill that scales faster than your output (get Nano Banana). The answer tells you where to subscribe.
