Nvidia halts AI server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries amid US export crackdown

Nvidia has told manufacturing partners including Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping AI servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, as Washington's new export rules close the remote-access route Chinese firms used to reach its chips.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

The halt follows new US export rules that took effect on November 19 and require licences for advanced chips going to those countries. It is not clear whether the pause is temporary.

Nvidia has told the companies that build its AI servers to stop shipping them to four Southeast Asian countries, freezing one of the main routes its most powerful chips have taken into the region — and, in Washington's view, onward to Chinese buyers.

The Financial Times reported on Thursday that manufacturing partners including Dell and Supermicro have told customers in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia that Nvidia will not authorise their orders to be shipped. It is not clear whether the halt is temporary, and Nvidia has not said publicly how long it will last.

What Nvidia told its partners#

The instruction covers servers containing Nvidia's AI chips, according to the FT's report, which cited three people familiar with the matter. Dell and Supermicro, which assemble Nvidia processors into server racks, passed the message to buyers in the four countries: pending orders cannot ship.

The pause looks like a compliance stopgap rather than a permanent ban, at least as the reporting stands. Nvidia and its partners are still working out how to meet the new US export rules, and the FT reports it is unclear how long the suspension will last.

The rule behind the halt#

The halt follows a new rule from the US Commerce Department, issued on November 16 and in effect since November 19. It requires export licences for shipments of advanced AI chips and servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil — and it targets a specific workaround: Chinese companies renting computing power from data centres abroad that run Nvidia hardware.

Under the rule, data centres already supplying large amounts of computing power to companies in China or other covered locations have 90 days from November 19 to obtain a licence if they want to keep doing so. One cloud executive told the FT there is "no guidance on execution" and "no one knows how to apply for this licence while the 90-day clock is ticking."

On Wednesday, the Commerce Department added Megaspeed — the Singapore-based cloud company at the centre of the chip-diversion allegations that engulfed Nvidia's leadership this month — to its Entity List, meaning US licences are now needed to ship covered goods to it.

Why Southeast Asia became the pressure point#

In recent years, Chinese AI labs have turned to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia to get access to Nvidia's most powerful chips, renting capacity through cloud providers that bought servers with Nvidia GPUs inside. The new rules are designed to close that gap.

The stakes for Nvidia are large. The company booked $104 billion in revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, a 72 percent increase on the year before, according to Engadget's reporting on the halt. That figure counts sales billed through Singapore rather than chips physically ending up there, but it shows how central the region has become to Nvidia's business — and why a shipment freeze there matters beyond the four countries named.

The crackdown also lands days after Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive, following his resignation earlier in the month. Huang is reportedly due to appear before three House committees on December 1, where lawmakers are expected to press him on the company's chip exports.

What happens next#

The open questions are practical ones: how long the halt lasts, what the licence process looks like, and whether the freeze pushes demand elsewhere. On Reddit, where the story surfaced in r/LocalLLaMA, the discussion quickly turned to workarounds — one commenter argued the grey market would simply move to Gulf states, while another, describing themselves as a systems integrator in the region, said US officials are already asking integrators to justify orders for B200 servers and that waiting times have stretched from a year to "basically unknown."

For buyers in the four countries, the immediate effect is the same either way: orders that were in the pipeline are not moving. For the rest of the industry, the halt is the clearest sign yet that Washington's export controls are no longer just rules on paper — they are stopping hardware at the loading dock.

What to watch: whether Nvidia or the Commerce Department clarifies how long the suspension lasts; the first licence decisions under the new rule; and whether other countries in the region see similar pauses.
