OpenAI claims the matrix multiplication exponent is exactly 9/4 — and that nothing can beat it

In a preprint dated November 24, OpenAI claims the exponent of matrix multiplication is exactly 9/4 — the upper bound it proved in October, now paired with a matching lower bound. If it survives peer review, it settles one of theoretical computer science's oldest questions, and rules out the long-held hope that the exponent could be 2.

One of theoretical computer science's oldest open questions may finally have a number attached to it. In a preprint dated November 24, OpenAI claims the exponent of matrix multiplication — the constant ω that governs how fast two matrices can be multiplied — is exactly 9/4, or 2.25, over every field. The paper pairs an upper bound OpenAI proved in October with a matching lower bound, which, if it holds, closes a question that has been open since Volker Strassen's 1969 algorithm first showed the naive cubic method could be beaten.

The claim surfaced on Reddit's r/singularity in the early hours of November 25, where it picked up hundreds of upvotes within the hour. The thread's top comments did not debunk it — but the poster framed it the right way, as conditional on full peer review — and the discussion quickly turned to the one detail that matters most: the lower bound is the new part, and it is the part that has to survive scrutiny.

What the paper actually claims#

ω is the exponent that describes how the arithmetic cost of multiplying two n-by-n matrices grows as n grows. The schoolbook method costs on the order of n³ operations; Strassen showed in 1969 that it could be done in fewer, and theorists have spent the decades since narrowing the range. OpenAI's preprint makes two claims that fit together. First, matrices can be multiplied with an exponent of 9/4 — the upper bound it established in October, now extended to every field. Second, and new, no method can do better: any formal arithmetic circuit needs at least ⌈n9/4/2⌉ nonscalar multiplication or division gates for matrices of size n, with n at least 2. The paper also pins down two related quantities — the geometric border rank of the multiplication tensor is at least n9/4, while its asymptotic rank equals n9/4.

The jump is large by the standards of the field. The previous best upper bound stood at about 2.37, from the laser method. Moving from roughly 2.37 to 2.25 in one step is, in a subject where progress usually arrives several decimal places deep, closer to a demolition — OpenAI's research summary calls it the largest single improvement in matrix multiplication bounds since 1979.

How OpenAI got here#

The November paper is the third act of a story that began in the autumn. On October 2, OpenAI posted the upper bound — ω ≤ 9/4 over the complex numbers — with a Lean formalization running to roughly 20,700 lines. Within weeks, independent groups were rebuilding the argument in cleaner forms: a November 6 exposition from LMU Munich extended the bound to every field, and an October 30 paper from Cornell's Rafael Pass gave a self-contained constructive proof with an explicit algorithm. The new preprint adds the missing half: the lower bound that makes 9/4 exact rather than merely achievable.

AI assistance is part of the story, not a footnote to it. OpenAI's research summary says AI played a role in the discovery, and the Pass paper notes that OpenAI's Codex (Astra) was used extensively to study the October proof and explore candidate constructions. The Lean formalization reportedly verifies the results for division-free circuits over all fields — machine-checked proof, which narrows the space where errors can hide without replacing expert review.

Why 9/4 ends the ω = 2 dream#

The lower bound is what gives the result its weight. An upper bound alone says a faster method exists; a matching lower bound says no faster method is possible, in this model of computation. For decades, the open hope — sometimes stated as a conjecture — was that ω might be 2, meaning matrix multiplication could be done in nearly quadratic time. A proven floor of 9/4 rules that out. The exact speed limit, if the proof stands, is 2.25: better than anything known, worse than the dream.

It also reframes the October result. When the upper bound appeared, it was already a breakthrough; what it lacked was any guarantee that 2.25 was the floor rather than a waystation. The November paper supplies that guarantee — or claims to.

What it doesn't mean#

It does not mean your GPU is about to get faster. The exponent counts arithmetic operations in an idealized model as matrices grow toward infinity; the constructions behind sub-Strassen bounds are so-called galactic algorithms, whose crossover points sit at astronomically large sizes. Nobody is rewriting BLAS on the strength of this paper, and the thread that surfaced it said so plainly.

And it does not mean the result is settled. A preprint has not passed peer review, and the combination of an extraordinary claim, a corporate author and AI-assisted discovery practically guarantees specialists will comb through every lemma — particularly the lower bound, which is the genuinely new argument. The Lean formalization helps: formal verification does not replace expert review, but it shrinks the places an error can hide. The honest status is the one the Reddit poster used: if this holds up under full peer review, a famous problem is closed.

Why it matters, and what to watch#

Even as pure theory, the result matters beyond mathematics. Matrix multiplication is the core operation inside the models OpenAI builds and sells, and the episode is the third in a remarkable week for AI-assisted mathematics on this blog's radar: the machine-checked Riemann proof and its human-readable reconstruction, Anthropic's VP ≠ VNP claim, and now an exact exponent. Whether that cluster is a coincidence or a pattern is itself worth watching.

What to watch: responses from complexity theorists, any independent attempt to verify the lower-bound argument, and whether the Lean formalization expands to cover more of the paper's claims. If the lower bound survives, textbooks get rewritten; if it doesn't, the upper bound alone still stands as the best known — and the hunt for the true floor resumes.
