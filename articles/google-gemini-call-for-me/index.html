Gemini can now call businesses for you: Google’s ‘Call for Me’ turns the assistant into a phone agent on the Pixel 11

Google’s new ‘Call for Me’ experiment lets Gemini place real phone calls to businesses from a Pixel 11 — navigating menus, waiting on hold, and talking to staff while you watch a live transcript. It’s the Duplex demo, eight years later, finally shipping as a product.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · September 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Google has spent eight years promising that its assistant would one day pick up the phone and handle your errands. This week, it finally put the feature in customers' hands. The company announced an early preview of “Call for Me,” a Pixel 11 experiment that lets Gemini dial real businesses on your behalf — introducing itself, navigating automated menus, waiting on hold, and carrying on a conversation with the employee who answers.

The move turns Gemini from an answering machine into a caller, and it lands at a moment when every major lab is racing to give its assistant a voice and a phone number. It is still a tightly fenced experiment: Pixel 11 only, United States only, and only for paying Gemini subscribers running the beta version of Google's Phone app. But the direction is unmistakable — the phone call, the last interaction still firmly stuck in the human era, is now an agent task.

What “Call for Me” actually does#

Tell Gemini to place a call in the Gemini app on your Pixel 11, and the assistant takes it from there. It dials from your own phone number, announces that it is an artificial intelligence program, and discloses that the call is being recorded. Then it does the full, tedious choreography of a business call: pressing through interactive menus, sitting through hold music, and negotiating the request with a person.

The jobs Google names are the classics everyone hates doing themselves: asking a store whether a product is in stock, booking a restaurant table, moving an appointment to a different date, or putting an item on hold. Where the feature breaks new ground is the approved personal information it can share mid-call — your name, contact details, that sort of thing — which lets it complete transactions that earlier calling features could only research. According to Google, you watch the whole thing unfold as a live text transcript and can jump in to take over the conversation at any point.

The guardrails: what it won't do#

The fence around the experiment is deliberately high. Callers must be 18 or older, in the US, own a Pixel 11, subscribe to Google's paid AI tier — starting at $5 a month — and run the Phone by Google public beta. Google says it is starting small because “real-world conversations are nuanced,” and it wants time to get the feature right before a wider rollout.

The behavior guardrails are stricter still. Gemini cannot make emergency calls, cannot handle payments, passwords, or Social Security numbers, and the terms forbid political messages, telemarketing, and placing calls on behalf of businesses or government agencies — personal use only. Because the calls go out over your own cellular line, standard carrier charges apply, and the business on the other end sees your real number. Miss the moment when a transaction needs a card number, and the line goes cold: any reservation requiring a deposit still forces you to watch the transcript and step in.

Eight years from Duplex to a phone agent#

If this sounds familiar, it's because Google has been auditioning the idea for nearly a decade. At its 2018 developer conference, the company stunned the industry with Duplex, a Google Assistant demo that called a hair salon to book an appointment — complete with natural-sounding “umm”s and “ah”s — and promptly ignited a debate about whether AI should have to identify itself. It did not ship as a product in anything like that form.

Since then, Google has shipped the feature in pieces. Last year it rolled out “Ask for Me,” which let Gemini call businesses to ask about pricing and services. Pixel owners have long had “Hold for Me” and “Talk to a Live Rep,” which wait on hold and hand you the phone when a human picks up, and “Direct My Call,” which transcribes automated menus so you can navigate them yourself. “Call for Me” is the first time Google has assembled the whole loop — dial, wait, converse — into one assistant you address in plain language, running under your own identity.

The phone-call agent race is on#

Google is not alone in treating the telephone network as the next agentic surface. Meta is testing a similar automated-calling feature for its Muse agent, though it drew pushback after some of those calls turned out to be routed through human contractors. Voice-call capabilities are popping up across the assistant landscape as labs look for tasks where agents visibly earn their keep — and nothing earns a consumer's affection quite like never sitting through hold music again.

That framing cuts both ways. The person avoiding a five-minute call is still dumping that five minutes on a front-desk employee, now adapting to synthetic cadence and the occasional misunderstanding. If enough businesses find automated callers annoying, the rational response is to deprioritize them — or hang up on the robotic self-introduction. Google's disclosure-first design (identifying itself, announcing recording) is partly etiquette and partly survival: one wave of annoyed merchants could poison the well for every agent that follows.

What to watch#

Three things decide whether “Call for Me” is a landmark or a footnote. First, merchant tolerance: do businesses accept bot calls gracefully, or does the feature earn Gemini a reputation as a robocall with a brand name? Second, reliability: Google's small-scale rollout is an admission that real conversations are messy, and the live-transcript design quietly admits the assistant still needs a human copilot. Third, expansion: Pixel 11, US-only, paid subscribers — how fast this leaves that corral says everything about Google's confidence.

The deeper signal is strategic. Eight years after Duplex, Google finally believes the technology is ready for its own phone number. The agent era was supposed to live inside apps and browsers; “Call for Me” bets it will also live on the world's oldest interactive network. Watch whether Meta's Muse and the rest follow within the quarter — and whether the people answering those calls can tell.
