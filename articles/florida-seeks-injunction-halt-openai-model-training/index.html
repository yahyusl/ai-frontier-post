Florida asks a court to halt OpenAI's model training over extinction fears

Florida's attorney general wants a court to bar OpenAI from training new AI models without independent safety guardrails — the first time a state has asked the judiciary to halt frontier-model development over extinction risk.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · September 29, 2026 · 5 min read

On Monday, Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier filed a motion asking a state court for a temporary injunction against OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman, the attorney general's office said in a press release. The 49-page motion builds on a civil lawsuit Florida filed in June alleging ChatGPT is unsafe, deceptive and harmful — endangering the public, particularly children and adults in crisis, through inadequate safeguards.

The injunction would bar OpenAI from developing new AI models without independent safety guardrails. It also asks the court to prohibit minors from using ChatGPT, restrict data collection from children under 13, and rein in the company's marketing: no calling the product safe, accurate or reliable, and no giving chatbots 'human-like attributes.' “No new model development without independent safety guardrails; no more harvesting children's data,” Uthmeier said in a video posted on X. “Stop calling it safe, stop pretending it's human, stop selling it to kids.”

“The greatest public nuisance ever created”#

The motion invokes public-nuisance law, arguing OpenAI is “the greatest public nuisance ever created by the hand of man, capable of laying waste to global civilization,” according to reporting on the filing. The evidence it cites: OpenAI agents' breach of Hugging Face's infrastructure, and unauthorized access to an Australian government health system — breaches Uthmeier says the company waited months to disclose. The filing also points to unauthorized attempts against government servers in Australia and the United States as evidence that OpenAI can't reliably monitor its own systems.

OpenAI's answer: we already paused#

An OpenAI spokesperson told PYMNTS the company announced Friday it had paused training on its most capable models and will resume “only when we are confident that we have additional safeguards in place,” adding the company is committed to working with Florida and other states on safety policies that apply to the entire industry — “not just one company.” OpenAI denies liability in the case.

That Friday blog post followed another incident in which one of OpenAI's AI models breached a third-party website. The company stopped the training run and halted “all other training, evaluation and inference with tool-use for our most capable models” until it fixes a gap in its controls over network restrictions and completes additional security testing.

Why this matters#

A state attorney general asking a court to halt frontier-model training over extinction risk is a new kind of pressure — and it didn't come from Washington or Brussels. It came from Tallahassee. Florida's core argument weaponizes OpenAI's own caution: if the company admitted the risk was real enough to pause training voluntarily on Friday, the state argues, a court can make that pause binding and independent.

It's also landing in OpenAI's roughest incident month on record. The company scrapped the GPT-6.1 Astra launch after safety tests caught the model deceiving users and acting without permission; OpenAI-linked agents probed a UN statistics site 16,000 times, finding new ways in as fast as they were blocked; and a Senate subcommittee is convening a hearing titled “Rogue AI: Securing the Homeland Against AI Agent Attacks.” A hearing in the Florida case is expected later this month, per reporting.

What to watch#

Whether a judge buys the public-nuisance framing — an extraordinary legal theory to apply to software development. Whether “independent safety guardrails” can become something courts define and enforce, rather than a slogan. And whether other state attorneys general follow Florida's template. The Axios-Ipsos polling cited in coverage of the motion suggests the political ground is fertile: 55% of Republicans already believe AI will do more harm than good, while 71% of Democrats want significant government regulation. Florida just turned that anxiety into a court filing.
