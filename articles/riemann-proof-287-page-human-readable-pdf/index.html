The machine-checked Riemann proof now has a 287-page human-readable version

Four days after the Lean archive landed, the Riemann hypothesis has something it did not have before: a version a mathematician can actually read. On November 24, Arizona State mathematician Jimmie Adriazola published a 287-page human-readable reconstruction of the Alpöge–Claude argument — the formal proof the Lean and Mathlib communities certified on November 21. The manuscript is public, free to read, and already the most talked-about maths post on Reddit tonight. It is also, by its author’s own account, unfinished business.

TL;DR: Jimmie Adriazola of Arizona State University has turned the machine-checked Alpöge–Claude proof of the Riemann hypothesis into a 287-page manuscript mathematicians can read from beginning to end, revised November 24. The formal proof it reconstructs spans 766,788 Lean declarations — far beyond human reading. The catch, stated plainly by the author: the manuscript is a reconstruction in progress, with open statements still listed, and does not yet stand as a proof independent of the Lean development.
  • What it is: a 287-page human-readable reconstruction of the Alpöge–Claude Riemann argument, published November 24 on GitHub under a CC-BY-4.0 licence. It grew from a 66-page first draft, through 225 pages on November 23.
  • How it was built: Adriazola worked backwards from the Lean declaration riemannHypothesis through its dependencies — in one stretch, 631 Lean theorems collapsed into eight propositions — using Claude, GPT and Kimi. Claude also drafted proofs, ran computations and prepared audit reports under his direction.
  • The timeline: Alpöge released the Lean archive on November 20, crediting himself with the proof plan and Claude with the idea of a chain of theta lifts of length about √X; the Lean and Mathlib communities certified the formal statement and proof on November 21.
  • The caveats: the author writes that he “cannot guarantee that everything in this repository is correct”; Section 19 lists open internal statements, and Section 17.4.3 proposes a shorter argument with no Lean certification. A referee pass on November 23 found the open-statements list incomplete — but identified no false statement.
  • The reaction: the r/accelerate thread announcing it reached roughly 447 upvotes and 109 comments within an hour — the fastest-moving maths post of the day — with top commenters reporting they had scrolled the PDF and found it genuinely readable.

Checking a proof used to mean reading it. The Alpöge–Claude argument for the Riemann hypothesis never offered that option: in machine form it runs to 766,788 Lean declarations, including 622,409 theorems, stitched together by an automated proof checker no human could follow end to end. Since the Lean archive appeared on November 20, the loudest complaint from mathematicians has been simple — nobody can read the thing.

Adriazola's manuscript is the first serious answer to that complaint. Starting from the Lean declaration riemannHypothesis, he traced every live dependency backwards and rewrote each piece as conventional mathematics, checking the hypotheses of the published theorems it invokes. The result reads like a paper: mathematicians can now review the argument from beginning to end without consulting Lean at all.

From 766,788 declarations to 287 pages#

The compression is the point. In one part of the argument, 631 Lean theorems reduced to eight propositions. The machinery underneath is formidable — chains of theta lifts, automorphic forms, Galois cohomology — but the manuscript packages it into named mechanisms with explicit boundaries, so a reader can see where each piece lives and what it depends on.

What it is — and what it isn't#

The manuscript's own status notes are unusually candid, and they matter more than the page count. Adriazola writes that he cannot guarantee everything in the repository is correct, and that the manuscript "does not yet constitute a proof of the Riemann hypothesis independent of the Lean development." Section 19 collects the internal statements for which a conventional proof is still being reconstructed, together with the published inputs whose exact forms still need source verification.

The sharpest example sits in Section 17. There, Adriazola departs from the formal proof and proposes a shorter argument for a uniform bound on a Galois cohomology group — an argument with no Lean certification. He flags it explicitly and asks for a check. A related step implies a bound on Tate–Shafarevich groups that would be stronger than the published bounds of Soulé (1999) and de la Torre Durán (2026); the manuscript states that step as a hypothesis and does not claim it.

The November 23 referee pass sharpened rather than settled the picture: it found the list of open statements incomplete — a GSp4 argument had been absorbed into the non-generic cases — and isolated points needing specialist attention, including a patching step and the role of an internal adjoint-lifting theorem. No false statement was identified. The effect, in Adriazola's words, was to make the remaining frontier more precise.

Why a readable version matters#

A machine-checked proof is only as trustworthy as the checker and the formalization — and only as useful as the community's ability to scrutinize it. The Lean certification of November 21 settled the formal question: the statement and proof check out in Lean. But formal certification does not tell mathematicians why the argument works, and it cannot catch a formalization that proves a subtly different statement than the one intended. That is what human reading is for, and it is why the illegibility of the archive has been the story's open wound since day one.

The reception suggests the wound was real. Within an hour of the announcement, the r/accelerate thread had gathered roughly 447 upvotes and 109 comments, with the top commenters reporting they had scrolled through the PDF and found it readable — one, self-described as no maths major, said they understood much of what the authors did and how. The manuscript gives mathematicians and interested readers the same thing: a map of the argument that a human can hold in their head.

What to watch#

Three things decide whether this becomes the definitive human account or a waypoint. First, the verification map Adriazola says comes next: a complete list of the remaining internal and external obligations, refereed, then worked through systematically. Second, the specialist checks he has explicitly requested — above all the Section 17 cohomology bound, where the manuscript departs from the certified route. Third, whether the compression holds: the manuscript grew from 66 to 287 pages as summarized packages were opened up, and the author wants it shorter, not longer.

The underlying claim has not changed since November 20: Alpöge's plan, Claude's theta-lift idea, a formal proof certified by the Lean community. What changed on November 24 is that the rest of mathematics can finally read it — with the gaps labelled, the shortcuts flagged, and the author inviting corrections in public. For a proof this consequential, that is the right kind of progress: not a victory lap, but a document the field can now actually argue with.

Frequently asked questions

Does the 287-page manuscript prove the Riemann hypothesis?

Not on its own. It is a human-readable reconstruction of Levent Alpöge’s Lean-formalized proof, which the Lean and Mathlib communities certified on November 21, 2026. The manuscript itself lists open internal statements that still need conventional proofs, and its author says it does not yet stand as a proof independent of the Lean development.

Who wrote the manuscript, and how?

Jimmie Adriazola, a mathematician at Arizona State University. He worked backwards from the Lean declaration riemannHypothesis through its dependencies, using Claude, GPT and Kimi in the reconstruction; Claude also drafted proofs, ran computations and prepared audit reports under his direction.

Where can I read it?

The manuscript and its LaTeX source are public on GitHub at jimjamzola/RH-Alpoge-Reconstruction, under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence. The current PDF runs to 287 pages and was revised on November 24, 2026.
