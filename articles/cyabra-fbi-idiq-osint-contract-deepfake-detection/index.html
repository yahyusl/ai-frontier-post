The FBI's new deepfake hunter: Cyabra wins a seat on the Bureau's five-year AI detection contract

Cyabra announced Monday it has won positions on the FBI's Specialized Open-Source Intelligence Tools contract — in both the Image Information and Video Information categories — putting its AI-powered deepfake-detection platform on the Bureau's approved vendor list for a five-year ordering period running through September 2031. The Bureau is buying evidence-grade answers to one question: is this image or video real?

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

The news came in a company press release Monday morning, syndicated widely and picked up by the financial press the same day. Cyabra Strategy Inc., the company's wholly owned subsidiary, is now eligible to compete for task orders from FBI headquarters divisions and field offices across the United States — any unit that needs to know whether an image or video is real can now route the question through this contract vehicle.

What the contract buys

The product is a cloud-native SaaS platform that scores the authenticity of images and video and returns supporting forensic signals. The detection menu, per the release: GAN- and diffusion-generated content, face swaps, splice points — plus C2PA content-provenance checks and account authenticity scoring, so analysts also get context on who is sharing the content. The logic is blunt: generative AI has made manipulated media faster, cheaper, and harder to spot by eye, and federal investigators need scalable analysis that produces evidence, not hunches.

"When an image or video surfaces in an investigation, the first question is whether it is real, and the next is whether the account sharing it is authentic," said Dan Brahmy, Co-Founder and CEO of Cyabra. "The FBI's decision to create dedicated image and video categories signals that investigative demand is moving toward evidence-based answers that hold up under scrutiny."

A hunting license, not a purchase order

An IDIQ position guarantees no revenue — it is a seat at the table, not a purchase order. Cyabra still has to beat other awardees for each task order, and the release's own fine print notes that task orders of "material value" are not guaranteed, and the company must still satisfy government security and personnel requirements before FBI users get access. That said, the company calls the award a pathway to recurring federal license revenue and a credible reference point as it chases more federal business.

It builds on Cyabra's existing public-sector contract vehicles and channel partnerships across U.S. federal, defense, and intelligence agencies — and follows the company's Sept. 30 acceptance into USSOCOM's SOF RACER consortium, which opened a direct lane to special-operations technology requirements. "This IDIQ gives FBI divisions a streamlined way to acquire image and video analysis in support of their investigative missions, and we look forward to competing to provide ours," said Keith E. Noble, Ph.D., the company's Vice President of U.S. Government Sales.

Why it matters

Federal procurement is where research hype goes to become infrastructure. The Bureau standing up dedicated image and video buckets inside its OSINT tools vehicle — rather than treating visual media as a footnote to text analysis — is the demand signal. Deepfake detection just moved from a research problem to a line item, and the vendors who can produce answers that hold up in an investigation are about to find out exactly how big that line item is.

Cyabra, which trades on Nasdaq under the ticker CYAB, describes the full platform at cyabra.com.
