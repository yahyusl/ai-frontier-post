Anthropic's 58-company study: AI isn't firing people — it's quietly freezing hiring

Anthropic's economists interviewed 66 people at 58 of its enterprise customers. Only two said AI had eliminated a job — but two-thirds said it had slowed hiring, and nine had cut offshore contractors. The layoff wave hasn't arrived in these companies. The hiring freeze already has.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 25, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Anthropic's economics team interviewed 66 people at 58 of its enterprise customers. Only two companies said AI had fully eliminated a job — but two-thirds said AI had slowed their hiring, nine had cut offshore contractors, and startups estimated they would need four times as many staff without AI. The disruption so far is a hiring freeze, not a layoff wave.
  • Two-thirds hiring less: people at two-thirds of the 58 companies said AI had reduced their hiring — mostly growth hires that never happened, plus roles left unfilled after departures.
  • Almost no layoffs: only two companies said AI automation had fully eliminated a position. Five pushed out employees who would not adopt AI; ten hired more, mostly into AI implementation and IT support.
  • Contractors first: nine companies cut contracted developers, customer service, legal and back-office staff, many already offshore. One finance firm cut well over 100 offshore contractors.
  • Startups run lean: the six startups that gave an estimate said they would need, on average, four times as many employees without AI.
  • Caveats: all 58 firms are Anthropic customers, chosen for range rather than sampled at random; value assessments are qualitative and self-reported.

The most feared version of the AI jobs story is the mass layoff: whole departments replaced by models in a single quarter. Anthropic's own economists went looking for it inside the companies that use Claude every day — and mostly didn't find it. What they found instead may be harder to see, and harder to reverse: the jobs that simply never get posted.

The report, Working with AI, was published on November 23 by three researchers on Anthropic's economics team, Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory. It rests on 66 interviews with people at 58 of Anthropic's enterprise customers in the US — AI-native startups, a century-old manufacturer, law firms, hedge funds, a trucking company, a publisher and a dental group. The authors are upfront about the limits: every firm in the sample buys from Anthropic, the sample was picked for range rather than drawn at random, and the value figures are what interviewees said, rarely backed by controlled tests. That honesty is part of what makes the findings worth taking seriously.

The headline number: hiring, not firing#

Across all 58 companies, only two interviewees said AI automation had fully eliminated a position and cost someone their job. Five companies described pushing out employees who refused to adopt AI tools. Ten said they were hiring more because of AI, mostly into AI implementation roles and IT support. If the story stopped there, it would read as reassurance.

It doesn't stop there. People at two-thirds of the companies said AI had reduced their hiring — mostly growth hiring that never happened, plus roles left unfilled after departures. As one interviewee put it, the company may not be firing, but it is not hiring either. An engineering leader at a health management company described the trade in blunt terms: the firm had added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people.

Where the cuts actually landed#

The clearest damage is not to full-time employees at all. Nine companies described cutting contracted software developers, customer service representatives, legal counsel and back-office staff — many of them already offshore. One finance company downsized well over 100 offshore data and developer contractors. The authors call this a possible early warning for the international business services industry: the outsourced jobs that went first were built on modular tasks, self-contained pieces of work that can be handed off separately, and that made them unusually easy to replace. One company built a document-review agent that cut the number of reviewers it expected to hire the following year by more than 90%.

The startup end of the sample runs leanest of all. One chief operating officer told the researchers that 90% of his operations budget went to AI tokens and 10% to headcount, with an explicit rule: don't hire anybody in the back office, automate it instead. A marketing startup described its "marketing factory" flipping from 80% human work and 20% AI to the reverse. The six startups that gave an estimate said they would need, on average, four times as many employees without AI.

What companies are actually building#

The report catalogs roughly 300 AI applications across the 58 firms, and describes 100 pieces of software in enough detail to sort. Four in five automated work people used to do by hand — updating financial spreadsheets, taking meeting notes, auditing 401(k) payments — while the remaining fifth replaced software the companies had been buying from outside vendors.

Most of the value, interviewees said, comes from doing more rather than spending less. About half of the output gains came from speeding up or expanding existing work; the other half from better quality or entirely new products — a dental company's after-hours answering service, a medical group's up-front cost estimates, a real estate manager mocking up vacant units with a tenant's own branding. And much of the building is now done by nontechnical staff: one accountant built an app that audits whether employers' 401(k) payments land in the right fund, work that used to take accountants 80 hours by hand.

Why the best results need reorganization#

The firms getting the most from AI, the authors argue, are the ones that reorganize around it. Top-down automation — pick a workflow, automate it, roll it out — can work, but it carries costs: job insecurity, fears of workplace surveillance, and resistance from the very experts needed to build the system. One operations supervisor starts each day by reviewing an AI report on the throughput and error rate of every member of his 12-person team; one executive called the effect a panopticon. Automations can also decay: a law firm's document-management tools became low-quality "zombie" apps hardly anyone used, and a conflict-checking agent was shelved after the staff it was meant to help pushed back.

Bottom-up adoption has the opposite problem: pockets of brilliance that never spread, and one-off apps nobody maintains — the accountant's 401(k) tool still wasn't in active use months later. The highest-value deployments sat in the middle: companies that broadened roles so one person owned a whole workflow, and moved staff toward the tasks AI cannot yet do. At one law firm, junior lawyers no longer write first drafts of some documents, so the firm pairs each junior with a senior attorney to mark up the AI's draft together — which is how the juniors still learn what a good draft looks like. The reorganization, in other words, is the product.

What to watch#

Three things will test whether this picture holds. First, whether the hiring freeze shows up in labor-market statistics: a job that is never posted leaves no layoff to count, so the signal will be in slower hiring rates and shrinking entry-level pipelines, not in unemployment claims. Second, whether the contractor cuts spread beyond offshore business services into domestic contract work. And third, whether the quantitative follow-up research the authors call for confirms what 66 qualitative interviews suggested.

The report's own closing line is the one to keep: none of this is a guarantee against AI-induced job loss. What it shows is the shape of the disruption so far — not a wave of pink slips, but a quiet decision, repeated across two-thirds of these companies, to grow without hiring. That is a smaller headline than a mass layoff, and a harder one to argue with.

Frequently asked questions

Does this study prove AI is not causing layoffs?

No. It covers 58 Anthropic enterprise customers who agreed to be interviewed — not a random or representative sample — and the value assessments are qualitative and self-reported. The authors state plainly that none of it is a guarantee against AI-induced job loss, and call for quantitative follow-up research.

Who is losing work first?

Offshore contractors doing modular, self-contained work: contracted software developers, customer service representatives, legal counsel and back-office staff. Nine of the 58 companies cut contractors, and one finance firm cut well over 100 offshore data and developer contractors.

What are companies actually getting from AI?

Mostly more output, not lower costs. About half the output gains came from speeding up or expanding existing work; the other half from better quality or entirely new products. Coding agents also let nontechnical staff build their own software — one accountant built a 401(k) audit tool that used to take 80 hours by hand.
