Chinese frontier AI models carry the fingerprint of export-controlled Nvidia Blackwell GPUs, forensic report finds

A forensic analysis of published model weights finds six recent Chinese models carry numerical patterns matching Nvidia’s export-controlled Blackwell GPUs — the first technical evidence behind years of allegations. The report itself stresses what the fingerprint does not prove.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 12, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Unemployment Labs researcher Will DePue reports that six models from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group, all released since April 2026, carry initialization fingerprints matching Nvidia Blackwell GPUs (B200/B300 or GB200/GB300) — chips that require a US export licence for China, with none reported. The evidence comes entirely from the models’ published weights, was reproduced on physical Blackwell hardware for two models, and agreed with disclosed hardware in all 24 validation cases. The fingerprint identifies the hardware that created the initial weights; it supports, without proving, that Blackwell also did the training — and it says nothing about where the chips were, how access was obtained, or whether any law was broken.
  • The six models: DeepSeek-V4-Flash (B200/B300) and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (GB200/GB300), Kimi K3 and Kimi K3.1-Pro (GB200/GB300), MiniMax-M3 (B200/B300), and Ling-3.1-flash (GB200/GB300) — all released between April and November 2026.
  • The method: the Philox pseudorandom-number seed and thread-count layout preserved in each model’s published initial weights; roughly six trillion seed-and-layout combinations searched; matches at 49 to 1,385 sigma per model.
  • The before-and-after: the same labs’ earlier models read as the older Hopper generation — DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) and Kimi K2.5 (January 2026) among them — while the newest models from Alibaba, Zhipu, Tencent, Xiaomi and StepFun still read as Hopper.
  • The limits: a fingerprint identifies the hardware that created the initial weights, not necessarily the hardware that trained the model; it cannot establish chip location, access, or a violation. The only alternative hardware identified is specially configured AMD MI300X chips, also export-restricted — ruled out for DeepSeek-V4-Flash, “very unlikely” for the other five.
  • The context: US officials and press reports had already alleged that DeepSeek and Moonshot AI were using Blackwell chips, without publishing technical evidence. Blackwell B200, B300, GB200 and GB300 exports to China require a US licence; none have been reported.

For years, US officials and press reports have alleged that Chinese AI labs were training their newest models on Nvidia’s Blackwell GPUs — chips Washington bars from export to China — without ever publishing technical evidence. On November 12, a forensic report from Unemployment Labs changed that: researcher Will DePue says the published weights of six recent Chinese models carry numerical fingerprints matching Blackwell hardware, and that the match was confirmed on physical Blackwell chips for two of them.

The six models come from four labs — DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group — and all were released since April 2026. The report is careful about what that proves: a fingerprint identifies the hardware that created a model’s initial weights. It supports, without proving, that Blackwell also did the training. It says nothing about where the chips were, how access was obtained, or whether any export control was violated.

The before-and-after is what makes the pattern hard to dismiss. DeepSeek-V3, released in December 2024, reads as Hopper — the lab itself disclosed H800 hardware. Kimi K2.5, from January 2026, also reads as Hopper. The Blackwell readings start with the April 2026 releases and continue through the autumn. And the pattern is not universal: the newest readable models from Alibaba, Zhipu, Tencent, Xiaomi and StepFun still read as Hopper, which cuts against a flaw in the method. The B200/B300 are single GPUs with 148 streaming multiprocessors; the GB200/GB300 are Grace Blackwell superchips with 152.

How a weight file betrays its hardware#

Training a model starts with initialization: random starting weights, drawn from a pseudorandom number generator. DePue’s method recovers traces of those initial values from the published weights, then searches for the hardware configuration and seed that reproduces them. The tell is in the fine details of how the numbers were generated — the Philox pseudorandom-number seed and the thread-count layout, which vary by chip. The search space is enormous: roughly six trillion seed-and-layout combinations. The matches came back at 49 to 1,385 sigma per model, against a chance benchmark of about 8 sigma.

For two models, the match was confirmed on real hardware. DePue reports that a single Blackwell B300 reproduced DeepSeek-V4-Flash’s initial weights, and a rented GB200 reproduced Kimi K3’s to a correlation of 0.999995. Across 24 cases where a developer had publicly named a model’s hardware, the method agreed every time — though 11 of those involve step-zero checkpoints and are not fully independent. Crucially, the evidence comes entirely from the models’ own published weights, not from leaks or insider accounts, which means anyone with the weights and the method can in principle check the work.

What the fingerprint does not prove#

The most important passage in the report is the one that limits it. A lab could initialize weights on one system and train on another, and a Hopper reading does not rule out later Blackwell training. The weights cannot say where the chips sat — Blackwell systems can be rented outside China, a point the report itself makes — or how access was obtained. The only alternative hardware the report identifies is specially configured AMD MI300X chips, which are also export-restricted; it rules that out for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and calls it very unlikely for the other five.

The report also ventures a scale estimate — roughly 1,500 to 3,500 Blackwell GPUs per run for one and a half to three months, about 12,000 across five runs — and immediately undercuts it: the author writes that he is “not confident in these estimates.” Finally, the report is the work of a single researcher, not a peer-reviewed paper, and no independent reproduction has yet been published. That is the next test, and it is one the method invites: the weights are public, and the fingerprint is either there or it is not.

Why it matters#

Export controls on advanced chips are the centerpiece of US policy toward Chinese AI, and until now the debate has run on allegations and logistics — seized shipments, smuggling arrests, shell companies. A forensic method that reads hardware out of the models themselves gives regulators something new: technical evidence that does not depend on catching a shipment. It also raises the question the report does not answer: if the chips are being used, how did they get there?

The finding also lands while Washington is reportedly weighing tighter rules on remote access to advanced chips: The Information has reported that the Commerce Department is finalizing a rule aimed at stopping Chinese AI companies from remotely using advanced chips in data centers abroad — a rule this finding would tend to support. For the labs named, the report is an accusation they have not yet answered publicly. For everyone else building on open weights, it shows a model’s training history is harder to hide than its builders may have assumed.

What to watch#

Three things. First, independent reproduction: the weights are public, the method is described, and a second lab confirming the fingerprints would turn a single-author finding into a settled fact. Second, responses — from DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax and Ant Group, from Nvidia, and from US officials — and whether any of them disputes the method or the readings. Third, enforcement: whether the finding shows up in export-control actions, licence decisions, or the remote-access rule reportedly in the works. Until then, the honest summary is the report’s own: strong evidence of access to Blackwell hardware, and no proof yet of how it was obtained or whether a law was broken.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Blackwell fingerprint?

A numerical pattern in a model’s published initial weights — the Philox random-number seed and thread-count layout — that identifies the GPU hardware that created them. DePue’s report matched six Chinese models to Blackwell B200/B300 or GB200/GB300 chips.

Does the report prove Chinese labs trained on smuggled Blackwell chips?

No. It proves the initial weights were created on Blackwell hardware, which supports but does not prove Blackwell did the training. It does not establish where the chips were, how access was obtained, or whether export controls were violated.

Which models and labs are named?

DeepSeek-V4-Flash and V4-Pro (DeepSeek), Kimi K3 and K3.1-Pro (Moonshot AI), MiniMax-M3 (MiniMax) and Ling-3.1-flash (Ant Group). Earlier models from the same labs read as Hopper; Alibaba, Zhipu, Tencent, Xiaomi and StepFun still read as Hopper.
