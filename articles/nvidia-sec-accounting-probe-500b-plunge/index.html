SEC is probing Nvidia’s accounting — and the stock just lost $500 billion in a day

Reuters reports SEC enforcement staff are examining potential accounting issues at Nvidia. Shares fell 14% on Friday, erasing about $491 billion in a single session — capping the stock’s worst week since 2008.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 21, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: The SEC is examining potential accounting issues at Nvidia, Reuters reported on Friday, citing two people briefed on the matter. Shares fell 14% in a day, erasing roughly $491 billion of market value. The probe caps a brutal six weeks in which the stock has lost about 48% from its October record — even though the business just reported $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue, up 103% year over year.

  • What it is not: an investigation is not a finding of wrongdoing; many SEC probes close without enforcement action.

Nvidia's terrible month got materially worse on Friday. Shares in the world's most important AI chipmaker fell 14% in a single session after Reuters reported that Securities and Exchange Commission enforcement staff are examining potential accounting issues at the company — wiping out roughly $491 billion of market value in one day.

The drop capped a week in which the stock fell 23%, its worst weekly slide since 2008, and its seventh straight week of losses. At Friday's close of $125.53, Nvidia is down about 48% from its record close of $239.24 on October 6. Roughly $2.7 trillion of market value has vanished in six weeks — a rout that has now dragged the company from the top of the global market-value rankings to fourth, behind Apple, Microsoft and Alphabet.

What Reuters reported#

The Wall Street Journal first reported on Tuesday that the SEC was investigating Nvidia, but could not establish what the probe was about. On Friday, Reuters — citing two people briefed on the matter — said the agency's enforcement staff are scrutinizing potential accounting issues at the company. The SEC declined to comment, and Nvidia did not immediately respond. Reuters could not determine when the inquiry began or which accounting practices are under review.

That uncertainty is part of why the market reaction was so sharp. The shares were already down more than 6% before the Reuters report landed, caught in a broad semiconductor sell-off in which AMD also lost 13% and Micron nearly 14%. But an accounting inquiry lands differently from the other problems Nvidia has collected this autumn: it goes directly at trust in the numbers, and the numbers are the one thing that had still been holding up.

What could be in scope#

Two areas are the most likely candidates for scrutiny, based on what Nvidia itself has disclosed.

The first is how the company reports where its sales come from. Nvidia breaks out revenue by where customers are headquartered, rather than where they are billed. On that basis, about 63% of last quarter's revenue came from US-based customers. The Financial Times has reported that Nvidia made the switch as sales billed to Singapore approached half of its total — an awkward look with Southeast Asia now a hub for chips smuggled into China, and roughly $104 billion of 2025 revenue booked through its Singapore unit. Interim chief executive Colette Kress has said more than 99% of the data-centre revenue invoiced to Singapore came from US-based customers.

The second is how Nvidia helps its customers pay for its chips. On Tuesday's earnings call, Kress said the company's guarantees totaled $118 billion at the end of the quarter, including $105 billion of credit support tied to a single data-center campus. When a chipmaker helps fund the projects that buy its chips, it gets harder to tell how much demand would exist on its own — and that is exactly the kind of disclosure the SEC is watching closely at AI companies. The agency's enforcement chief told Reuters last month that it was examining AI-company disclosures for fuller risk reporting.

A pile of problems#

The probe does not arrive in isolation. Co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive earlier this month, and the Justice Department is reviewing Nvidia's planned $12.9 billion purchase of Hugging Face, according to Reuters. The company is also searching for a permanent chief executive, with Kress — its longtime chief financial officer — holding the role on an interim basis. An independent board committee investigation is running alongside the SEC's, with a 10-Q filing due in the coming weeks.

What makes the sell-off striking is that the underlying business has not yet shown the strain. Revenue jumped 103% to $115.8 billion last quarter, and the company guided to about $130 billion this quarter, with Kress pointing to roughly 75% revenue growth next fiscal year. Investors are now pricing the governance crisis, not the income statement: at Friday's close the stock trades at about 8.4 times forward earnings, against about 29 times for AMD.

What to watch#

Three things will decide how serious this becomes. First, whether the SEC's focus emerges — and whether it touches revenue recognition or the customer-financing guarantees, the two areas where an adverse finding would matter most. Second, the coming 10-Q and the board committee's findings, which may force Nvidia to say more than it has so far. And third, the permanent CEO appointment, which the market is treating as the cleanest way to draw a line under the leadership crisis.

One caution is worth keeping in mind through all of it: an SEC investigation is not a finding of wrongdoing, and many probes close without any enforcement action. But accounting questions hit at the heart of what investors pay for — trust in the numbers — and until that trust is restored, the stock is likely to stay volatile even after a 48% drop.

Why did the stock fall so far?
