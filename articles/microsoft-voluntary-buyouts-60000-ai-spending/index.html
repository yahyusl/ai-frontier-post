Microsoft offers voluntary buyouts to 60,000 employees as AI spending climbs to $175B

Microsoft is offering voluntary exits to about 60,000 employees — roughly 27% of its workforce — as it holds down labour costs while funding a $175B AI infrastructure buildout. Decisions are due by early December, with departures at year-end.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · October 27, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees — roughly 27% of its workforce — in the US, Canada, the UK and India, as it contains costs while funding a $175B AI infrastructure buildout. No departure target has been disclosed; decisions are due by early December.
  • Eligible pool: about 60,000 employees, roughly 27% of Microsoft's 223,000 full-time staff as of June 30, 2026.
  • Scope: level 70 and below in participating teams, including Copilot, AI experiences, Xbox and Windows and Devices; data-centre, sales and security teams largely excluded.
  • Terms: voluntary and time-limited; standard severance varying by level and tenure, plus career-transition support; exits at year-end.
  • Context: $175B in forecast 2026 capital expenditure, following an April early-retirement round and July cuts of 4,800 jobs.

Microsoft has opened the exit door to more than a quarter of its workforce. On Monday, the company began offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, presenting the move as a new option for people already thinking about leaving — and as a way for its divisions to reshape themselves while the company pours money into AI infrastructure.

The scale is what sets this apart from a routine trim. Sixty thousand is not a headcount target \u2014 Microsoft has not said how many people it expects to go \u2014 but the size of the eligible pool, spread across four countries and including teams that build the AI products at the centre of the spending.

Who is eligible, and who is not#

The offer, called a Voluntary Exit Offer, covers employees in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and India at level 70 and below in Microsoft's internal job ladder — a band that reaches up to some vice-president-level staff. It is time-limited and voluntary: each eligible employee receives an individual offer and decides for themselves.

Teams in scope include AI experiences, Copilot, finance, human resources, Xbox and Windows and Devices. Reporting differs slightly on the edges of the exclusion list: Reuters' account names marketing, commercial business and security as excluded, while other coverage lists data-centre, sales and marketing, and cybersecurity teams among those left out.

Those who accept get severance under Microsoft's standard framework, with payouts varying by job level and length of service, plus career-transition support. Eligible employees have until early December to decide, and approved exits take effect at the end of the year. Chief people officer Amy Coleman told staff the offer gives employees considering a future outside the company “a new choice to make that transition now with support”, and gives participating organisations “another way to adapt their workforce to changing needs”.

The $175B reason#

The timing is not accidental. Microsoft has forecast $175 billion in capital expenditure for calendar 2026, most of it aimed at the data centres and chips its AI products run on. Like its peers, it is spending at a pace that has investors asking, with increasing sharpness, when the returns will justify the bill.

A voluntary programme lets the company answer part of that question without announcing a mass layoff. Analysts read the move as an attempt to reshape the workforce and ease cost pressure through departures people choose, rather than through forced cuts, at the moment the AI buildout needs the most cash. Microsoft's shares rose about 1% on Monday, after gaining roughly 10% over the year to date, according to Reuters reporting — a muted reaction that suggests investors treated the offer as cost management, not a change of strategy.

The first real test of that reading comes quickly: Microsoft was due to report quarterly results on Wednesday, after markets close, giving management its first chance to explain how headcount, costs and capital spending fit together.

A year of shrinking, in stages#

This is the third workforce move of the year, and by far the largest pool:

  • April: an early-retirement programme for about 8,750 US employees whose age and years of service added up to 70 or more. Roughly 30% accepted.
  • July: about 4,800 jobs cut, roughly 2.1% of the global workforce, centred on the Xbox gaming business, which also planned to divest up to five studios.
  • October: voluntary buyouts offered to about 60,000 employees across four countries, with no disclosed target.

That history gives only a rough sense of uptake: if acceptance matched April's roughly 30%, departures would number around 18,000, but this pool is far broader, so April's rate is a guide at best, not a forecast.

There is also a sharper question inside the team list: Copilot and AI-experiences staff are eligible, so some people building the products the spending is meant to advance are being offered the door, while data-centre teams are largely kept. Whether that is a deliberate shift from product organisations to infrastructure, or a broad programme catching teams unevenly, only the acceptance pattern will reveal.

What to watch#

Three things matter more than the headline number. First, the acceptance rate: Microsoft has disclosed no target, so the December decisions — and whether the company ever publishes a total — will show whether this was a release valve or a restructuring by another name. Second, Wednesday's earnings commentary, where any change to capital-spending guidance will say more about the AI bill than the buyout programme does. And third, which teams actually shrink: heavy uptake in Copilot or AI-experiences roles would tell a different story from uptake concentrated in corporate functions.

Beyond Microsoft, the programme is a template worth watching. Every hyperscaler faces the same squeeze — enormous AI infrastructure costs against a payroll built for a different era — and a voluntary route, if it works, is politically and practically easier than a layoff round. The real story here is not 60,000 departures. It is a company choosing to shrink its payroll by invitation rather than by decree, at the exact moment its AI ambitions demand the most capital.
