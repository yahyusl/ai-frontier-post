Canva cuts 1,271 jobs in its first mass layoff as AI costs bite and growth stalls

Canva is cutting or relocating 1,271 roles — about 22% of its workforce — after growth slowed for the first time in a decade. AI is at the centre of the story, but not in the way the headlines suggest: it is the cost and the competitor, not the replacement.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 8, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Canva is cutting or relocating 1,271 roles, about 22% of its workforce, after growth slowed for the first time in a decade. The company cut its 2026 revenue growth forecast from 30% to 20%, partly because of the cost of running AI features, and early investors have marked its valuation down to about $35 billion. Canva says AI is not replacing the departing staff — but AI costs and AI competition are central to why the restructure is happening.
  • Announced: November 3, 2026, in a note to staff from co-founder and CEO Melanie Perkins; reported by TechCrunch on November 4. The first mass layoff in Canva’s history since its founding in 2013.
  • Scale: 1,271 roles affected — cut, changed or relocated — about 22% of a workforce of roughly 5,800. Canva has not said how many are outright exits.
  • The money: revenue growth forecast cut from 30% to 20%, partly on ballooning AI costs; early investors marked the valuation down to about $35 billion, from $42 billion.
  • The package: at least six months’ pay, departing staff keep their MacBooks, and a $10,000 grant to start a business or community project.
  • The restructure: product, growth and platform teams merge into a single product group, work centralises in Sydney, and the new structure takes effect in January 2027.

Canva is cutting 1,271 roles — about 22% of its workforce — in the first mass layoff of its 13-year history. Co-founder and CEO Melanie Perkins told staff on November 3 that growth had slowed for the first time in a decade. The cuts, reported by TechCrunch on November 4, merge the product, growth and platform teams into a single group and centralise more work in Sydney.

The story is being told as an AI layoff. That is the wrong frame. Nothing in Canva’s own account says AI is doing the work of the people leaving. What the reporting shows is a company squeezed by AI on two sides at once: the cost of running AI features for hundreds of millions of mostly free users, and rivals — including the chatbots themselves — giving away the design work Canva used to sell.

What actually happened#

The headline number needs a qualifier. The 1,271 roles are not all job losses: Canva says some will be cut outright, some will change, and some will be asked to relocate to what Perkins called “centralised hubs.” Canva has not said how many fall into each bucket. The reported package: at least six months’ pay including statutory entitlements, departing staff keep their company MacBooks, and a $10,000 grant from the Canva Collective Fund to start a business or community project.

Perkins called it “our hardest year yet.” Canva has around 260 million monthly users and about $3.5 billion in annual revenue, per WebProNews — but growth has stalled for the first time since its 2013 founding. A company-wide meeting is scheduled for November 10, with team-level meetings from November 11; the new structure takes effect in January 2027.

AI is the cost, not the replacement#

The cost side is specific to Canva’s business model. Most of its hundreds of millions of users do not pay, so every AI image or design a free user generates costs the company money. In August, The Information reported Canva had underestimated how heavily its new AI features would be used. Days later it cut its 2026 revenue growth forecast from 30% to 20% — a cut TechCrunch tied to ballooning AI costs — and early investors marked the valuation down to about $35 billion, from $42 billion.

The pricing whiplash shows how hard the company has found the maths. In April, at its Create event, Canva launched Canva AI 2.0 — agents, connectors to Slack, Gmail and Drive, and memory — and began moving from feature-based subscription tiers to a business built around AI credits. By September the free plan’s AI allowance had fallen from 200 standard uses to 20, and Canva Pro’s price rose from $144 to $180 a year before falling back to $144 within weeks.

Canva’s answer is to own more of the stack: it has been shipping its own AI models, including a foundation model called Craft, to cut reliance on outside providers — and it plans to sell Craft inside ChatGPT and Gemini, turning two big competitors into distribution channels.

And AI is the competition too#

The second squeeze is the market itself. TechCrunch notes that Canva faces newer AI design and presentation startups, plus general-purpose chatbots like ChatGPT and Gemini whose image generation lets people put together posters and presentations without opening a design tool at all. That is the demand-side version of the same problem: the thing Canva sells is increasingly available free, inside products people already use. It is a sharp reversal for a company that spent the year betting its future on AI — the April relaunch was supposed to make Canva the place AI-assisted design happens, and instead the AI bill arrived before the AI revenue did, with the planned listing slipping to 2027.

The part the headlines get wrong#

It matters that the framing is accurate, because it changes the lesson. Canva’s memo never mentions AI, and a company spokesperson said the changes are not about “using AI to do the work of people who are leaving.” Oxford Economics found AI accounted for about 4.5% of US job losses in 2025 — a real number, but nowhere near the main driver of restructuring. Canva is not the company that replaced its designers with a model. It is the company that built its business around AI features and then discovered it could not afford to give them away.

That distinction is also why the reaction has been unusually muted. The package — six months’ pay, a laptop, a $10,000 grant with no equity taken — has drawn praise rather than anger. The people paying for the miscalculation are still 1,271 staff, and no severance package changes that. But the story is not “AI took their jobs.” It is “AI cost more than the business model allowed.”

What to watch#

Three things decide whether this is a one-off correction or the start of a longer squeeze. First, the November 10 all-hands and the January 2027 reorganisation — whether the merged product group actually ships faster, or just smaller. Second, Craft: if Canva can sell its own image model inside ChatGPT and Gemini, the cost problem becomes a revenue line. Third, the growth forecast itself — whether 20% holds. And behind all of it, the question every design-software company is now asking: what is left to sell when the chatbot makes the poster for free?

Frequently asked questions

Did AI replace the 1,271 workers?

Nothing in the reporting says so. Canva says the changes are not about using AI to do the work of departing staff. AI shows up in this story as a cost — running AI features for hundreds of millions of mostly free users — and as a competitor, in the form of chatbots that generate designs on request.

How many people are actually losing their jobs?

Canva has not said. The 1,271 figure covers roles that will be cut, changed or relocated, and the company has not broken out how many are outright exits. Departing staff get at least six months’ pay, keep their MacBooks, and can apply for a $10,000 grant to start a business or community project.

What is Canva doing about the AI costs?

It is building its own models, including a foundation model called Craft, to cut its reliance on outside providers, and it has moved toward usage-based pricing — the free plan’s AI allowance fell from 200 to 20 standard uses in September. It also plans to sell Craft inside ChatGPT and Gemini.
