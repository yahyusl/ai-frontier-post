British AI chip startup Fractile raises $720M at a $7B valuation, with UK state backing

Fractile, a London- and Bristol-based startup designing chips for AI inference, has raised $720 million at a $7 billion valuation — with the UK government’s Sovereign AI fund and the British Business Bank in the round. The catch: the company has never shipped a chip, and the state won’t say how much of its own money is in the deal.

A British startup that has never shipped a chip is now worth $7 billion. Fractile, a London- and Bristol-based company designing processors for AI inference, has raised $720 million in a Series C round, Reuters reported on Wednesday — with the UK government's Sovereign AI fund and the British Business Bank among the investors. The government describes the round as worth more than £500 million and the valuation as more than £5 billion; at the exchange rate Reuters cites, the two accounts line up.

The money is real. The chip is not — yet. Fractile plans to launch its first commercial chip next year, and no independent benchmark has tested its performance claims. What investors just bought, in other words, is a thesis: that the bottleneck in AI is no longer training models but running them, and that the hardware built for that job will look very different from today's GPUs.

The round: $720 million at a $7 billion valuation#

The round was led by Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed and Redpoint, Fractile told Reuters. Other investors include Google's venture arm CapitalG, BlackRock, DST Global, Elad Gil, Balderton and SV Angel, alongside the state's Sovereign AI fund and the British Business Bank.

The $7 billion figure comes from a person involved in the deal, speaking to Reuters — a transaction price, not a public-market verdict. It is also a steep climb. Fractile was reported in April to be raising at a $1 billion valuation, closed a $220 million round in May, and six months later the price is $7 billion.

Fractile was founded in the summer of 2022 by Walter Goodwin, who had been doing doctoral research in robotics and AI at Oxford, and emerged from stealth in July 2024 with $15 million in seed funding from backers including Kindred Capital, the NATO Innovation Fund and Oxford Science Enterprises.

What Fractile is actually building#

Fractile designs chips for AI inference — the stage at which a trained model generates responses — rather than for training. Its premise, laid out in the company's own writing, is that the time AI systems take to produce useful outputs will limit their impact, and that today's hardware is the wrong shape for the job. The company says it is building chips and systems that generate thousands of tokens per second while supporting large models at very long contexts, and that limited memory-bandwidth scaling on current architectures is the key constraint.

The work spans the full stack — chips, software and machine-learning research — and the pitch is the familiar trio: faster, more energy-efficient and less costly. The company points to drug discovery, software engineering and materials discovery as the kind of work that gets cheaper when inference gets faster. None of it has been demonstrated on shipping silicon yet, and the company has published no independent benchmarks.

The government's small cheque and big message#

The state is in the round, but its cheque is the one number nobody will disclose. The government's announcement calls the public contributions 'commercially sensitive' and declines to break them out; its own notes to editors say Sovereign AI's typical equity investments run £1 million to £10 million. Against a round worth more than £500 million, the state's cash is most likely a small slice of the total — the value to Fractile is the endorsement, and to the government, the story.

That story is industrial policy, stated plainly. 'For too long, Britain has produced some of the world's best scientific ideas, only to see too much of the technology, industry and wealth they create built elsewhere,' AI Minister Kanishka Narayan said in a statement. 'We are determined to change that.' Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds said the fundraising would boost Britain's ability to make the chips powering AI at home, and pointed to Fractile's plans to expand in Bristol.

The announcement sits inside a wider push. The Budget confirmed a further £140 million for Sovereign AI — £60 million for fund investments and £80 million for a research-and-development procurement scheme — and Sovereign AI is expanding into physical and industrial AI, including robotics and advanced manufacturing. The Chancellor also expanded the British Business Bank's investment capacity by £5 billion, to £30.6 billion, and the government points to up to £2 billion of investment in AI compute infrastructure over the coming years.

The context is Britain's awkward position in chips: home to Arm, whose designs sit in billions of devices, but with a semiconductor manufacturing industry far smaller than those in the US and Asia, as Reuters notes. Fractile is the government's bet that the next wave of chip value can be captured at home rather than watched from the sidelines.

What to watch#

First, the chip. Fractile plans to launch its first commercial chip next year, and everything about the $7 billion valuation rides on it arriving on time and performing as claimed. A slip, or a first benchmark that disappoints, reprices the whole story — and the inference-chip field is crowded, with Nvidia dominant and a queue of challengers all promising the same leap.

Second, the government's follow-through. A small equity cheque buys a press release; the real test is whether Sovereign AI's procurement money and the compute roadmap turn into orders for British hardware. If Fractile's first systems end up sold mostly to overseas buyers, the sovereign-capability story gets harder to tell.

Third, the valuation itself. Seven billion dollars for a company with no shipped product and no disclosed revenue is a bet on a market — inference — that every chipmaker on earth is now chasing. The round says investors believe the inference bottleneck is real and that a new architecture can win it. The chip, when it arrives, will say whether they were right.

What does Fractile do?

It designs chips and systems for AI inference — the stage at which a trained model generates responses — rather than for training. Its bet is that inference is limited by memory bandwidth, not raw compute, and that fusing memory and compute together breaks the bottleneck. The work spans chips, software and machine-learning research.

How much did the UK government actually invest?

It has not said. The government describes the public contributions as commercially sensitive and does not break them out. Its own notes to editors say Sovereign AI’s typical equity investments run £1 million to £10 million, so the state’s cash is most likely a small fraction of a round worth over £500 million.

When will Fractile’s first chip ship?
