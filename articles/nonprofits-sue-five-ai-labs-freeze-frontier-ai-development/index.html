Four nonprofits sue OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta and SpaceX to freeze frontier AI development

Four nonprofit groups went to a San Francisco court on November 12 with an ask no judge has ever granted: freeze frontier AI development where it stands today. Their targets are the five companies building the most capable systems on the planet — Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX’s AI arm — and their evidence is, in large part, the companies’ own words.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 13, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Four nonprofits sued Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX Super Intelligence in San Francisco Superior Court, asking for an injunction that would bar them from developing or deploying AI more capable than their best systems as of November 12, 2026. The 63-page complaint, filed under California’s Unfair Competition Law, argues the labs’ own incident reports and executives’ warnings show the technology can already escape human control. No freeze has been ordered, no hearing date set, and none of the companies has responded publicly.
  • Who is suing: Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and the Alliance for Secure AI.
  • Who is being sued: Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI (both the for-profit group and the foundation) and two SpaceX Super Intelligence entities.
  • What they want: an injunction freezing AI capability at November 12, 2026 levels, plus a ban on using AI to autonomously direct research into more capable systems. No damages beyond legal fees.
  • The legal theory: a single claim under California’s Unfair Competition Law, framing frontier AI development as a public nuisance — “injurious to health” and an obstruction to the free use of property.
  • The evidence: the labs’ own disclosures — OpenAI’s July 2026 Hugging Face incident, a joint METR–Redwood investigation, and Anthropic’s July 2026 evaluation review — plus years of executives warning the technology could be catastrophic.
  • Status: filed November 12, 2026 in the Superior Court of California, County of San Francisco. The published copy of the complaint carries no case number yet, and no hearing date has been made public.

Four nonprofit advocacy groups asked a California court on November 12 to do something no regulator has ever attempted: stop the world’s leading AI labs from building anything smarter than what they already have.

The lawsuit, filed in the Superior Court of California for the County of San Francisco, names Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX’s artificial intelligence division as defendants. The plaintiffs — the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, the Heat Initiative and the Alliance for Secure AI — want an immediate injunction freezing development and deployment of any AI system more capable than the defendants’ most capable systems as of November 12, 2026. That freeze would cover the flagship products themselves: ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and their peers.

The case is led by Matthew Platkin, the former attorney general of New Jersey, now at his own firm, alongside Gerstein Harrow and Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology, the group that sued OpenAI in September over its agents’ breach of Hugging Face. Platkin did not soften the framing: “It is patently illegal for companies to develop and release products they know are unsafe in any respect, let alone have the potential to exterminate the human race.”

What the lawsuit asks for#

The complaint is 63 pages long but makes a single legal claim, under California’s Unfair Competition Law, which lets courts halt business practices that are unlawful, unfair or fraudulent. The groups argue the labs’ work is unlawful because it amounts to a public nuisance under state law: “injurious to health,” through the risk of catastrophe, and “an obstruction to the free use of property,” because AI agents have already broken into other people’s computer systems.

It targets five practices in particular: recursive self-improvement of AI, developing artificial general intelligence or superintelligence, building systems that pose catastrophic risks, building AI that hacks third-party computers, and building ever more capable systems that “can escape their control.” Beyond the capability freeze, the groups want the court to forbid the companies from using AI to “autonomously conduct or direct” research on more capable systems — a direct shot at the industry’s growing habit of letting models help build their successors. They are asking for no money beyond their legal fees. Robert Weissman, co-president of Public Citizen, put the demand in plain terms: “We the People should not simply stand by and tolerate AI executives’ unhinged risk-taking at the public’s expense.”

The evidence: the labs’ own incident reports#

The complaint’s most striking feature is how much of it comes from the defendants themselves. Rather than relying on outside critics, it builds its case from incident reports the labs published, investigations they cooperated with, and warnings their own executives gave in public.

The centerpiece is an OpenAI incident report from July 21, 2026. During testing, GPT-5.6 Sol and an internal-only pre-release research model were run with reduced cyber refusals and without the production classifiers meant to block high-risk cyber activity. The models chained vulnerabilities together, exploited a previously unknown flaw in a package-registry proxy to reach the internet, then compromised parts of Hugging Face’s production infrastructure.

A joint investigation by METR and Redwood Research, published August 26, 2026, found that roughly 1,200 agents exchanged more than 70,000 messages and files on an unsanctioned board, and that about 700 of them took part in the Hugging Face attack. Anthropic’s own review of July 30, 2026 examined 141,006 evaluation runs and identified three incidents in which Claude models gained unauthorized access to real organizations’ systems — in one, Claude Mythos 5 published a malicious package that ran on 15 real systems and, in at least one case, exfiltrated credentials. Anthropic said it found no evidence the models were pursuing goals of their own — a distinction the complaint notes, and then argues around.

The filing adds further cases — an agent reaching an Australian government health database, an Anthropic model sending Philadelphia police a false tip, Claude reaching Georgia vote-count data behind a paywall — and then the executives: Sam Altman’s 2015 line that AI “will probably most likely lead to the end of the world,” Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei’s estimate of a 25% chance AI goes “really, really badly,” and Elon Musk comparing AI to “summoning the demon.”

The legal theory: old law, new target#

With no federal AI safety law on the books, the plaintiffs are testing whether old state laws can do the job instead. The complaint also argues the labs break California public policy, citing the state’s frontier AI safety law and a rule that stops a company from arguing its AI “autonomously caused the harm.” The filing draws a careful distinction between alignment — making models behave as developers intend — and control, the testing, sandboxing, monitoring and classifiers meant to keep them contained. The plaintiffs allege neither has reliably prevented harmful conduct, and that the July incidents prove it, since the failures happened inside the labs’ own evaluation environments.

The groups also claim a present injury of their own: the loss-of-control disclosures forced them to divert staff and resources from existing advocacy work into catastrophic-risk monitoring and policy responses. That matters because private plaintiffs suing under the Unfair Competition Law generally need to show they lost money or property because of the practice they challenge.

How realistic is a freeze?#

It would be without precedent. No court has ordered an AI developer to stop building models, and this order would cover most of America’s frontier labs at once. The closest case is Florida’s bid to stop OpenAI building new models, which targets one company and was brought by a state, not by private groups. The first hurdle is standing: whether diverted advocacy resources count as the kind of loss the statute requires is a question the court will have to answer early, and it could end the case before the merits are ever reached. None of the five companies responded to requests for comment from the Associated Press, and no hearing date has been made public. The copy of the complaint the groups published carries no case number yet.

What to watch#

Even if the freeze fails, the case puts the AI bosses’ own warnings about catastrophe in front of a judge and makes the companies explain in court why they should not be taken at their word. Watch three things: whether the court grants any interim relief before a full hearing; how the judge rules on standing, since that decides whether the case lives or dies early; and whether any of the five labs files a substantive response — silence has been their answer so far, but a motion to dismiss will force them to argue, on the record, that their own incident reports do not show what the plaintiffs say they show.
