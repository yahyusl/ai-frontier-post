arXiv caps every author at two submissions a month as AI-generated spam overwhelms its moderators

arXiv now limits every submitter to two papers per calendar month and three active submissions at a time — the preprint server’s bluntest response yet to AI-generated submission spam. September brought a record 40,363 submissions and nearly 9,000 support tickets.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · September 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The fastest-moving part of AI research just got a speed limit bolted onto it. On October 1, arXiv — the preprint server where almost every serious AI result lands first — capped every author at two submissions per calendar month, with a maximum of three active submissions at any given time. The rule applies across all submitters and all subject categories, and it is already in effect.

It is the most bluntly consequential moderation change in arXiv's 35-year history, and the reason is no secret: AI tools have made it trivially cheap to generate plausible-looking papers, and the server's volunteer moderators are drowning. Last September arXiv received a record 40,363 submissions in a single month — and, crucially, almost 9,000 support tickets from the authors of the papers that didn't make it through.

The new rule#

The announcement, published on the arXiv blog on October 1, is explicit about the mechanics. Two submissions per calendar month per submitter; rejected submissions count against the quota, because it is submissions — not accepted papers — that consume moderator time. Delete a paper before it is announced and it doesn't count, but submit two sloppy drafts in the first week of the month and you're locked out until the next one starts.

A few details matter. The limit applies to submitters, not co-authors: your co-authors' quotas are untouched when you hit submit. The three-active-submissions ceiling was already in place since 2024, but now it stacks on top of the monthly cap — if last month's papers are still sitting in the moderation queue, they eat into your three active slots. And the backlog is the problem the policy is meant to fix, so the queue is exactly where you should expect friction.

ArXiv frames the policy as a stopgap: a breathing-room measure "while we determine what may be the new best practice for authors employing more and more advanced AI tools." That is an honest admission that nobody — including arXiv — has settled where the line sits between a researcher using AI to help write and an author flooding the server with machine-generated filler.

The numbers behind it#

The blog post's submission statistics are striking. In September 2016, arXiv received 9,869 submissions. In September 2024, 20,569. This September, 40,363 — a doubling in two years. The computer science AI category grew more than sixfold over the same period, with other categories doubling. Submissions have climbed every year since ChatGPT's release, and the moderation team is still built the way it always was: unpaid volunteers reading submissions by hand.

Thomas Dietterich, chair of arXiv's editorial advisory council, put the human cost bluntly in the announcement: "a relatively small proportion of authors are submitting a large number of low-quality papers and consuming a disproportionate fraction of the moderators' time. This is unfair to authors who continue to submit quality papers — their papers can be delayed for days or weeks as a result."

What the moderators are seeing, arXiv says, is a taxonomy of spam: thin papers of narrow scope, "salami" papers where one work is sliced into a set of small submissions, and dense AI-written papers that look substantial at a glance. ArXiv's policy already permits AI as a research tool, as long as its use is disclosed and the work meets scholarly standards — but, the post says, much of what is arriving doesn't satisfy either requirement.

How the community reacted#

The news surfaced on r/MachineLearning, where the thread pulled in over a hundred upvotes and the kind of comment-section pile-on that follows any arXiv moderation news. Dietterich took to X with his own one-line explanation: "Because of the exponential increase in submissions, arXiv is limiting the number of papers that a person can submit each month to two." AI researchers shared the announcement across Bluesky as well; one arXiv moderator, Taylor Smith, called it "a necessary move (at least temporarily)."

Not everyone agrees. AI researcher Nathan Lambert wrote on his Substack that arXiv should be heading in the opposite direction — accepting nearly any computer science research and sorting quality later — calling the trend "the early stages of a slow death of arXiv." His prediction: the peer-review requirement arXiv recently attached to review and position papers will within a few years extend to technical papers too. Under today's rules, he noted, he couldn't have posted his own widely-read RLHF book PDF to arXiv.

What it changes for researchers#

For AI labs racing to establish priority on a result, two submissions a month is a real constraint, not a paperwork annoyance. Labs that split big projects across several papers, or push quick follow-ups correcting and extending earlier work, now have to ration which results go up and when. A rejected submission eating into that quota raises the cost of a sloppy first draft considerably — get the category wrong and you've burned half your month for nothing.

This is also the second squeeze in five months. Earlier this year, arXiv introduced what 404 Media described as a one-strike rule: authors caught submitting papers with hallucinated references or leftover LLM instructions face a one-year ban, followed by a requirement that future submissions clear peer review before appearing as preprints. Separately, review and position papers in computer science now need acceptance at a peer-reviewed venue before they can be posted. Each rule attacks the same underlying number — and each one makes the front door of the world's main preprint server a little narrower.

What to watch#

The open question is whether the limit actually reduces the flood, or just redistributes it. Submitters can still coordinate across co-authors — the quota counts per submitter — and determined paper mills can play the calendar. Meanwhile, sister servers are moving the same direction: SocArXiv recently adopted a two-papers-per-week limit of its own, explicitly assuming overflow submissions are LLM-generated.

Watch what happens to CS.AI volume in the next few months. If September's 40,363 was the peak, the cap worked. If the number holds steady, arXiv will have learned something harder: that when the cost of producing a paper falls to near zero, the cost of judging it becomes the entire system — and throttling submitters is only a stopgap, not a fix.
