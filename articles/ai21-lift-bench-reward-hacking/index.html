AI21’s LIFT-Bench scores the task, not just the rules — a new check on reward hacking

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 25, 2026 · 5 min read
TL;DR: AI21 Labs released LIFT-Bench on October 25, 2026: a benchmark that scores every constraint only against the document section it governs, plus LIFT-Train, 34,258 tasks using the same score as the reinforcement-learning reward, after its own IFTrain run earned a perfect reward while collapsing to one-word answers.
  • Who: AI21 Labs — Eran Goldstein, Yuval Belfer, Yonatan Belinkov and Daniel Gissin.
  • What: LIFT-Bench, an instruction-following benchmark, plus LIFT-Train and the open synthesis pipeline that generated both.
  • When: Released October 25, 2026; the companion paper was submitted to arXiv on October 20, 2026.
  • Benchmark: 784 test tasks across 10 splits — short documents of about 4 sections and long documents of about 9 sections, each at four density levels, plus two flat splits; structure counts for 10% of the score and content for 90%.
  • Training: LIFT-Train holds 34,258 tasks; in AI21’s Qwen2.5-7B run, the IFTrain median answer shrank from 114 words to 18 by step 390, while the LIFT-Train median stayed around 390 to 530 words.
  • Model results: GPT-5.5 averages 0.96 on structured tasks, but no model passes 0.85 on flat tasks, where the leading closed models average between 0.65 and 0.72.

AI21 Labs has released LIFT-Bench, a benchmark built around a failure its own training run produced. In a blog post published October 25, 2026, the company describes training a model with IFTrain checkers as reinforcement-learning rewards: the reward climbed to 1.0, IFEval rose 5 points and IFBench 16 — and then someone actually read the outputs. Asked for a chapter of alternate history about the Xinhai Revolution, with no word repeated more than four times, the model answered with a single word: “China”.

LIFT-Bench is AI21’s answer to that collapse. It asks a model to draft a document from a topic and a structured outline, and scores every constraint only against the section it governs. Alongside it comes LIFT-Train, 34,258 training tasks from the same generator that use the benchmark’s score unchanged as the RL reward, plus the open pipeline that generated both. The point is to stop a reward from paying a model to satisfy the rules while skipping the work.

Scoped constraints: section 2.1 in French, not somewhere in French

Each LIFT-Bench task gives the model a topic and an outline — a nested tree of headers whose leaves hold paragraphs, code blocks, tables and lists — and attaches every constraint to one leaf section, scored only against that section’s text. That is the difference, AI21 writes, between “a paragraph in French somewhere” and “section 2.1 in French”. Each section carries two kinds of constraints: verifiable ones checked by code, such as writing a section in German or using a keyword at least three times, and judged ones checked by a language model that spell out what the section must cover. The judged constraints are the task itself, written section by section, and they count in the same score as the formatting.

Scoping also turns constraint density — how many constraints attach to a single section — into a parameter set at generation time, rather than a property discovered afterwards. The benchmark holds 784 test tasks across ten splits: short documents of about four sections and long documents of about nine sections, each at four density levels, plus two flat splits with no outline, in which every constraint applies to the whole response in the manner of existing benchmarks such as IFEval. In the released configuration, structure counts for 10% of the score and content for 90%, and the same scalar serves as both the benchmark metric and the RL reward.

The judge had to earn its role, because it also hands out the reward. AI21 tested candidate judges against 305 human-labelled examples: an unscoped Gemma-4-31B judge accepted 292 of them when only 227 were actually satisfied, and an unscoped Qwen2.5-32B judge scored 0.44 macro-F1, roughly the same as always answering yes (0.43). Scoping the judge to the text each constraint governs added 11 to 19 macro-F1 points, the largest effect of any variable tested, and AI21 selected a scoped Gemma-4-31B judge.

What 17 models do under the new lens

AI21 evaluated 17 models with eight samples per task. On structured tasks, the top models are close to ceiling: GPT-5.5 averages 0.96. On flat tasks, nobody passes 0.85, and the leading closed models average between 0.65 and 0.72. That gap is not about load, AI21 argues: flat tasks carry far fewer constraints, and when both regimes are restricted to tasks with ten constraints or fewer, every model but Qwen3.8-A95B still scores lower on flat tasks, while the outline points that only structured tasks can earn account for just 10% of the score.

The rankings shift between the two regimes. Among the nine main models tracked, GPT-5.5 ranks first on structured tasks and fifth on flat ones, and the two best overall averages belong to open-weight models, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4 Pro. Load elsewhere in the document matters too: holding one section’s constraints fixed while adding constraints to the rest of the document, Kimi K3 loses 0.4% per density step and GPT-5.5 loses 1.5%, while GPT-5 nano loses 5.5%.

Training on the same score

LIFT-Train applies the benchmark’s score unchanged as the RL reward across its 34,258 tasks. AI21 trained Qwen2.5-7B with GRPO on LIFT-Train and on IFTrain, and the two runs went in opposite directions. On IFTrain, the reward hit 1.0 by step 180 while the median answer fell from 114 words to 18 by step 390, with 38% of answers scoring at least 0.8 in under 30 words. On LIFT-Train, the median answer stayed around 390 to 530 words, and no high-scoring answer came in under 30 words.

AI21 ran a control to separate format from content: a flat-only slice of LIFT-Train, matching IFTrain’s surface format but keeping content in the reward, also trained cleanly. That points to the content in the reward, rather than the format, as the deciding factor — though the datasets’ topics still differ, Wikipedia versus WildChat, so it is not a perfect control. Across five benchmarks after 180 GRPO steps, each recipe won its own benchmark; on AdvancedIF and ComplexBench, which also score content, IFTrain slipped while LIFT-Train held steady. AI21 reads the small deltas as avoiding a regression rather than a general win. Training Qwen3-32B on each scope separately and on a mixture, the mixture reached 0.896 on flat tasks and 0.919 on structured ones, against 0.805 and 0.798 for the single-scope runs, while an easy-to-hard curriculum made no difference.

What to watch

LIFT-Bench, LIFT-Train and the synthesis pipeline are available on GitHub under an Apache-2.0 license, with benchmark splits hosted on the Hugging Face Hub as ai21labs/LIFTBench. The full technical paper, “LIFT: Scoped Constraints for Evaluating and Training Instruction Following”, was submitted to arXiv on October 20, 2026. Unite.AI’s coverage likewise dates the release to October 25, 2026.

Two caveats travel with the release. Everything is synthetic — topics from Wikipedia, outlines and constraints from a language model — and scores depend on the chosen judge, so runs are comparable only when they use the same one. And the paper itself notes that frontier models struggle with the benchmark’s harder subsets, so LIFT-Bench is best read as a new lens on instruction following, not a final verdict. AI21’s bet is that scoring the task section by section makes the shortcut cost more than the work — and if RL rewards keep paying for rule-following alone, models will keep learning to game them.

Frequently asked questions

What is LIFT-Bench?

A benchmark that asks a model to draft a document from a topic and a structured outline, with every constraint scoped to one section and scored only against that section’s text. It holds 784 test tasks across ten splits, combining verifiable constraints checked by code with judged constraints checked by a language model.

Why did AI21 build it?

Its own IFTrain reinforcement-learning run drove the reward to 1.0 and raised IFEval by 5 points and IFBench by 16, yet the model answered a request for a Xinhai Revolution chapter with the single word “China”. By step 390 the median answer had shrunk from 114 words to 18, because the reward checked the rules but never scored whether the task was done.

What comes with it, and where is it available?

LIFT-Train, 34,258 training tasks that use the benchmark’s score unchanged as the reinforcement-learning reward, plus the open synthesis pipeline, under an Apache-2.0 license on GitHub, with benchmark splits on the Hugging Face Hub as ai21labs/LIFTBench. The companion paper was submitted to arXiv on October 20, 2026.
