Cainiao opens its warehouses to train embodied AI robots

Cainiao is turning its warehouses into a school for robots. Alibaba’s logistics arm announced on November 6 that it will open its global warehouse network to AI and robotics partners, offering live logistics operations as a training ground for embodied AI — the physical AI systems that have to learn in the real world, not just in simulation.

The pitch is aimed at a bottleneck every robotics company knows: a robot that works in a demo bay often fails on a real warehouse floor, where parcels vary, aisles are crowded and the exceptions never stop. Cainiao says it will give partners exactly that messy reality. Its network covers the full chain from domestic pickup and cross-border line-haul to overseas fulfillment, handling high volumes of multi-SKU shipments — the kind of varied, unpredictable work robots need to learn from.

A training ground, not a lab

Cainiao is opening dozens of industry-specific proof-of-concept scenarios covering warehouse picking, intelligent navigation and delivery, high-altitude inspection, security operations, aviation logistics and auto parts management. Partners get a platform for joint R&D, real-machine training and business validation — in other words, a place to prove a robot can hold a job before trying to sell it.

The company has signed initial cooperation agreements with leading firms in AI computing and embodied intelligence, and nearly 100 representatives from across the industry chain attended the launch event in Hong Kong. No partner names were disclosed.

What robots are already doing there

The partnership is not starting from zero. Cainiao says it has already scaled pilot deployments across express delivery, e-commerce, FMCG and auto parts scenarios, covering three tiers of work: basic tasks such as parcel feeding and labeling, mid-level precision tasks such as small-item sorting, and advanced long-horizon tasks such as palletizing and complex depalletizing. The progression matters: it is the path from single-point operations to robots that can handle a whole shift.

The move lands in a week when physical AI is having a moment. Serve Robotics launched Physical AI Services on November 5, a platform offering connectivity, data curation and a foundation-model catalog to other robotics companies. Cainiao is coming at the same problem from the other side: instead of selling the platform, it is selling access to the physical world the platform needs to learn from. Embodied foundation models are also arriving fast — AGIBOT unveiled its GO-3 Preview model on November 3 — and every one of them needs real-world data to get better.

Global Tech in Three Days

Alongside the partnership, Cainiao unveiled a service aimed at the other end of the robotics business: getting hardware built, shipped and supported worldwide. “Global Tech in Three Days” offers AI computing and robotics companies end-to-end supply chain support spanning prototype testing, scaled delivery and overseas operations, including customs support, forward and reverse logistics, high-value cargo insurance, forward warehousing and spare-parts management.

Global express is designed for on-demand needs such as R&D testing, customer demos and small-batch replenishment; global air freight is tailored for large, high-value shipments including AI infrastructure equipment, control systems and core modules; and the warehouse network supports regional stocking, multi-country warehousing and local delivery.

Why now, and what is missing

“AI has become a new engine of productivity,” said William Xiong, CEO of Cainiao Global. “As AI and embodied intelligence move from the lab into real industry, we want to leverage our global network to help partners accelerate value creation.” He added that the goal is to “make intelligent technologies go global as efficiently and simply as e-commerce parcels.” Cainiao says the next decade could bring efficiency improvements of 10 to 50 times in the sector.

The honest caveats are worth stating. No partner names, investment figures or deployment timelines were disclosed, and the “world’s largest” training cluster claim has no published size or benchmark behind it — it is a statement of intent about a network that is still being opened up. The release also offers no success rates or throughput figures for the pilots. What is concrete is the access itself: a global logistics operator handing robotics companies the keys to its working floors. For a field that has spent years arguing that simulation is not enough, that is the news.
