Accelya launches FLX Recover: agentic AI that coordinates airline disruption recovery without ripping out the PSS

Accelya has launched FLX Recover, an agentic-AI platform that rebooks and looks after disrupted airline passengers from the first delay signal through to resolution — announced on Tuesday at the World Aviation Festival in Lisbon. The twist: it works inside the systems airlines already run, rather than asking them to rip anything out.

MD Marcus Doyle, Senior Technology Writer · October 13, 2026 · 4 min read

The problem it targets is enormous. In August 2026 alone, Cirium counted almost 60,000 flight cancellations worldwide, while European air-traffic-flow delays exceeded four million minutes, according to EUROCONTROL. Every one of those is a chain of decisions — operations, commercial teams, customer service — often made across systems that were never designed to work together.

What the agents actually do

FLX Recover runs on FLX AIViator, Accelya’s AI intelligence layer, with specialized agents that assess the impact of a disruption, rank passengers by priority, evaluate multiple recovery options, and coordinate the actions that follow — reaccommodation, passenger care, communications, and downstream financial workflows. If a hub departure is cancelled, the agents can work through affected passengers in parallel, weighing available flights, onward connections, interline alternatives, cost, care and assistance options, loyalty value, and the airline’s own commercial priorities. Depending on policies set by the airline, the system can recommend an action for human approval or execute it automatically — with actions logged and reviewable, and the airline defining the boundaries within which the agents operate.

Open by design, built on Bedrock

The product is PSS-agnostic: it can run as part of Accelya’s FLX ONE platform or plug into an airline’s existing passenger service systems, order management, CRM, communications, and operational applications through open interfaces. That’s the pitch to airlines still paying down decades of IT — agentic AI as a layer, not a rip-and-replace.

The infrastructure is Amazon’s. FLX Recover is built on AWS using Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and was engineered with RapidScale, an AWS Premier Services Partner, drawing on Accelya’s work with AWS and Vista Equity Partners’ Agentic Factory. Bedrock AgentCore supplies the identity and observability plumbing that matters when agents move from recommending to acting across enterprise systems.

Governance is doing real work in this pitch. The announcement stresses that airlines decide which scenarios can be automated and which require human review, with every action logged and reviewable — the kind of control layer enterprises have been asking for as agents move from suggestions to system-level actions. Whether those boundaries hold under the pressure of a full hub shutdown is the question no launch demo can answer.

The journey, not just the seat

Accelya is framing this as passenger-first rather than seat-first. The system looks beyond the next available seat to reconstruct the wider journey — onward connections, loyalty status, group and family travel, special assistance requirements, seats, baggage, ancillary purchases, and compensation obligations — alongside the operational and commercial impact of each option. That matters as airlines move toward Offers and Orders, where a personalized order, not just a ticket, has to be recovered when plans change.

Available now

“Disruption is a critical moment in an airline’s relationship with its customers,” said Sam Gilliland, Accelya’s chief executive, in the announcement. “The challenge isn’t simply finding a recovery option; it’s making the right decision quickly and consistently across thousands of passenger circumstances, then carrying it through.”

FLX Recover is available now from Accelya and through AWS Marketplace, and is being showcased at the World Aviation Festival through October 15. Accelya says it powers more than 200 airlines, processes over 9 trillion offers a year, and settles more than $100 billion annually — scale that gives this launch a plausible runway, provided airlines are willing to hand their disruption playbooks to agents.
