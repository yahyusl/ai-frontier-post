Alphabet misses for the first time in nearly four years as AI spending burns $9.1B in cash — while Cloud doubles and Gemini hits 1 billion users

Alphabet grew revenue 25% and doubled its cloud business, and the stock still fell more than 12% in a day. The quarter exposed the tension at the heart of the AI buildout: growth is real, but so is the bill.

MDMarcus DoyleSenior Technology Writer · October 29, 2026 · 7 min read
TL;DR: Alphabet grew revenue 25% and doubled its cloud business, yet shares fell 12.6% on Thursday after its first revenue-and-profit miss in nearly four years. A paper loss on equity holdings dented reported profit, but the deeper worry is cash: $51.4 billion of capital spending left free cash flow at negative $9.1 billion.
  • Revenue: $127.58 billion, up 25%, against $128.22 billion expected; adjusted earnings of $2.67 per share against $3.02 expected.
  • Reported profit fell: net income of $26.2 billion and earnings of $2.12 per share, after an $8.66 billion loss on equity securities, driven primarily by an unrealized loss on the SpaceX stake.
  • Cash burn: $51.36 billion of capital spending left free cash flow at negative $9.07 billion, the second straight negative quarter; the 2026 spending guide of $195–$205 billion stands, with 2027 set to be significantly higher.
  • Cloud doubled: Google Cloud revenue rose 103% to $30.71 billion, with operating income of $11.12 billion; the Gemini app passed one billion users and model APIs now handle nearly 32 billion tokens a minute.
  • Search paid less: search revenue grew 12% to $63.35 billion, below the $64.96 billion expected, with lower monetization in AI Mode cited on the earnings call.

Alphabet just delivered one of the strongest operating quarters in its history, and the market punished it anyway. Revenue rose 25% to $127.6 billion, operating income climbed 30%, and Google Cloud more than doubled. Yet the shares fell more than 10% in after-hours trading on Wednesday and closed Thursday down 12.6%, at $283.82 for the Class A shares, according to CNBC market data. It was, by multiple accounts, the company's first miss on both revenue and profit in nearly four years.

The contradiction is the story. Almost everything about Alphabet's underlying business got better in the third quarter — except the two things investors were watching most closely: how much cash the AI buildout consumes, and whether AI-flavoured search earns as much as the search it replaces. On both counts, the quarter gave them reasons to worry.

The miss, by the numbers#

The headline figures, for the quarter ended September 30, 2026, show a company growing fast and still falling short of a very high bar:

Operating income rose 30% to $40.6 billion, a 31.8% margin. Net income, by contrast, fell to $26.2 billion from $35.0 billion a year earlier. The gap between those two results is where the quarter's real drama sits.

A paper loss did most of the damage#

The single biggest dent in reported profit came from outside Alphabet's operating businesses. The company booked an $8.66 billion loss on equity securities — the stakes it holds in other companies — which cut net income by $6.7 billion after tax, or $0.55 per share. That is the difference between the reported $2.12 per share and the adjusted $2.67 figure analysts compare against consensus.

Chief financial officer Anat Ashkenazi told the earnings call the loss came primarily from an unrealized loss on Alphabet's investment in SpaceX after a decline in that company's share price. Unrealized matters here: Alphabet still owns the stake, so this is a valuation swing on paper rather than cash walking out the door. But it counts in reported earnings, and it turned what would have been a narrow miss into a conspicuous one. A year earlier, the same line had been a $12.8 billion gain — a reminder of how much noise these holdings now inject into Alphabet's bottom line.

The bill for the AI buildout#

Strip out the paper loss, and the deeper investor concern remains: the sheer cost of staying in the AI race. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on property and equipment in the quarter, more than double the $24.0 billion of a year earlier, and its free cash flow came in at negative $9.1 billion. That is the second consecutive quarter of negative free cash flow, after negative $5.9 billion in the second quarter.

Management did not signal relief. Alphabet kept its 2026 capital spending forecast of $195 billion to $205 billion, and Ashkenazi said the company continues to expect spending to increase significantly in 2027, with details to come on the fourth-quarter call. CNBC also noted that long-term debt has risen to $125.1 billion from $21.6 billion a year earlier. None of this suggests distress — Alphabet remains enormously profitable — but it does reframe the AI boom as a capital-intensive grind rather than a windfall, and markets repriced accordingly.

The quarter's real winner: Cloud#

Against that backdrop, Google Cloud was the exception that proved the demand is real. Revenue grew 103% to $30.7 billion, comfortably ahead of expectations, and cloud operating income more than tripled to $11.12 billion from $3.59 billion a year earlier. Alphabet said sales of its TPU systems were a meaningful contributor, and demand is strong enough that Google is renting some capacity from third parties to keep up.

The consumer AI story was just as striking. Chief executive Sundar Pichai said the Gemini app has passed one billion users, which the company describes as the fastest-growing product in its history. Its first-party model APIs now process nearly 32 billion tokens per minute, more than four times the year-ago rate, and Pichai said search query growth is the highest it has been in five years. The same day, Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, its newest frontier model, aimed first at cyber defenders. Whatever the market's verdict on the quarter, usage of Alphabet's AI products is not the problem.

Search is growing — but paying less#

The uncomfortable question is monetization. Search revenue grew 12% to $63.35 billion, but that fell short of the $64.96 billion analysts expected, and YouTube advertising rose 9% to $11.2 billion. On the earnings call, chief business officer Philipp Schindler pointed to lower monetization in AI Mode searches as one reason search grew more slowly than expected. Currency moves and softer retail and media spending also weighed on the result, according to coverage of the call.

Google's argument is that this is a timing problem, not a structural one: most AI Mode queries are incremental, Schindler said, creating more monetization opportunities over time. The company is acting on that logic — on Wednesday it expanded AI Mode advertising to 10 more countries. Whether AI answers can eventually earn like classic search results is now one of the most important open questions in the company's story, because it determines whether surging query growth translates into surging revenue.

What to watch#

Three things will decide whether this selloff looks like an overreaction or an early warning. First, the 2027 capital spending number Alphabet promised for its fourth-quarter call — the market will read it as a statement of how long the cash-burn phase lasts. Second, whether AI Mode monetization starts closing the gap with traditional search in the coming quarters, or whether cheaper AI answers permanently dilute the company's most profitable product. Third, whether Cloud can sustain triple-digit growth as capacity expands, and whether the equity portfolio stops swinging reported earnings by billions in either direction. The AI business inside Alphabet has never looked stronger. This quarter showed that strength alone is no longer enough.
