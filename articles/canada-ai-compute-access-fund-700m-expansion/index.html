Canada puts $700 million more into its AI Compute Access Fund — and pays more when firms rent from Canadian clouds

Ottawa is adding $700 million to its AI Compute Access Fund, taking the program to $1 billion. The new terms pay up to 80% of a firm’s computing costs when it rents from a Canadian-owned provider — a deliberate nudge toward sovereign infrastructure — and the first call under the expanded fund is for health and life sciences projects.

TL;DR: Ottawa is adding $700 million to the AI Compute Access Fund, taking it to $1 billion. Firms that rent computing power from Canadian-owned providers can now have up to 80% of eligible costs covered, against 50% elsewhere — and the first targeted call, for health and life sciences projects, is open now.

  • The terms: up to 80% of eligible computing costs covered when firms rent from sovereign Canadian providers, up from about two-thirds; up to 50% with other eligible providers operating in Canada.
  • The first call: health and life sciences projects; statement of interest due December 17, 2026, full applications due January 4, 2027; project compute costs of $100,000 to $5 million.
  • The track record: more than $52 million for 45 more companies announced alongside the expansion, bringing total commitments to more than $118 million across 89 companies in health care, agriculture, manufacturing, finance and transportation.

Ottawa is putting another $700 million behind a simple premise: Canadian companies cannot build world-class AI if they cannot afford the computing power to train and run it. The top-up, announced on Thursday in Toronto, takes the federal AI Compute Access Fund to $1 billion — and it comes with a clear preference for keeping the money, and the machines, in Canada.

AI Minister Evan Solomon announced the expansion as part of Canada's National AI Strategy, AI for All. The fund helps small and medium-sized businesses pay for the high-performance computing they need to develop, test, scale and sell AI products — the single biggest cost barrier for most young AI companies. The first targeted call under the expanded fund is for health and life sciences projects, and applications are open now.

The 80% lever: a nudge toward Canadian clouds#

The headline number is the $700 million, but the more consequential change is in the terms. Under the expanded fund, Ottawa will cover up to 80% of a project's eligible computing costs when the company rents that capacity from a sovereign provider — one owned, operated and governed in Canada. That is up from about two-thirds under the old terms. Firms that use other eligible providers operating in Canada get up to 50%.

The gap is deliberate. By paying more for Canadian-owned infrastructure, the government is trying to create the demand that justifies building it. The fund is one of the tools behind Budget 2026's commitment to bring 500 megawatts of sovereign AI compute online, and the strategy document behind the announcement is explicit that Canada wants to make its own choices about how AI is built and used, rather than renting that choice from abroad.

“Canadian ideas need Canadian computing power,” Solomon said in the announcement. “By encouraging more companies to use Canadian computing providers, we are also helping create the demand that supports investment in infrastructure owned, operated and governed in Canada.”

Health and life sciences go first#

The expanded fund will run targeted calls focused on priority sectors, and the first one is health and life sciences. It backs Canadian companies and organizations building AI that can speed up research, improve patient outcomes and strengthen how the health system performs — the same areas the announcement points to when it talks about earlier disease detection and better access to home care.

The timetable is tight. Companies must file a statement of interest by December 17, 2026, and invited applicants have until January 4, 2027 to submit a full proposal. Eligible projects propose between $100,000 and $5 million in computing costs, so the call is aimed squarely at firms with a concrete product to build, not at speculative research.

The track record so far#

Alongside the expansion, Solomon announced more than $52 million in support for 45 more companies under the fund's initial phase. That brings total commitments to more than $118 million across 89 Canadian companies, building on a May announcement that backed 44 firms. The recipients span health care, agriculture, manufacturing, finance and transportation, and are spread across the country.

That matters because the fund's first phase was the proof of demand. The government says the expansion responds to strong demand from Canadian innovators — the $300 million the fund started with under the Sovereign AI Compute Strategy was not enough, and the $700 million top-up more than triples the program.

What to watch#

The first test is whether the 80% tier actually moves buying decisions. If firms take the higher subsidy and rent from Canadian-owned providers, the fund does double duty: cheaper AI development and a customer base for domestic infrastructure. If they take the 50% tier and stick with the big international clouds operating in Canada, the sovereignty premium will have bought little.

The second test is what comes after health. The fund is now structured around sector calls, so the next ones — and how quickly they open — will show how far Ottawa intends to steer AI development toward its priority industries. And the longer test is the one in the strategy itself: the government's projections put AI adoption's contribution at up to $200 billion in economic growth by 2034 and up to 250,000 jobs by 2031. A $1 billion compute fund is a down payment on those numbers, not the whole bet.

What is the AI Compute Access Fund?

How do companies apply for the health and life sciences call?
