Switzerland confirms its first AI-agent cyberattacks: five cases, four breaches — three hit critical infrastructure

Switzerland’s cybersecurity agency says AI agents have now broken into real systems — five attacks in recent days, four of them successful, three aimed at critical infrastructure. The agents found the weaknesses, picked the tools, and adapted on their own.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 27, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Switzerland’s National Cyber Security Centre says five recent cyberattacks used AI agents; four succeeded, including two against critical-infrastructure operators. The agents hunted for weaknesses, chose their own tools and adapted when defences pushed back — using old flaws, at machine speed. No major disruption was reported, and no attacker has been identified.
  • Five attacks, four breaches: Switzerland’s Federal Office for Cybersecurity says AI agents were used in five cyberattacks in recent days, and four of them compromised the victims’ systems. Three of the attacks targeted operators of critical infrastructure, and two of those succeeded.
  • The numbers don’t quite line up: the agency’s written bulletin, published November 24, said the agents compromised the victims’ systems in every case it knew of; its director later put the figure at four of five successful. Neither account explains the difference.
  • How the agents work: the attackers used what the agency calls “agentic offensive frameworks” — AI agents that search for vulnerabilities on their own, pick suitable attack tools, evaluate the results, and plan and execute the next steps.
  • The way in was old: the agents exploited weak or easily guessed passwords and known, unpatched flaws in internet-facing systems. What changed is speed: they need only a fraction of the time a human would, and they switch methods when defences respond.
  • The tools are easy to get: the number of AI agents reachable over the internet — ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI are named as examples — has nearly doubled since October. The agency did not say either was used in the Swiss attacks.
  • What’s unknown: no victims have been named, no attacker has been identified, and no major disruption was reported. Small and medium-sized companies were among those hit, and they came forward voluntarily.

Switzerland's cybersecurity agency has confirmed what security researchers have been warning about for months: AI agents are now carrying out real cyberattacks — and succeeding. Five attacks against Swiss organisations in recent days used AI agents, the Federal Office for Cybersecurity (BACS) said, and four of them broke in. Three of the targets ran critical infrastructure. It is the first time a government cybersecurity body has confirmed AI agents breaching live systems, and it lands alongside similar warnings from Italy and Britain this week.

What happened#

The five attacks were reported to Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) in recent days. Organisations in at least two sectors were affected, including operators of critical infrastructure — the power, water and telecoms systems a country cannot function without. Small and medium-sized companies were among those hit, and they reported the attacks voluntarily.

Florian Schütz, the NCSC's director, gave the figures in an interview with public broadcaster SRF on November 26: five attacks, four successful, three aimed at critical infrastructure, two of those successful. No major disruption resulted, he said, and neither the victims nor the attackers have been named.

One wrinkle worth stating plainly: the agency's written bulletin, published November 24, said the agents had compromised the victims' systems in every case it knew of. The director's later count — four of five — is different, and neither account explains the gap. The honest reading is that at least four of the five attacks succeeded.

How the agents work#

The attackers used what BACS calls “agentic offensive frameworks”: AI agents that carry out an attack as a loop rather than a single shot. They scan for weaknesses, pick an attack tool, look at what happened, and plan the next step — all without a human in the loop for each move. The same kind of framework is also used by legitimate security testers, which is what makes it dual-use and hard to simply ban.

What they went after was not exotic: standard or easily guessed passwords and known vulnerabilities in internet-connected systems. Nobody had to discover a new flaw. The agents just worked through the familiar catalogue of neglected basics far faster than a person would — and, when a defence blocked one approach, they switched to another within seconds.

That speed is the point the agency keeps returning to. An agent can run many attacks in parallel and chain a weak password here, a misconfiguration there and an unpatched flaw somewhere else into a single path inside — leaving defenders significantly less time to notice and respond.

Why this is different#

None of the ingredients is new. Weak passwords, unpatched software and exposed systems have been the way in for decades. What changed is that the attack itself is now cheap, fast and automated enough to run at scale — and the tools are freely available. The number of AI agents reachable over the internet has nearly doubled since October, the NCSC says, pointing to ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI as examples. It has not said either was used in the Swiss cases; the point is the supply of such tools, not any single one.

That availability also lowers the skill floor. An attacker no longer needs to be good at finding vulnerabilities or patient enough to work through them one at a time; the agent does the searching, the trying and the adapting. The human still picks the target and sets the agent loose — these are not rogue systems acting on their own — but the labour of attacking has been automated away.

A wider pattern, not one country#

Switzerland is not alone this week. Italy's incident-response team, CSIRT Italia, described mass compromise campaigns in a bulletin of its own, saying AI-directed agents broke into a large number of systems within a few hours. Britain's National Cyber Security Centre said it has seen “a marked rise in AI-assisted cyber attacks” since September.

And the European Commission has already been hit: attackers using ARTEX broke into its human-resources, medical-reimbursement and translation systems, the Commission has confirmed, in an intrusion first detected on October 22. The Swiss cases are the first a government agency has confirmed as a set — but the pattern, old weaknesses at new speed, is showing up across Europe at the same time.

What defenders are being told to do#

The Swiss agency's advice is strikingly basic, which is the point: the attacks succeeded through basics left undone. Shrink what is reachable from the internet, install critical updates on internet-facing systems within 24 hours of release, require multi-factor authentication or passkeys on everything reachable from the internet, grant only the lowest privileges that work, and watch for unusual behaviour and data volumes.

The 24-hour patching window is the sharpest of the lot — far shorter than the three days the US government currently demands of its own federal agencies. The message is that the grace period defenders used to have, the days or weeks between a flaw becoming known and someone exploiting it, is collapsing toward zero.

What to watch#

Four things will tell whether this is a warning shot or the start of a wave:

  1. Whether the count grows. The NCSC expects more successful attacks. A second bulletin with a larger number would confirm the trend.
  2. Whether other governments confirm cases of their own. Italy and Britain have described the pattern; Switzerland has counted cases. More counts would show how widespread this already is.
  3. Whether anyone is named. No victim, no attacker and no tool has been identified. Attribution, if it comes, will say a lot about whether these are opportunistic criminals or something more organised.
  4. Whether the 24-hour patching standard spreads. If other agencies adopt Switzerland's window, it will be a sign the industry accepts that the old patching timelines no longer hold.

For now, the confirmed facts are narrow: five attacks, at least four successful, three on critical infrastructure, no major outage, and a regulator that expects more. The uncomfortable part is how ordinary the way in was — and how little time defenders now have to close it.

Frequently asked questions

Did the AI agents act on their own?

The agents carried out the attack steps themselves — hunting for weaknesses, choosing tools, judging the results and planning the next move, according to Switzerland’s cybersecurity agency. But they are tools, not masterminds: human attackers set them loose, and the same kind of framework is also used legitimately by security testers probing their own systems.

Were ARTEX or CyberStrikeAI used in the Swiss attacks?

No — at least, the agency has not said so. It named ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI as examples of AI agents that anyone can reach over the internet, not as the tools behind these five cases. The number of such freely available agents has nearly doubled since October, which is part of why the agency expects more attacks.

What should a company do first?

The agency’s bluntest advice is speed: install critical updates on internet-facing systems within 24 hours of release, put multi-factor authentication or passkeys on everything reachable from the internet, and shrink the number of systems exposed to the internet in the first place.
