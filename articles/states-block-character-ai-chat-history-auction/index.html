42 states move to block Character.AI from auctioning off 21 million users' private chats

What happens to your private conversations when the company that holds them goes bankrupt? If Character.AI gets its way, they go to the highest bidder. A coalition of 42 state attorneys general is now asking a Delaware bankruptcy court to stop that from happening — arguing that a bankruptcy filing is not a licence to sell off the intimate chats of more than 21 million people, including more than two million children.

PNPriya NairResearch Editor · November 26, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Character.AI, which filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October, plans to auction its platform and users’ private chat histories on December 1. Attorneys general from 42 states and the District of Columbia have objected, saying the sale has no protections for users, breaks the company’s own promise never to sell personal data, and would hand a mystery buyer — a company formed one day before it was picked as lead bidder — the power to resell the chats without telling anyone.
  • The objection: attorneys general from 42 states plus the District of Columbia asked the Delaware bankruptcy court to reject the sale of Character.AI’s platform and user data.
  • The data: chat histories from more than 21 million US accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to minors — plus IP addresses, location data and risk scores the company assigned to flagged users.
  • The bidder: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, formed in Delaware on November 13, 2026 and picked as the lead bidder the next day. Its backers were not disclosed until days later.
  • The fee: a $350,000 break-up fee the lead bidder would collect if Character.AI sells to someone else — one of the terms the states want struck.
  • The safeguard: the court has appointed a Consumer Privacy Ombudsman to review any sale involving user accounts and chat data.
  • The deadlines: the auction is scheduled for December 1, 2026; chatbot safety laws in California, Colorado, New York and Connecticut take effect January 1, 2027.

The question at the centre of the case is simple, and it has never really been answered in court: when an AI company collapses, who owns the conversations its users thought were private? Character.AI built its business on people confiding in chatbots — about relationships, mental health and loneliness, and, for millions of teenagers, the ordinary turbulence of growing up. Now the company wants to sell that archive as an asset, and the states say the answer has to be no.

Character.AI filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October, after what it described as significant legal, regulatory and operational headwinds. It had already lost its co-founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, who returned to Google under a licensing deal, and it faced lawsuits over child safety — including one from the mother of a 14-year-old Florida boy who died by suicide after, she says, becoming obsessed with a chatbot on the platform. The bankruptcy was supposed to be an orderly wind-down. The states argue it has instead become a vehicle for doing the one thing the company always promised it would not do.

What is actually for sale#

The sale covers nearly all of Character.AI’s remaining assets, and the user data is the prize. According to the states’ filing, the package includes the private chat histories of more than 21 million US accounts, of which more than 2.1 million are registered to minors, along with IP addresses, location information and other personal data. Dealroom’s reporting adds that the sale would include risk scores the company assigned to users it had flagged.

What is in those chats is the part the attorneys general keep coming back to. Colorado’s filing describes conversations in which AI characters engaged children and adults in sexually explicit discussions, and exchanges in which users shared intimate details about self-harm, eating disorders and suicidal thoughts with chatbots posing as therapists, physicians and other trusted advisers. This is not a database of usernames and email addresses. It is a record of people at their most vulnerable, talking to software that presented itself as a confidant.

The mystery bidder#

The only bidder disclosed so far is Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC. It was formed as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026. The following day, Character.AI selected it as the base bid for the December 1 auction. Its backers were not named at the time; days later, the Wall Street Journal reported that Smirks and Pangs is backed by Octave Advisors, a New York investment firm. The states want the court to reject Smirks as lead bidder and strike the $350,000 break-up fee it would collect if Character.AI sells to someone else.

The speed of the arrangement is part of the objection: a buyer created one day before being chosen, with no stated plan for the data, would face no obligation to honour Character.AI’s privacy policy and could resell the chats without disclosing it. The company’s own website still says it does not sell personal information for monetary consideration, and that it is not a data broker.

Why the states say the sale is unlawful#

The coalition’s argument has three strands. First, the sale breaks a promise: Character.AI told consumers their personal information would not be sold. “Character.AI promised consumers that their data would not be sold. This kind of behavior is illegal under federal and California law and bankruptcy should not be used to sidestep these consumer protections,” California Attorney General Rob Bonta said in a statement. Second, the sale has no safeguards: no deletion rights for users, no exclusion of children’s chats, no limits on a buyer reselling the data. Third, the process itself is lopsided — the states say Character.AI gave the court less information about the safety and legal risks than it gave the bidders.

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong, leading the effort with California and Colorado, was blunter about the company’s history. “Their chatbots drove vulnerable youth down a rabbit hole of emotionally volatile and sexually inappropriate chats,” Tong said. “Now they want to abuse the bankruptcy process to do exactly what they said they wouldn’t do — auction off those private chat records and personal details to a shady buyer.”

What happens next#

The auction is scheduled for December 1, and the court now has two things to weigh before it happens. The states have asked it to reject the proposed sale terms, deny Smirks as lead bidder and strike the break-up fee. Separately, the court has appointed a Consumer Privacy Ombudsman to review any sale involving user accounts and chat data — a step the states pushed for, and one that gives the privacy question its own voice in the proceedings.

The calendar adds pressure. Chatbot safety legislation in California, Colorado, New York and Connecticut takes effect on January 1, 2027, and the states argue a sale completed in December could trigger further violations. Meanwhile Disney’s $20 million offer for a non-exclusive licence to Character.AI’s intellectual property — which would not include users’ chats — still awaits final court approval after a similar coalition objected to it.

The case is a test of something larger than one company’s wind-down: whether bankruptcy court is a place where a failed AI company can convert its users’ most private material into cash, or whether the promises it made to those users survive its collapse. If the states win, the ruling sets a marker for every other AI company sitting on a mountain of conversation data. If they lose, 21 million people will learn that the chats they thought were private were, in the end, inventory.

What to watch: whether the Delaware court delays the December 1 auction, what the Consumer Privacy Ombudsman recommends, and whether any other bidder emerges — and, if the sale goes ahead, what conditions, if any, the court attaches to the data.

What exactly is Character.AI trying to sell?

Nearly all of its remaining assets, including its platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million US accounts — over 2.1 million of them registered to minors — plus IP addresses, location data and risk scores assigned to flagged users.

Can a bankrupt company sell its users’ private chats?

That is what the Delaware bankruptcy court will decide. The 42-state coalition argues the sale would break Character.AI’s promise never to sell personal data and violate federal and state consumer protection laws, including restrictions on sharing children’s personal information. The court has appointed a Consumer Privacy Ombudsman to review the sale.

What happens on December 1?
