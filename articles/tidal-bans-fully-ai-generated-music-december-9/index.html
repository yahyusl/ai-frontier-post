Tidal bans fully AI-generated music: 29 million tracks to be deleted from December 9

Tidal will start deleting wholly AI-generated music from its catalog on December 9, after finding that 43% of the tracks delivered to it over the past 90 days were made entirely by AI — a flood its June labeling policy failed to hold back.

SASofia AlmeidaTutorials & Reviews Lead · November 24, 2026 · 6 min read
TL;DR: Tidal will reject and remove any wholly AI-generated track from December 9, deleting about 29 million songs by the end of the year. The move follows a flood in which 43% of recent deliveries were fully AI-made — 94% of them from Suno — while 96% of the AI-tagged tracks in its catalog have never been streamed. Music made with AI assistance, rather than generated wholesale, stays.

  • The line: AI-assisted music stays. Only tracks generated entirely by AI go — an escalation of the June policy that merely labeled and demonetized them.

Tidal is done tagging AI music and hoping the problem goes away. The streaming service announced on Tuesday that it will begin rejecting and removing wholly AI-generated tracks on December 9 — deleting roughly 29 million songs from its catalog by the end of the year and turning away an estimated 80,000 to 85,000 new AI-generated submissions every day.

It is the hardest line any major streaming service has taken against AI-generated music, and Tidal is framing it as a rescue operation for the human artists its listeners actually come to hear.

What changes on December 9#

From December 9, any wholly AI-generated track submitted to Tidal will be rejected and removed, the company said in a post on X. The purge covers the existing catalog too: tracks already on the service that Tidal determines were generated entirely by AI tools will be taken down, and Tidal says they will be completely gone by the beginning of the year.

The ban reaches upstream as well. Distributors will be barred from delivering wholly AI-generated tracks to Tidal at all, and the company says it “will reject deliveries if their metadata says they were generated solely using AI tools.” What survives is music in which AI played a supporting role: tracks “assisted by AI tools, but not the sole outputs, will be allowed on the platform.”

The numbers that forced the move#

Tidal published the figures that pushed it over the edge, and they are stark. Over the past 90 days, 43% of all tracks distributed to the service were detected as wholly AI-generated. On November 6, the company recorded its biggest single-day influx yet: more than 60% of the deliveries it received that day were fully AI-made.

The catalog already carries the evidence. About 27 million tracks — 6.3% of everything available on Tidal — are tagged as AI-generated, and 96% of them have received zero streams in the last three months. Tidal attributes 94% of the AI-badged tracks to Suno, the AI music generator.

That combination — enormous volume, almost no listening — is the core of Tidal's argument. “It's true that not all songs catch on immediately, and artists should have time to build their fanbases,” the company said. “But this situation is different — at this scale, the authentic music and artists that Tidal subscribers are looking to listen to and discover get buried.”

Impersonation is the sharper edge of the same problem. Tidal says it has already taken down more than 65,000 AI-generated tracks over suspected impersonation of artist names — recordings that can divert streams and royalties away from real artists while confusing their fans.

How Tidal plans to tell the difference#

Enforcement rests on two checks: audio analysis and metadata. The audio side comes from Ircam Amplify, the commercial arm of the French audio research institute IRCAM, which Tidal named as its AI detection partner. Ircam Amplify says its detector can analyze more than 250,000 tracks an hour and claims a false-positive rate below 0.02%.

That false-positive figure matters more than it looks. As Ircam Amplify chief executive Nathalie Birocheau put it, “when a streaming service tells listeners that a track is AI-generated, it puts its reputation behind that decision” — a wrong call can cast doubt on a real artist's work. Tidal's second check, distributor metadata, is meant to catch what the audio analysis misses: if a delivery declares itself wholly AI-generated, it is rejected on the paperwork alone.

The industry's split response#

Tidal is not the first platform to draw a line, but it is now the furthest any major streamer has gone. Bandcamp banned music created “wholly or in substantial part by AI” in January. Deezer, which has reported that fully AI-generated tracks account for more than 44% of its daily deliveries — nearly 90,000 a day in June — has kept the music but said it will let users filter it out of search results, and takes down tracks used to inflate streams fraudulently.

The biggest services have chosen the gentler path. Spotify and YouTube Music label AI-generated music rather than removing it. Tidal tried that first: its June policy tagged wholly AI-generated tracks in the app and blocked them from earning royalties. Five months later, the company concluded the softer approach had failed — the flood kept growing, and demonetized tracks still buried human artists in search and discovery.

What to watch#

Three things decide whether this ban works. The first is execution on December 9: deleting 29 million tracks without sweeping up human artists whose work trips the detector. The second is Suno, the source of 94% of the flagged tracks, and whether its users route around the ban through other distributors. The third is whether the bigger platforms follow — Spotify and YouTube Music have so far treated labeling as enough, and Tidal's numbers are an argument that it is not.

Tidal closed its announcement with a promise to keep publishing what it finds. “Tidal will always fight for artists to get paid more,” the company said. “Everything in this update comes back to that.” The test of the policy starts in two weeks.

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is Tidal banning?

Any track Tidal determines was generated wholly by AI tools — a recording with no human performance, writing, or production behind it. The ban covers new submissions and the existing catalog, and it also bars distributors from delivering wholly AI-generated tracks to the service.

Will music made with AI assistance still be allowed?

Yes. Tracks that were assisted by AI tools but are not solely AI outputs stay on the platform. The line Tidal is drawing is between AI as a production tool and AI as the entire creator — only the second category is being removed.

Why is Tidal doing this now?

Because the June policy of labeling and demonetizing fully AI-generated tracks did not slow the flood: 43% of tracks delivered to Tidal in the past 90 days were detected as wholly AI-generated, peaking above 60% on a single day in early November. Tidal says the sheer volume buries real artists and diverts royalties through impersonation.
